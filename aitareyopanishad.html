ऐतरेयोपनिषद्

Aitareyopaniṣad

Aitareya Upaniṣad

Ṛgveda · meanings following Śaṅkara's bhāṣya

Three adhyāyas, five khaṇḍas, 33 mantras (Aitareya Āraṇyaka 2.4–6): the creation of the worlds and their guardians by the Self and its entry into the body; the three births; and the Self as consciousness. Meanings and translation follow Śaṅkara's bhāṣya.

Om. My speech is established in my mind; my mind is established in my speech. O manifest one, be manifest to me. You two are the linch-pins of the Veda for me. May what I have heard not forsake me. By this that I have studied I join days and nights. I shall speak what is right; I shall speak what is true. May that protect me; may that protect the speaker. प्रथमः खण्डः
Prathamaḥ khaṇḍaḥ · Adhyāya 1 · First khaṇḍa
आत्मा वा इदमेक एवाग्र आसीत् । नान्यत्किञ्चन मिषत् । स ईक्षत लोकान्नु सृजा इति ॥ १ ॥
ātmā vā idam eka evāgra āsīt | nānyat kiñcana miṣat | sa īkṣata lokān nu sṛjā iti || 1 ||
Padaccheda
आत्मा वै इदम् एकः एव अग्रे आसीत् न अन्यत् किञ्चन मिषत् सः ईक्षत लोकान् नु सृजै इति
ātmā vai idam ekaḥ eva agre āsīt na anyat kiñcana miṣat saḥ īkṣata lokān nu sṛjai iti
Word by word
In the beginning this was the Self alone, one only; there was nothing else whatever that winked. He reflected: ‘Let me now create worlds.’

Introduction Introducing the Upaniṣad: action together with the knowledge of the lower Brahman is finished, and its highest end, the merging in the deity that is called prāṇa, has been summed up through the knowledge of the uktha. Some hold that this merging is the highest human end, that it is liberation, to be reached by knowledge and rites combined, and that there is nothing beyond it. To set them aside the text goes on, ‘the Self, verily …’, to enjoin the knowledge of the Self alone. How is it known that what follows enjoins a knowledge unconnected with action? Because no other purpose is understood; and because it will show that the deities named before, fire and the rest, belong to saṃsāra, having the faults of hunger and the like (1.2.1), while the supreme Brahman is heard to be beyond hunger and the like.

Introduction Introducing the Upaniṣad, the objection: granted that the knowledge of the Self alone is the means of liberation; still it is the performer of rites who is entitled to it here, since no other, a man of another āśrama who does no rites, is mentioned, and the knowledge is begun directly after the rite of the thousand bṛhatī verses. Nor is this knowledge unconnected with action, for it ends as the earlier one did: as the brāhmaṇa and the mantra ‘the sun is the self’ (Ṛgveda 1.115.1) said that the man connected with rites, as the sun, is the self of all creatures moving and unmoving, so here ‘this is Brahmā, this is Indra’ ends with ‘all that is led by consciousness’ (3.1.3); the Saṃhitopaniṣad too speaks of him in connection with rites (‘the Bahvṛcas consider him in the great uktha’) and ends ‘in all beings they call him Brahman’. If it is said that settling again, with the question ‘who is this Self?’ (3.1.1), a self already settled is a useless repetition: no, it settles further properties of that same self of the man of rites, the creating, sustaining and withdrawing of the world, or it is for meditation alone; or the self that is seen as different in the sphere of rites is to be meditated on as non-different when no rite is being done. The Vājasaneyins say ‘he who knows knowledge and ignorance, both together …’ (Īśa 11) and ‘only doing works here should one wish to live a hundred years’ (Īśa 2); mortals have no life beyond a hundred years in which to give up action and meditate on the Self; ‘as long as he lives he offers the agnihotra’, ‘they burn him with his sacrificial vessels’, and the text on the three debts say the same. So the texts on wandering mendicancy (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 3.5.1, 4.4.22) are praise of the knowledge of the Self, or are meant for those not entitled to rites.

Introduction Introducing the Upaniṣad, the reply: no; when the highest truth is known and no fruit is seen, action is not possible. One who knows as his Self ‘I am Brahman’, the supreme, whose desires are attained, free of all the faults of saṃsāra, sees no purpose for himself in what is done or to be done. If he acts because he is enjoined: no, for he has seen the Self that is not an object of injunction. He who sees gaining the desired and avoiding the undesired as his purpose and seeks the means is the object of injunction; not he who sees himself as Brahman. If even he were enjoined, no one would be unenjoined and every action would have to be done by everyone always. Nor can he be enjoined by anyone, for the Veda itself issues from him: no one is commanded by words that arise from his own knowledge, nor a wise master by an undiscerning servant. If the Veda, being eternal and independent, can command all, the same fault stands. If the scripture enjoins on the same man both the duty of action and the knowledge of the Self: it cannot teach of one thing what is contradictory, as heat and cold of fire. The wish to gain the desired and avoid the undesired is not made by scripture, for it is seen in all creatures, even cowherds; scripture teaches what is not got of itself. Having produced the knowledge of the Self, which is opposed to anything being left to do, how could it again produce a duty, like cold in fire or darkness in the sun? And it does teach it: ‘let him know: he is my Self’ (Kauṣītaki 3.9), ‘consciousness is Brahman’ (3.1.3), ‘it knew only itself’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.4), ‘you are that’ (Chāndogya 6.8–16). The knowledge of Brahman as the Self, once arisen, is not sublated, so it cannot be called unarisen or mistaken.

Introduction Introducing the Upaniṣad, on renunciation: if it is said that giving up action is equally without purpose (‘nor has he anything to gain here by what is not done’, Gītā 3.18): no, for renunciation (vyutthāna) is merely not acting. That there is a purpose is caused by ignorance and is not a property of the thing; activity of speech, mind and body is seen in one driven by thirst for a purpose; wife, sons, wealth and the rest, the fivefold, are all objects of desire (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.4.17), ‘both these are desires only’ (3.5.1, 4.4.22). In the knower, who lacks the faults of ignorance and desire, such activity cannot arise; renunciation is the mere absence of action, not something positive to be performed like a sacrifice. It is the property of the man of knowledge, so no purpose need be sought: one does not ask what purpose is served when a man who walked in the dark does not fall into pit, mud and thorns once the light has risen. If, then, the knower may stay in the householder's state without acting: no, that state is prompted by desire; renunciation is the mere absence of the fixed tie with sons, wealth and the like. Hence service of the teacher and austerity also are not binding on the knower. Some householders, afraid of begging and of contempt, argue that the mendicant too keeps rules of begging, so the householder who wants only to keep the body alive, free of both desires, may stay at home on bare food and clothing. No: holding to a particular house of one's own is prompted by desire; and without it he is in fact a mendicant. If, as the mendicant keeps rules in begging and in purity, the knowing householder should keep to the daily rites because they are enjoined for life: this was answered, the knower is not an object of injunction and cannot be enjoined. The lifelong injunctions are not thereby useless: they hold for those who do not know. The regularity of the mendicant's activity for the bare upkeep of the body is not what prompts it, as the going of thirst is not the purpose of one who sips water for purification; and it continues from earlier habit, since to break it would cost effort.

Introduction Introducing the Upaniṣad, the conclusion: because renunciation, though it follows of itself, is stated again, the knower must take to it; and one who does not yet know but seeks liberation must also take to wandering mendicancy: ‘calm, restrained’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.23), for calm, restraint and the other means of seeing the Self are not possible in the other āśramas; ‘to those beyond the āśramas he taught the supreme purifier’ (Śvetāśvatara 6.21); ‘not by action, not by offspring or wealth, by renunciation some reached immortality’ (Kaivalya 2). The highest fruit of the householder's rites and the knowledge that goes with them has been summed up as merging in the deity, which is within saṃsāra; had the knowledge of the supreme Self belonged to the man of rites, the summing up would not have been of such a fruit. Nor is that a subordinate fruit, for the knowledge that leads to immortality has for its object the Self in which all name, form and action are negated (‘where all has become his very Self’ against ‘where there is duality, as it were’, Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.4.14). The bar of the debts holds only for one who does not know, for gaining the worlds of men, fathers and gods (1.5.16); for the knower who seeks the world of the Self there is none: ‘what shall we do with offspring’ (4.4.22), the Kāvaṣeya seers, and the Kauṣītakins' ‘the ancients who knew did not offer the agnihotra’ (2.5). Nor do debts bar the unknowing seeker from mendicancy: before he becomes a householder there is no debt, and ‘let him go forth from home, having become a forest-dweller, or else from studentship itself’ (Jābāla 4). Mendicancy is not for the unqualified only; they are spoken of separately. To do as one likes is quite out of the question for the knower, for that belongs to the deluded; even enjoined action is felt by him as a heavy burden. ‘Knowledge and ignorance together’ (Īśa 11) does not mean that both are in one man at one time, any more than the knowledge of silver and of the shell in the shell: ‘far apart and opposed are ignorance and what is known as knowledge’ (Kaṭha 1.2.4). Austerity, service of the teacher and the like, the means by which knowledge arises, are called ignorance because they are action; by them he brings forth knowledge and crosses death, that is desire, and then, desireless, reaches immortality by the knowledge of Brahman. ‘A hundred years’ (Īśa 2) holds for one who does not know. So what follows is begun to show the knowledge of the actionless Brahman alone as one's Self.

Bhāṣya Ātman is from āp (to pervade), ad (to eat) or at (to go on always): the supreme, all-knowing, all-powerful, free of hunger and every property of saṃsāra, by nature eternally pure, awake and free, unborn, undecaying, deathless, immortal, fearless, without a second. ‘This’, the world divided by name, form and action, was the Self alone before the creation. Is it not he alone now? It is; but there is a difference. Before its origin the world, its names and forms unmanifest, one with the Self, was the object of the one word and idea ‘Self’; now, its names and forms manifest, it is the object of many words and ideas and of the one word ‘Self’ as well: as foam, before its name and form are set apart from water, is the object of the one word ‘water’, and afterwards of both. ‘Nothing else that winked’: nothing active, or anything else; unlike the independent pradhāna of the Sāṃkhyas, which stands apart from the Self, or the atoms of the followers of Kaṇāda. How could he reflect before the creation, having no body or organs? By his all-knowing nature: ‘without hands or feet he is swift and grasps’ (Śvetāśvatara 3.19). ‘Worlds’: Ambhas and the rest, the places where creatures experience the fruits of their actions.

Word by word
He created these worlds: Ambhas, the Marīcis, Mara and the Āpas. That Ambhas is beyond heaven; heaven is its support. The Marīcis are the mid-region. Mara is the earth. Those that are below are the Āpas.

Introduction Having so reflected.

Bhāṣya As a skilled carpenter here thinks ‘let me make a palace of such a kind’ and then makes it. But the carpenter has material; how does the Self create worlds without material? There is no fault: name and form, unmanifest and one with the Self, denoted by the one word ‘Self’, are the material of the manifest world, as water is of foam. Or rather, as a knowing magician, without material, projects himself as another self that seems to walk in the sky, so the all-knowing god, all-powerful, of great māyā, projects himself as another, in the form of the world; this is the more fitting, and so the views that the effect, or the cause, or both are non-existent find no place. Having brought forth the egg in the order beginning with space, he created the worlds, which the text itself explains. Ambhas, beyond heaven, is so called because it bears water; heaven is its support. The mid-region below heaven is the Marīcis: plural, though one, because of its many places, or from its connection with the rays (marīci). The earth is Mara, because beings die (mriyante) in it. Those below the earth are the Āpas, from āp (to reach). Though the worlds are made of the five elements, they are called by names of water because water predominates in them.

स ईक्षतेमे नु लोकाः । लोकपालान्नु सृजा इति । सोऽद्भ्य एव पुरुषं समुद्धृत्यामूर्च्छयत् ॥ ३ ॥
sa īkṣateme nu lokāḥ | lokapālān nu sṛjā iti | so 'dbhya eva puruṣaṃ samuddhṛtyāmūrcchayat || 3 ||
Padaccheda
सः ईक्षत इमे नु लोकाः लोकपालान् नु सृजै इति सः अद्भ्यः एव पुरुषम् समुद्धृत्य अमूर्च्छयत्
saḥ īkṣata ime nu lokāḥ lokapālān nu sṛjai iti saḥ adbhyaḥ eva puruṣam samuddhṛtya amūrcchayat
Word by word
He reflected: ‘Here now are the worlds; let me now create guardians of the worlds.’ Having drawn out from the waters themselves a Puruṣa, (a form like a man,) he gave it shape.

Introduction Having created the four worlds, which are the seats where all creatures take the fruits of their actions.

Bhāṣya The Lord reflected again: these worlds I have created would perish without protectors, so let me create guardians to protect them. ‘From the waters’: from the five elements in which water predominates, the same from which he created Ambhas and the other worlds. ‘A Puruṣa’: of the shape of a man, with head, hands and the rest. He drew it out as a potter takes a lump of clay from the earth, and massed it together by joining its own limbs.

तमभ्यतपत् । तस्याभितप्तस्य मुखं निरभिद्यत यथाण्डम् । मुखाद्वाक् । वाचोऽग्निः । नासिके निरभिद्येताम् । नासिकाभ्यां प्राणः । प्राणाद्वायुः । अक्षिणी निरभिद्येताम् । अक्षीभ्यां चक्षुः । चक्षुष आदित्यः । कर्णौ निरभिद्येताम् । कर्णाभ्यां श्रोत्रम् । श्रोत्राद्दिशः । त्वङ्निरभिद्यत । त्वचो लोमानि । लोमभ्य ओषधिवनस्पतयः । हृदयं निरभिद्यत । हृदयान्मनः । मनसश्चन्द्रमाः । नाभिर्निरभिद्यत । नाभ्या अपानः । अपानान्मृत्युः । शिश्नं निरभिद्यत । शिश्नाद्रेतः । रेतस आपः ॥ ४ ॥

Word by word
He brooded over him. Of him who was so brooded over the mouth burst forth, like an egg; from the mouth speech, from speech fire. The nostrils burst forth; from the nostrils prāṇa, from prāṇa air. The eyes burst forth; from the eyes sight, from sight the sun. The ears burst forth; from the ears hearing, from hearing the quarters. The skin burst forth; from the skin the hairs, from the hairs herbs and trees. The heart burst forth; from the heart the mind, from the mind the moon. The navel burst forth; from the navel apāna, from apāna death. The generative organ burst forth; from the generative organ seed, from seed the waters.

Bhāṣya ‘he brooded over’ that lump of the shape of a man: he thought on it, he formed a resolve, as in ‘whose austerity consists of knowledge’ (Muṇḍaka 1.1.9). Heated by that austerity, the Lord's resolve, an opening of the shape of a mouth arose in the lump, as a bird's egg bursts. From the mouth came forth the organ of speech, and from speech fire, the guardian who presides over it. So in every case three burst forth in order: the seat, the organ, and the deity. The heart is the seat of the inner organ; the mind is the inner organ. The navel is the place where all the prāṇas are bound; ‘apāna’ means the organ of excretion, because it is joined with apāna, and death is the deity that presides over it. The generative organ is the seat of the organ of generation; the organ itself is called ‘seed’ together with the seed, since it serves to emit it.

ता एता देवताः सृष्टा अस्मिन्महत्यर्णवे प्रापतन् । तमशनापिपासाभ्यामन्ववार्जत् । ता एनमब्रुवन्नायतनं नः प्रजानीहि । यस्मिन्प्रतिष्ठिता अन्नमदामेति ॥ १ ॥
tā etā devatāḥ sṛṣṭā asmin mahaty arṇave prāpatan | tam aśanāpipāsābhyām anvavārjat | tā enam abruvann āyatanaṃ naḥ prajānīhi | yasmin pratiṣṭhitā annam adāmeti || 1 ||
Padaccheda
ताः एताः देवताः सृष्टाः अस्मिन् महति अर्णवे प्रापतन् तम् अशना-पिपासाभ्याम् अन्ववार्जत् ताः एनम् अब्रुवन् आयतनम् नः प्रजानीहि यस्मिन् प्रतिष्ठिताः अन्नम् अदाम इति
tāḥ etāḥ devatāḥ sṛṣṭāḥ asmin mahati arṇave prāpatan tam aśanā-pipāsābhyām anvavārjat tāḥ enam abruvan āyatanam naḥ prajānīhi yasmin pratiṣṭhitāḥ annam adāma iti
Word by word
These deities, thus created, fell into this great ocean. He subjected him (that first-formed Puruṣa) to hunger and thirst. They said to him (the creator): ‘Provide for us an abode, established in which we may eat food.’

Bhāṣya The deities, fire and the rest, whom the Lord created with the intention that they should guard the worlds, fell into this great ocean of saṃsāra: whose water is the pain that springs from ignorance, desire and action; whose great sea-beasts are sharp disease, old age and death; without beginning, without end, shoreless, without support; where the only moment of rest is the drop of pleasure born of objects and senses; whose great waves are the hundreds of evils raised by the wind of thirst for the objects of the five senses; loud with the cries and wailing of those gone to the many hells, Mahāraurava and the rest; on which the raft is knowledge, stocked with the provisions of the virtues of the self, truth, uprightness, giving, compassion, non-injury, calm, restraint and firmness; whose course is the company of the good and the renunciation of all; and whose farther shore is liberation. Therefore even the end described before, the merging in fire and the other deities, which is the fruit of knowledge and rites performed together, is not enough to still the pain of saṃsāra: that is what is meant here. Since this is so, the supreme Brahman, the Self of oneself and of all beings, the cause of the origin, continuance and dissolution of the world, is to be known in order to still all the pain of saṃsāra: ‘this is the path, this the action, this Brahman, this the truth’ (Aitareya Āraṇyaka 2.1.1), ‘there is no other path for going’ (Śvetāśvatara 3.8, 6.15).

Bhāṣya ‘him’ is that Puruṣa, the lump produced first, the seed from which seats, organs and deities arose; him he joined with hunger and thirst. Because he, the cause, has the faults of hunger and the like, the deities, his effects, have them too. Pressed by hunger and thirst they said to ‘him’, the grandfather, the creator: appoint for us a seat in which we shall be able to eat food.

ताभ्यो गामानयत् । ता अब्रुवन्न वै नोऽयमलमिति । ताभ्योऽश्वमानयत् । ता अब्रुवन्न वै नोऽयमलमिति ॥ २ ॥
tābhyo gām ānayat | tā abruvan na vai no 'yam alam iti | tābhyo 'śvam ānayat | tā abruvan na vai no 'yam alam iti || 2 ||
Padaccheda
ताभ्यः गाम् आनयत् ताः अब्रुवन् न वै नः अयम् अलम् इति ताभ्यः अश्वम् आनयत् ताः अब्रुवन् न वै नः अयम् अलम् इति
tābhyaḥ gām ānayat tāḥ abruvan na vai naḥ ayam alam iti tābhyaḥ aśvam ānayat tāḥ abruvan na vai naḥ ayam alam iti
Word by word
He brought them a cow. They said: ‘This is surely not enough for us.’ He brought them a horse. They said: ‘This is surely not enough for us.’

Introduction The Lord, thus addressed.

Bhāṣya A lump of the shape of a cow, drawn out from those same waters and given shape as before, he showed to them. Seeing the form of a cow they said: this lump is not enough for us as a seat in which to eat food; it is not fit for eating. The cow being refused, he brought them a horse, and they said the same.

ताभ्यः पुरुषमानयत् । ता अब्रुवन्सुकृतं बतेति । पुरुषो वाव सुकृतम् । ता अब्रवीद्यथायतनं प्रविशतेति ॥ ३ ॥
tābhyaḥ puruṣam ānayat | tā abruvan sukṛtaṃ bateti | puruṣo vāva sukṛtam | tā abravīd yathāyatanaṃ praviśateti || 3 ||
Padaccheda
ताभ्यः पुरुषम् आनयत् ताः अब्रुवन् सुकृतम् बत इति पुरुषः वाव सुकृतम् ताः अब्रवीत् यथा-आयतनम् प्रविशत इति
tābhyaḥ puruṣam ānayat tāḥ abruvan sukṛtam bata iti puruṣaḥ vāva sukṛtam tāḥ abravīt yathā-āyatanam praviśata iti
Word by word
He brought them a man. They said: ‘Ah, well made!’ Man indeed is what is well made. He said to them: ‘Enter, each into your own abode.’

Introduction When all (the others) had been refused.

Bhāṣya He brought them a man, who is of their own source. Seeing a man, their own source, they were no longer distressed and said: this seat is well made. Therefore man alone is ‘the well made’, because he is the cause of all meritorious action; or he is called sukṛta because he was made by (the Lord) himself (svayam), by his own powers of māyā. The Lord, thinking ‘this seat pleases them, for all delight in their own source’, said to them: enter each the abode that is fit for your own work, speaking and the rest.

अग्निर्वाग्भूत्वा मुखं प्राविशत् । वायुः प्राणो भूत्वा नासिके प्राविशत् । आदित्यश्चक्षुर्भूत्वाक्षिणी प्राविशत् । दिशः श्रोत्रं भूत्वा कर्णौ प्राविशन् । ओषधिवनस्पतयो लोमानि भूत्वा त्वचं प्राविशन् । चन्द्रमा मनो भूत्वा हृदयं प्राविशत् । मृत्युरपानो भूत्वा नाभिं प्राविशत् । आपो रेतो भूत्वा शिश्नं प्राविशन् ॥ ४ ॥
agnir vāg bhūtvā mukhaṃ prāviśat | vāyuḥ prāṇo bhūtvā nāsike prāviśat | ādityaś cakṣur bhūtvākṣiṇī prāviśat | diśaḥ śrotraṃ bhūtvā karṇau prāviśan | oṣadhivanaspatayo lomāni bhūtvā tvacaṃ prāviśan | candramā mano bhūtvā hṛdayaṃ prāviśat | mṛtyur apāno bhūtvā nābhiṃ prāviśat | āpo reto bhūtvā śiśnaṃ prāviśan || 4 ||
Padaccheda
अग्निः वाक् भूत्वा मुखम् प्राविशत् वायुः प्राणः भूत्वा नासिके प्राविशत् आदित्यः चक्षुः भूत्वा अक्षिणी प्राविशत् दिशः श्रोत्रम् भूत्वा कर्णौ प्राविशन् ओषधि-वनस्पतयः लोमानि भूत्वा त्वचम् प्राविशन् चन्द्रमाः मनः भूत्वा हृदयम् प्राविशत् मृत्युः अपानः भूत्वा नाभिम् प्राविशत् आपः रेतः भूत्वा शिश्नम् प्राविशन्
agniḥ vāk bhūtvā mukham prāviśat vāyuḥ prāṇaḥ bhūtvā nāsike prāviśat ādityaḥ cakṣuḥ bhūtvā akṣiṇī prāviśat diśaḥ śrotram bhūtvā karṇau prāviśan oṣadhi-vanaspatayaḥ lomāni bhūtvā tvacam prāviśan candramāḥ manaḥ bhūtvā hṛdayam prāviśat mṛtyuḥ apānaḥ bhūtvā nābhim prāviśat āpaḥ retaḥ bhūtvā śiśnam prāviśan
Word by word
Fire, having become speech, entered the mouth. Air, having become prāṇa, entered the nostrils. The sun, having become sight, entered the eyes. The quarters, having become hearing, entered the ears. Herbs and trees, having become hairs, entered the skin. The moon, having become mind, entered the heart. Death, having become apāna, entered the navel. The waters, having become seed, entered the generative organ.

Introduction Having received the Lord's leave, ‘so be it’, like the commanders of the army and other officers in a (king's) city.

Bhāṣya Fire, the deity that identifies itself with speech, became speech itself and entered its own source, the mouth. The rest is to be understood in the same way.

तमशनापिपासे अब्रूतामावाभ्यामभिप्रजानीहीति । ते अब्रवीदेतास्वेव वां देवतास्वाभजाम्येतासु भागिन्यौ करोमीति । तस्माद्यस्यै कस्यै च देवतायै हविर्गृह्यते भागिन्यावेवास्यामशनापिपासे भवतः ॥ ५ ॥
tam aśanāpipāse abrūtām āvābhyām abhiprajānīhīti | te abravīd etāsv eva vāṃ devatāsv ābhajāmy etāsu bhāginyau karomīti | tasmād yasyai kasyai ca devatāyai havir gṛhyate bhāginyāv evāsyām aśanāpipāse bhavataḥ || 5 ||
Padaccheda
तम् अशना-पिपासे अब्रूताम् आवाभ्याम् अभिप्रजानीहि इति ते अब्रवीत् एतासु एव वाम् देवतासु आभजामि एतासु भागिन्यौ करोमि इति तस्मात् यस्यै कस्यै च देवतायै हविः गृह्यते भागिन्यौ एव अस्याम् अशना-पिपासे भवतः
tam aśanā-pipāse abrūtām āvābhyām abhiprajānīhi iti te abravīt etāsu eva vām devatāsu ābhajāmi etāsu bhāginyau karomi iti tasmāt yasyai kasyai ca devatāyai haviḥ gṛhyate bhāginyau eva asyām aśanā-pipāse bhavataḥ
Word by word
Hunger and thirst said to him: ‘Provide (an abode) for us two.’ He said to them: ‘I give you a share in these very deities; I make you sharers in them.’ Therefore, for whatever deity an oblation is taken up, hunger and thirst are sharers in it.

Introduction When the deities had thus obtained their seats.

Bhāṣya Hunger and thirst, being without a seat, said to the Lord: think of, appoint, a seat for us two. The Lord said to them: being states, you cannot be eaters of food without resting on something conscious; therefore I favour you with a part in the working of these very deities, fire and the rest, in the body and among the gods; whatever share, an oblation or the like, belongs to a deity, by that same share I make you two sharers. Because the Lord so ordained at the beginning of creation, even now, for whatever deity an oblation of boiled rice, cake or the like is taken up, hunger and thirst have a share in that deity.

तृतीयः खण्डः
Tṛtīyaḥ khaṇḍaḥ · Adhyāya 1 · Third khaṇḍa
स ईक्षतेमे नु लोकाश्च लोकपालाश्च । अन्नमेभ्यः सृजा इति ॥ १ ॥
sa īkṣateme nu lokāś ca lokapālāś ca | annam ebhyaḥ sṛjā iti || 1 ||
Padaccheda
सः ईक्षत इमे नु लोकाः च लोकपालाः च अन्नम् एभ्यः सृजै इति
saḥ īkṣata ime nu lokāḥ ca lokapālāḥ ca annam ebhyaḥ sṛjai iti
Word by word
He reflected: ‘Here now are the worlds and the guardians of the worlds; let me create food for them.’

Bhāṣya The Lord reflected: these worlds and guardians that I have created are joined with hunger and thirst, so they cannot subsist without food; let me therefore create food for the guardians. In the world, lords are seen to be free to favour and to restrain their own people; so the great Lord, being lord of all, is free to restrain and to favour all.

saḥ apaḥ abhyatapat tābhyaḥ abhitaptābhyaḥ mūrtiḥ ajāyata yā vai sā mūrtiḥ ajāyata annam vai tat
Word by word
He brooded over the waters. From them, so brooded over, a solid form was born. That solid form which was born, that indeed is food.

Bhāṣya Wishing to create food, the Lord brooded over those same waters named before. From them, as the material, was born a ‘solid form’: something dense, able to sustain, made up of the moving and the unmoving.

तदेनत्सृष्टं पराङत्यजिघांसत् । तद्वाचाजिघृक्षत् । तन्नाशक्नोद्वाचा ग्रहीतुम् । स यद्धैनद्वाचाग्रहैष्यदभिव्याहृत्य हैवान्नमत्रप्स्यत् ॥ ३ ॥
tad enat sṛṣṭaṃ parāṅ atyajighāṃsat | tad vācājighṛkṣat | tan nāśaknod vācā grahītum | sa yad dhainad vācāgrahaiṣyad abhivyāhṛtya haivānnam atrapsyat || 3 ||
Padaccheda
तत् एनत् सृष्टम् पराङ् अत्यजिघांसत् तत् वाचा अजिघृक्षत् तत् न अशक्नोत् वाचा ग्रहीतुम् सः यत् ह एनत् वाचा अग्रहैष्यत् अभिव्याहृत्य ह एव अन्नम् अत्रप्स्यत्
tat enat sṛṣṭam parāṅ atyajighāṃsat tat vācā ajighṛkṣat tat na aśaknot vācā grahītum saḥ yat ha enat vācā agrahaiṣyat abhivyāhṛtya ha eva annam atrapsyat
Word by word
This (food), once created, turned away and sought to flee. He sought to seize it with speech. He could not seize it with speech. If he had seized it with speech, one would have been satisfied by merely speaking of food.

Bhāṣya The food, created in front of the worlds and guardians for their sake, like a mouse within reach of a cat, thought ‘this eater of food is my death’, turned back and wished to get beyond its eaters: it began to run away. Seeing what the food intended, that lump which is the aggregate of the worlds and guardians, made up of body and organs, being the first-born and seeing no other eaters of food, wished to seize it with speech, by the act of speaking, and could not. Had that first-born embodied one seized it with speech, the whole world, being his effect, would be satisfied by merely uttering ‘food’; and this is not so; hence we understand that the first-born too could not seize it with speech.

Introduction Introducing the adhyāya: the meaning of the adhyāya just ended is this. He who makes the origin, continuance and dissolution of the world, who is not in saṃsāra, all-knowing and all-powerful, created this whole world in the order beginning with space without taking any thing other than himself, and himself entered all the bodies that have prāṇa in order to awaken to his own Self; and having entered he awoke directly to his own Self as it is: ‘I am this Brahman’. Therefore he alone is the one Self in all bodies, and there is no other; and another too should know ‘he is my Self, I am Brahman’. It may be objected: for the all-pervading Self of all there is nothing, not so much as the tip of a hair, that he has not entered; how then did he cleave the parting and enter, like an ant into a hole? But this is a very small objection, and much more could be objected: that without organs he reflected; that without material he created the worlds; that he drew a Puruṣa out of the waters and gave it shape; that from his brooding the mouth and the rest burst forth, and from them fire and the other guardians; their being joined with hunger and thirst, their asking for an abode, the showing of the cow and the rest, their entering each its abode, the flight of the food, the wish to seize it with speech and the rest: all this is on a level with the cleaving of the parting and the entry. Let it all be unfitting, then? No: only the awakening to the Self is meant here, so all this is explanatory statement (arthavāda), and there is no fault. Or the god of great māyā, all-knowing and all-powerful, did all this as a magician would. The more fitting view is that the elaboration of stories is, as in the world, for ease of understanding; for no fruit is held to come from knowing the story of creation, but immortality is the fruit, known in all the Upaniṣads and in the smṛtis (Gītā 13.27), of knowing the oneness of the Self.

Introduction Introducing the adhyāya, the objection: there are three selves. One is the jīva, the enjoyer and agent, who transmigrates, known to all the world and to scripture. A second conscious self, the Lord, all-knowing, the maker of the world, is inferred from the making of worlds and bodies with their many seats fit for the experience of the fruits of the actions of many creatures, as a carpenter is from the making of a city or a palace. The third is the Puruṣa of the Upaniṣads, ‘from whom words turn back’ (Taittirīya 2.4.1), ‘not this, not this’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 3.9.26). These three being unlike one another, how can it be known that there is one Self only, without a second, not transmigrating? And how is even the jīva known? As hearer, thinker, seer, commander, proclaimer, knower. But that is contradictory: he is the unthought thinker, the unknown knower, and ‘you cannot think the thinker of thought’ (3.4.2). It would be contradictory if he were known by perception, like pleasure; but perception of him is denied, and he is known by the mark of hearing and the rest. How even by a mark? While the self hears a sound it is occupied with the act of hearing alone, so that acts of thinking and knowing, about itself or anything else, are not possible; so too in thinking. And hearing and the rest act only on their own objects: the thinker's thinking is not possible on anything but what is to be thought. Everything can be thought by the mind? True, but nothing can be thought without a thinker; so the thinker of all is thinker only and cannot be what is thought, and there is no second thinker of the thinker. If he were thought by himself, there would be two, the self by which and the self which is thought, or one self split in two like a bamboo; either way it does not hold, as of two lamps one cannot be the lighter and the other the lit, being alike. Nor has the thinker any time free from thinking the thinkable in which to think the self. And if he thinks the self by a mark, the same two faults follow.

Introduction Introducing the adhyāya, the reply: if he is known neither by perception nor by inference, how is it said ‘let him know: he is my Self’ (Kauṣītaki 3.9), and how ‘hearer, thinker’? It may be said that the self has the properties of hearing and the rest and also their absence, by turns: when it hears it does not think, as Devadatta when he walks does not stand; so the followers of Kaṇāda and others hold that knowledge is born of conjunction and is not simultaneous (‘my mind was elsewhere, I did not see’; the non-arising of cognitions together is the mark of the mind). But then the sense of the śruti does not hold, for hearing and the rest are accepted as eternal: ‘there is no loss of the hearing of the hearer’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.3.27). Nor does it follow that cognitions arise together, against experience, or that the self has no ignorance. The seeing of the eye and the other organs, which are non-eternal, have form, and are subject to conjunction and disjunction, is non-eternal, like the blazing of fire that is born of contact with grass; but the eternal, formless Self, not subject to conjunction and disjunction, cannot have a non-eternal seeing born of conjunction: ‘there is no loss of the seeing of the seer’ (4.3.23). So there are two seeings, the non-eternal one of the eye and the eternal one of the Self; and likewise two hearings, two thinkings, two knowings, outer and inner; and thus the text ‘the seer of seeing, the hearer of hearing’ fits. In the world too the seeing of the eye is known to be non-eternal, lost and gained as the darkening of the eye comes and goes, and the eternity of the Self's seeing is known: one whose eyes have been taken out says ‘today I saw my brother in a dream’, and a deaf man ‘today I heard a mantra in a dream’. If the Self's seeing were born of contact with the eye it would perish with it, and he would not see blue or yellow in a dream. The Self's eternal seeing is what grasps the outer, non-eternal seeing; because the outer seeing arises and passes away, people mistake the Self's seeing that grasps it for non-eternal too, as the sight that follows a whirling firebrand seems itself to whirl: ‘it thinks as it were, it moves as it were’ (4.3.7). Since the Self's seeing is eternal, it is neither simultaneous nor successive. The error of the world and of the logicians, who lack the tradition of scripture, that the Self's seeing is non-eternal comes from the adjunct of the outer seeing; and the imagining of a difference between jīva, Lord and supreme Self has this same cause.

Introduction Introducing the adhyāya, the conclusion: he who wishes to imagine, in that nature which no word or idea reaches, in which all the distinctions of speech and mind become one, ‘it is, it is not; one, many; with qualities, without; it knows, it knows not; active, inactive; with fruit, without; with seed, seedless; pleasure, pain; middle, not middle; void, not void; the other, I, another’, surely wishes to roll up the sky like a hide, to climb it with his feet as by a stair, to see the track of fish in the water and of birds in the sky: ‘not this, not this’, ‘from whom words turn back’, ‘who truly knows?’ (Ṛgveda). How then is he known as ‘he is my Self’? They tell a story. A simple man was told by some people, on account of some fault, ‘shame on you, you are no man’. To be assured that he was a man he went to someone and said ‘tell me, sir, who I am’. The other, seeing his simplicity, said ‘I will teach you by steps’, denied that he was a thing that does not move and so on, said ‘you are not a non-man’, and stopped. The simple man said: ‘you set out to teach me and have fallen silent; why do you not teach me?’ Your words are like his. One who does not grasp that he is a man when told ‘you are not a non-man’, how will he grasp it when told ‘you are a man’? Therefore the way of awakening to the Self is the teaching just as scripture gives it, and no other: what fire is to burn cannot be burnt by anything else. So scripture, setting out to teach the nature of the Self, says ‘not this, not this’, as in the denial of being a non-man, and stops; and it teaches ‘without inside, without outside’, ‘this Self is Brahman, the experiencer of all’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.5.19), ‘you are that’ (Chāndogya 6.8–16), ‘where all has become his very Self, by what should he see whom?’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.4.14). As long as a man does not know this Self in this way, he takes the adjunct, marked by the outer non-eternal seeing, for himself, through ignorance thinks the properties of the adjunct his own, and transmigrates under the power of ignorance, desire and action, returning again and again among gods, animals and men, from Brahmā down to a clump of grass. In what states he lives so, in an unbroken succession of births and deaths like the current of a river, the śruti now shows, to produce dispassion.

Bhāṣya This one, who has ignorance, desire and action and takes them for his own, having performed sacrifice and other rites, comes from that world by the way of smoke and the rest, becomes food, and is offered in the fire that is man. In that man the transmigrating self, through the juices of the body in their order, first becomes an embryo in the form of seed. This seed is the vigour, the essence of the body, produced from all the limbs, the parts, of the lump that is made of food; being the man's own self it is called ‘a self’, and he bears that self, become an embryo as seed, in himself, in his own body. When his wife is in season he pours it into the woman, the fire that is woman, and then the father begets it. This going out from its place at the time the seed is poured is the first birth of this transmigrating self, the first state to become manifest.

These and other workings of the inner organ serve the perception of the perceiver, who is consciousness alone; they are adjuncts of Brahman, whose nature is pure consciousness, and so become names of it, born of those adjuncts. All these are names of consciousness, not of itself directly: as it is said, ‘when breathing he comes to be called breath’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.4.7).

एषः ब्रह्मा एषः इन्द्रः एषः प्रजापतिः एते सर्वे देवाः इमानि च पञ्च महाभूतानि पृथिवी वायुः आकाशः आपः ज्योतींषि इति एतानि इमानि क्षुद्र-मिश्राणि इव बीजानि इतराणि च इतराणि च अण्डजानि च जारुजानि च स्वेदजानि च उद्भिज्जानि च अश्वाः गावः पुरुषाः हस्तिनः यत् किञ्च इदम् प्राणि जङ्गमम् च पतत्रि च यत् च स्थावरम् सर्वम् तत् प्रज्ञा-नेत्रम् प्रज्ञाने प्रतिष्ठितम् प्रज्ञा-नेत्रः लोकः प्रज्ञा प्रतिष्ठा प्रज्ञानम् ब्रह्म
eṣaḥ brahmā eṣaḥ indraḥ eṣaḥ prajāpatiḥ ete sarve devāḥ imāni ca pañca mahābhūtāni pṛthivī vāyuḥ ākāśaḥ āpaḥ jyotīṃṣi iti etāni imāni kṣudra-miśrāṇi iva bījāni itarāṇi ca itarāṇi ca aṇḍajāni ca jārujāni ca svedajāni ca udbhijjāni ca aśvāḥ gāvaḥ puruṣāḥ hastinaḥ yat kiñca idam prāṇi jaṅgamam ca patatri ca yat ca sthāvaram sarvam tat prajñā-netram prajñāne pratiṣṭhitam prajñā-netraḥ lokaḥ prajñā pratiṣṭhā prajñānam brahma
Word by word
This is Brahmā; this is Indra; this is Prajāpati; (this is) all these gods; and these five great elements, earth, air, space, water and light; and these (creatures) mixed with the small, the seeds of one kind and of another: those born of eggs, those born of wombs, those born of sweat and those born of sprouts; horses, cattle, men, elephants; whatever living thing there is here, that walks and that flies, and what is unmoving. All that is led by consciousness; it is established in consciousness. The world is led by consciousness; consciousness is its support. Consciousness is Brahman.

Bhāṣya This Self whose nature is consciousness is the lower Brahman, the prāṇa that is in all bodies, the self of consciousness: Hiraṇyagarbha, who has entered the adjuncts that are the inner organs like the reflections of the sun in different waters. He is Indra, by his quality or as king of the gods; he is Prajāpati, the first-born embodied one from whom fire and the other guardians were born through the bursting forth of the mouth and the rest; he is all these gods, fire and the rest. The five great elements, earth and the rest, are the material of all bodies, in the form of food and eater of food. ‘Mixed with the small’: together with the small creatures, snakes and the like (iva has no meaning here). ‘Seeds’ are causes, named in two groups, ‘of one kind and of another’: the egg-born, birds and the like; the womb-born, men and the like; the sweat-born, lice and the like; the sprout-born, trees and the like. ‘That walks’ is what moves on feet, ‘that flies’ what moves through the sky, ‘unmoving’ what does not move. All that is he alone.

Bhāṣya ‘led by consciousness’: prajñā is consciousness, and that is Brahman; netra is that by which one is led; that which has consciousness for its leader is prajñā-netra. ‘Established in consciousness’: resting on Brahman at the times of its origin, continuance and dissolution. ‘The world is led by consciousness’, as before; or the whole world has consciousness for its eye. Consciousness is the support of the whole world; therefore consciousness is Brahman. This Brahman, with every distinction of adjunct gone, is spotless, stainless, actionless, at peace, one, without a second, to be known by the setting aside of every particular, ‘not this, not this’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 3.9.26), beyond the reach of all words and ideas. By connection with the adjunct of utterly pure consciousness it is the all-knowing Lord, who sets in motion the unmanifest seed of the world common to all, and as the controller is called the Inner Ruler. The same, identifying itself with the intellect that is the seed of the manifest world, is called Hiraṇyagarbha. The same, having for its adjunct the first body that arose within the egg, is called Virāj, Prajāpati. With the adjuncts of fire and the rest that arose from that, it is called a deity. So too in the particular bodies, from Brahmā down to a clump of grass, Brahman takes this and that name and form. That one, divided by all its adjuncts, is known in every way by all creatures and by the logicians and imagined in many ways: ‘some call him Agni, others Manu, Prajāpati, some Indra, others prāṇa, others the eternal Brahman’ (Manu 12.123).
