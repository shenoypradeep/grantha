ॐ भद्रं कर्णेभिः शृणुयाम देवा भद्रं पश्येमाक्षभिर्यजत्राः । स्थिरैरङ्गैस्तुष्टुवांसस्तनूभिर्व्यशेम देवहितं यदायुः ॥ स्वस्ति न इन्द्रो वृद्धश्रवाः स्वस्ति नः पूषा विश्ववेदाः । स्वस्ति नस्तार्क्ष्यो अरिष्टनेमिः स्वस्ति नो बृहस्पतिर्दधातु ॥ ॐ शान्तिः शान्तिः शान्तिः ॥
oṃ bhadraṃ karṇebhiḥ śṛṇuyāma devā bhadraṃ paśyemākṣabhiryajatrāḥ | sthirairaṅgaistuṣṭuvāṃsastanūbhirvyaśema devahitaṃ yadāyuḥ || svasti na indro vṛddhaśravāḥ svasti naḥ pūṣā viśvavedāḥ | svasti nastārkṣyo ariṣṭanemiḥ svasti no bṛhaspatirdadhātu || oṃ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ ||
Padaccheda
ॐ भद्रम् कर्णेभिः शृणुयाम देवाः भद्रम् पश्येम अक्षभिः यजत्राः स्थिरैः अङ्गैः तुष्टुवांसः तनूभिः व्यशेम देव-हितम् यत् आयुः स्वस्ति नः इन्द्रः वृद्ध-श्रवाः स्वस्ति नः पूषा विश्व-वेदाः स्वस्ति नः तार्क्ष्यः अरिष्ट-नेमिः स्वस्ति नः बृहस्पतिः दधातु ॐ शान्तिः शान्तिः शान्तिः
oṃ bhadram karṇebhiḥ śṛṇuyāma devāḥ bhadram paśyema akṣabhiḥ yajatrāḥ sthiraiḥ aṅgaiḥ tuṣṭuvāṃsaḥ tanūbhiḥ vyaśema deva-hitam yat āyuḥ svasti naḥ indraḥ vṛddha-śravāḥ svasti naḥ pūṣā viśva-vedāḥ svasti naḥ tārkṣyaḥ ariṣṭa-nemiḥ svasti naḥ bṛhaspatiḥ dadhātu oṃ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ
Word by word
Om. May we hear with our ears what is auspicious, O gods; may we see with our eyes what is auspicious, O you who are worthy of worship. Praising you with firm limbs and bodies, may we attain the life that the gods have appointed. May Indra of great fame grant us well-being; may Pūṣan who knows all grant us well-being; may Tārkṣya of the unharmed wheel-rim grant us well-being; may Bṛhaspati grant us well-being. Om. Peace, peace, peace.

Note The peace invocation of the Upaniṣads of the Atharvaveda, printed at the opening of the edition. Śaṅkara's Praśna bhāṣya does not comment on it; the translation gives the plain sense of the words.

माण्डूक्योपनिषत्
Māṇḍūkyopaniṣat · Twelve mantras, with Gauḍapāda's kārikās
ॐ इत्येतदक्षरमिदं सर्वं तस्योपव्याख्यानं भूतं भवद्भविष्यदिति सर्वमोंकार एव । यच्चान्यत्त्रिकालातीतं तदप्योंकार एव ॥ १ ॥
oṃ ity etad akṣaram idaṃ sarvaṃ tasyopavyākhyānaṃ bhūtaṃ bhavad bhaviṣyad iti sarvam oṃkāra eva | yac cānyat trikālātītaṃ tad apy oṃkāra eva || 1 ||
Padaccheda
ॐ इति एतत् अक्षरम् इदम् सर्वम् तस्य उपव्याख्यानम् भूतम् भवत् भविष्यत् इति सर्वम् ओंकारः एव यत् च अन्यत् त्रि-काल-अतीतम् तत् अपि ओंकारः एव
oṃ iti etat akṣaram idam sarvam tasya upavyākhyānam bhūtam bhavat bhaviṣyat iti sarvam oṃkāraḥ eva yat ca anyat tri-kāla-atītam tat api oṃkāraḥ eva
Word by word
Om: this syllable is all this. A clear exposition of it (is begun): all that has been, is and will be is the syllable Om alone; and whatever else is beyond the three times, that too is the syllable Om alone.

Bhāṣya Śaṅkara opens with two verses. ‘I bow to that Brahman which, having pervaded the worlds with the spreading rays of its consciousness that pervade all that moves and stands, having enjoyed the gross enjoyments, and again those born of desire and lit up by the intellect, having drunk in all particulars, sleeps enjoying sweetness, and makes us enjoy by illusion; which by the counting of illusion is the fourth, supreme, deathless, unborn.’ ‘May that fourth protect us, who, as the self of all, having eaten the gross enjoyments that merit brings, and then the other, subtle ones made by the power of his own mind, by his own light, slowly sets them all again in himself and, leaving every particular, is free of the host of guṇas.’

Introduction Introducing the Upaniṣad: ‘Om, this syllable is all this’: the exposition of it, this set of four chapters (prakaraṇa), a gathering of the essence of the sense of the Vedānta, is begun. Hence its connection, subject and purpose need not be told separately: those of the Vedānta are its own; yet one who sets out to explain a work should tell them briefly. A teaching is connected with its subject by revealing a means that has a purpose, and so has connection, subject and purpose. What is the purpose? As for one afflicted by sickness the ceasing of the sickness is health, so for the Self that takes itself to be in pain, the stilling of the manifold of duality is health: the state of non-duality is the purpose. Since the manifold of duality is made by ignorance, it would be stilled by knowledge; so this is begun to make known the knowledge of Brahman, as the texts show: ‘where there is duality, as it were’, ‘where there is as it were another, there one would see another, one would know another’, ‘where all has become his very Self, by what would he see whom, by what would he know whom?’.

Bhāṣya On the four chapters: the first, resting chiefly on scripture, settles the syllable Om and is a means of knowing the truth of the Self. The second shows by reasons the unreality of that duality on whose stilling non-duality is known, as the truth of the rope is known when the imagined snake is stilled. The third, lest non-duality too be thought unreal, shows by reasoning that it is real. The fourth sets aside, by their own arguments, the other, non-Vedic doctrines that stand against the reality of non-duality, which are untrue since they contradict one another.

Bhāṣya How is settling the syllable Om a means of knowing the truth of the Self? By the texts ‘Om is that’, ‘this is the support’, ‘this, Satyakāma’, ‘one should concentrate on the Self as Om’, ‘Om is Brahman’, ‘Om alone is all this’. As the non-dual Self, the highest truth, is the ground of the imagining of prāṇa and the rest, as the rope is of the imagined snake, so the whole manifold of speech, whose objects are those imaginings of the Self as prāṇa and the rest, is Om alone. And Om is the very nature of the Self, since it names it. All the imagining of the Self as prāṇa and the rest, named by the words that are modifications of Om, has no being apart from its name: ‘a modification is a name, resting on speech’, ‘all this of his is bound by speech as the cord and by names as the nooses’, ‘all this is names’.

Bhāṣya All this mass of things that are named is not apart from its name, and the name is not apart from Om: so Om alone is all this. The supreme Brahman too is known only through the means of name and named, so it is Om alone. upavyākhyāna: a clear telling of this syllable Om, whose nature is the higher and the lower Brahman, clear because, being the means of knowing Brahman, it is near to Brahman; ‘is to be known as begun’ is to be supplied. What is bounded by the three times is Om by the reasoning given; and what is beyond the three times, known from its effects and not bounded by time, the unmanifest and the like, that too is Om alone.

सर्वं ह्येतद्ब्रह्मायमात्मा ब्रह्म सोऽयमात्मा चतुष्पात् ॥ २ ॥
sarvaṃ hy etad brahmāyam ātmā brahma so 'yam ātmā catuṣpāt || 2 ||
Padaccheda
सर्वम् हि एतत् ब्रह्म अयम् आत्मा ब्रह्म सः अयम् आत्मा चतुष्पात्
sarvam hi etat brahma ayam ātmā brahma saḥ ayam ātmā catuṣpāt
Word by word
For all this is Brahman. This Self is Brahman. This same Self has four quarters.

Introduction Though name and named are one, the statement ‘Om, this syllable is all this’ was made with the name as chief. What was so stated is stated again with the named as chief, so that the oneness of name and named may be known; otherwise, since the named is known in dependence on the name, it might be thought that the named is the name only by a figure. The purpose of knowing their oneness is that, dissolving name and named together by a single effort, one may know Brahman, which is other than both. So it will be said: ‘the quarters are the measures and the measures are the quarters’.

Bhāṣya All that was said to be Om only is Brahman. That Brahman, spoken of as out of sight, he points out directly and in particular: ‘this Self is Brahman’. ‘This’: with a gesture he points to it as the inner Self, as it is being divided into four quarters. This Self, named by Om, which stands as higher and lower, has four quarters as a kārṣāpaṇa coin has, not as a cow has. Since the fourth is reached by dissolving the three, Viśva and the others, each in the one after it, the word pāda means for them ‘that by which one reaches’; for the fourth it means ‘that which is reached’.

जागरितस्थानो बहिःप्रज्ञः सप्ताङ्ग एकोनविंशतिमुखः स्थूलभुग्वैश्वानरः प्रथमः पादः ॥ ३ ॥
jāgaritasthāno bahiḥprajñaḥ saptāṅga ekonaviṃśatimukhaḥ sthūlabhug vaiśvānaraḥ prathamaḥ pādaḥ || 3 ||
Padaccheda
जागरित-स्थानः बहिः-प्रज्ञः सप्त-अङ्गः एकोनविंशति-मुखः स्थूल-भुक् वैश्वानरः प्रथमः पादः
jāgarita-sthānaḥ bahiḥ-prajñaḥ sapta-aṅgaḥ ekonaviṃśati-mukhaḥ sthūla-bhuk vaiśvānaraḥ prathamaḥ pādaḥ
Word by word
The first quarter is Vaiśvānara, whose place is the waking state, who is conscious of what is outside, who has seven limbs and nineteen mouths, and who enjoys the gross.

Introduction How has it four quarters? He says:

Bhāṣya Bahiṣprajña: his consciousness is of objects other than himself; the sense is that his consciousness, made by ignorance, appears as though of the outside. Seven limbs: ‘of this Self, Vaiśvānara, the head is the bright (heaven), the eye is the many-formed (sun), the breath is that of varied paths (the wind), the trunk is the wide (space), the bladder is wealth (water), the feet are the earth’; and, to complete the imagined Agnihotra, the āhavanīya fire is called his mouth. Nineteen mouths: the ten organs of knowing and of acting, the five breaths, prāṇa and the rest, the mind, the understanding, the sense of ‘I’ and the thinking faculty (citta); they are like mouths, that is, doors of perception. So qualified, Vaiśvānara enjoys through these doors the gross objects, sound and the rest. Vaiśvānara: because he leads all men in many ways; or he is all (viśva) and he is man (nara), Viśvānara, and Viśvānara is Vaiśvānara, because he is not other than the self of all bodies. He is the first quarter because the later quarters are reached through him.

Bhāṣya Objection: when the four quarters of this inner Self are the subject, in ‘this Self is Brahman’, how can heaven and the rest be its head and limbs? There is no fault: what is meant is that the whole manifold, with the sphere of the gods, has four quarters through this Self. So, when the whole manifold is stilled, non-duality is established; the one Self is seen in all beings and all beings in the Self, and the sense of the text ‘but he who sees all beings (in the Self)’ is gathered up. Otherwise the inner Self would be seen as bounded by one's own body, as by the Sāṃkhyas and others, and then the special teaching of the Veda, non-duality, would be lost, there being no difference from the view of the Sāṃkhyas. And it is held that all the Upaniṣads teach the oneness of the Self of all. So it is right that the seven limbs are spoken of with the oneness in view of this self in the body with Virāj, the Self in the sphere of the gods, whose limbs are heaven and the rest; and there is the sign ‘your head would have fallen off’. The oneness with Virāj stands also for that (of Taijasa and Prājña) with Hiraṇyagarbha and the unmanifest; it is said in the Madhu-brāhmaṇa, ‘this Puruṣa in this earth, made of light, deathless, and this in the body’. The oneness of the sleeper and the unmanifest is settled already, since both are without particulars. So it will be established that when all duality is stilled there is non-duality.

स्वप्नस्थानोऽन्तःप्रज्ञः सप्ताङ्ग एकोनविंशतिमुखः प्रविविक्तभुक्तैजसो द्वितीयः पादः ॥ ४ ॥
svapnasthāno 'ntaḥprajñaḥ saptāṅga ekonaviṃśatimukhaḥ praviviktabhuk taijaso dvitīyaḥ pādaḥ || 4 ||
Padaccheda
स्वप्न-स्थानः अन्तः-प्रज्ञः सप्त-अङ्गः एकोनविंशति-मुखः प्रविविक्त-भुक् तैजसः द्वितीयः पादः
svapna-sthānaḥ antaḥ-prajñaḥ sapta-aṅgaḥ ekonaviṃśati-mukhaḥ pravivikta-bhuk taijasaḥ dvitīyaḥ pādaḥ
Word by word
The second quarter is Taijasa, whose place is dream, who is conscious of what is within, who has seven limbs and nineteen mouths, and who enjoys the subtle.

Bhāṣya The consciousness of waking, which has many means and appears as though of the outside though it is only a stirring of the mind, lays in the mind an impression of the same kind. The mind, so impressed, like a painted cloth, needing no outer means and driven by ignorance, desire and action, appears as in waking. So it is said: ‘taking with him a portion of this all-containing world’; and in the Atharvaṇa, beginning ‘(all) becomes one in the highest god, the mind’, ‘there, in dream, this god experiences his greatness’. The mind is within, compared with the senses; he whose consciousness in dream has the form of the mind's impressions is antaḥprajña. Taijasa (‘of light’): because he is the subject in a consciousness that is empty of objects and is mere light. For Viśva the consciousness enjoyed is gross, having objects; here the consciousness enjoyed is of impressions only: so the enjoyment is pravivikta, subtle. The rest is the same.

यत्र सुप्तो न कञ्चन कामं कामयते न कञ्चन स्वप्नं पश्यति तत्सुषुप्तम् । सुषुप्तस्थान एकीभूतः प्रज्ञानघन एवानन्दमयो ह्यानन्दभुक्चेतोमुखः प्राज्ञस्तृतीयः पादः ॥ ५ ॥
yatra supto na kañcana kāmaṃ kāmayate na kañcana svapnaṃ paśyati tat suṣuptam | suṣuptasthāna ekībhūtaḥ prajñānaghana evānandamayo hy ānandabhuk cetomukhaḥ prājñas tṛtīyaḥ pādaḥ || 5 ||
Padaccheda
यत्र सुप्तः न कञ्चन कामम् कामयते न कञ्चन स्वप्नम् पश्यति तत् सुषुप्तम् सुषुप्त-स्थानः एकी-भूतः प्रज्ञान-घनः एव आनन्द-मयः हि आनन्द-भुक् चेतः-मुखः प्राज्ञः तृतीयः पादः
yatra suptaḥ na kañcana kāmam kāmayate na kañcana svapnam paśyati tat suṣuptam suṣupta-sthānaḥ ekī-bhūtaḥ prajñāna-ghanaḥ eva ānanda-mayaḥ hi ānanda-bhuk cetaḥ-mukhaḥ prājñaḥ tṛtīyaḥ pādaḥ
Word by word
Where one asleep desires no desire and sees no dream, that is deep sleep. The third quarter is Prājña, whose place is deep sleep, who has become one, who is a mass of consciousness only, who abounds in bliss and enjoys bliss, and who is the doorway to consciousness (of dream and waking).

Introduction Sleep, whose mark is not waking to the truth, is alike in the states with and without seeing; so ‘where one asleep’ and the rest is a qualification, to pick out deep sleep. Or: sleep, as not waking to the truth, is the same in all three states; so he sets deep sleep apart from the two before.

Bhāṣya In deep sleep there is neither the seeing of dreams, which is a grasping of things as other than they are, as in the two states before, nor any desire. ekībhūta: the whole of duality, a stirring of the mind, divided in the two states, has, without giving up that nature, become undistinguished, like the day with all its variety swallowed by the darkness of night. Hence the cognitions that are the stirrings of the mind in dream and waking are as though massed together; this state, being undistinguished, is called prajñānaghana, as at night everything, not distinguished because of the darkness, is like one mass. eva: there is nothing of another kind apart from consciousness. ānandamaya: abounding in bliss, because the pain of the effort of the mind's stirring as object and subject is absent; not bliss itself. ānandabhuk: as in the world one who stays without effort is called happy, an enjoyer of bliss, so this state of utter effortlessness is experienced by him; ‘this is his highest bliss’. cetomukha: because he is the doorway to the consciousness of dream and the rest; or, consciousness, whose mark is waking, is his door, his mouth, for coming to dream and the rest. Prājña: his is the knowing of past and future and of all objects; even when asleep he is called so from his former state. Or, mere knowing is the form peculiar to him, while the other two have knowledge of particulars as well.

एष सर्वेश्वर एष सर्वज्ञ एषोऽन्तर्याम्येष योनिः सर्वस्य प्रभवाप्ययौ हि भूतानाम् ॥ ६ ॥
eṣa sarveśvara eṣa sarvajña eṣo 'ntaryāmy eṣa yoniḥ sarvasya prabhavāpyayau hi bhūtānām || 6 ||
Padaccheda
एषः सर्व-ईश्वरः एषः सर्वज्ञः एषः अन्तर्यामी एषः योनिः सर्वस्य प्रभव-अप्ययौ हि भूतानाम्
eṣaḥ sarva-īśvaraḥ eṣaḥ sarvajñaḥ eṣaḥ antaryāmī eṣaḥ yoniḥ sarvasya prabhava-apyayau hi bhūtānām
Word by word
He is the lord of all, he is the knower of all, he is the inner ruler, he is the source of all, for he is the origin and the end of beings.

Bhāṣya He (Prājña), abiding in his own nature, is the lord of all, the ruler of the whole of what is differentiated, with the sphere of the gods; (the lord) is not something of another kind than this, as others hold; ‘for the mind, dear one, is bound to prāṇa’. He, present in all the states of difference, is the knower of all. He is the inner ruler: entering within, he is the controller of all beings. From him the world, with its differences as told, is born: so he is the source of all. And since that is so, he alone is the origin and the end of beings.

Kārikā 1
अत्रैते श्लोका भवन्तिatraite ślokā bhavanti
बहिःप्रज्ञो विभुर्विश्वो ह्यन्तःप्रज्ञस्तु तैजसः । घनप्रज्ञस्तथा प्राज्ञ एक एव त्रिधा स्मृतः ॥ १ ॥
bahiḥprajño vibhur viśvo hy antaḥprajñas tu taijasaḥ | ghanaprajñas tathā prājña eka eva tridhā smṛtaḥ || 1 ||
Padaccheda
बहिः-प्रज्ञः विभुः विश्वः हि अन्तः-प्रज्ञः तु तैजसः घन-प्रज्ञः तथा प्राज्ञः एकः एव त्रिधा स्मृतः
bahiḥ-prajñaḥ vibhuḥ viśvaḥ hi antaḥ-prajñaḥ tu taijasaḥ ghana-prajñaḥ tathā prājñaḥ ekaḥ eva tridhā smṛtaḥ
Word by word
Viśva, all-pervading, is conscious of what is outside; Taijasa is conscious of what is within; Prājña likewise is a mass of consciousness: one alone is thought of in three ways.

Note The kārikās are Gauḍapāda's (Āgama-prakaraṇa, 1–29). In Śaṅkara's text they stand in four groups, after mantras 6, 7, 11 and 12, each brought in by the words atraite ślokā bhavanti, ‘on this there are these verses’. Śaṅkara glosses the words here: on this matter, as it has been stated, there are these verses.

Bhāṣya The purport is this. Because he is in the three states by turns, and because he is recognised through the memory ‘I am that same one’, it is established that he is other than the three states, one, pure and unattached; and from the śruti's illustrations of the great fish and the rest.

Kārikā 2
दक्षिणाक्षिमुखे विश्वो मनस्यन्तस्तु तैजसः । आकाशे च हृदि प्राज्ञस्त्रिधा देहे व्यवस्थितः ॥ २ ॥
dakṣiṇākṣimukhe viśvo manasy antas tu taijasaḥ | ākāśe ca hṛdi prājñas tridhā dehe vyavasthitaḥ || 2 ||
Padaccheda
दक्षिण-अक्षि-मुखे विश्वः मनसि अन्तर् तु तैजसः आकाशे च हृदि प्राज्ञः त्रिधा देहे व्यवस्थितः
dakṣiṇa-akṣi-mukhe viśvaḥ manasi antar tu taijasaḥ ākāśe ca hṛdi prājñaḥ tridhā dehe vyavasthitaḥ
Word by word
Viśva is in the right eye, his doorway; Taijasa is within, in the mind; Prājña is in the space in the heart: in three ways he is stationed in the body.

Introduction This verse is to show that the three, Viśva and the others, are experienced in the waking state itself.

Bhāṣya On Viśva: the right eye itself is the doorway; there, chiefly, Viśva, the seer of gross things, is experienced, as the śruti says: ‘Indha by name is this person in the right eye’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.2.2). Indha, of shining quality, is Vaiśvānara. The Virāj self within the sun and the seer in the eye are one. Objection: Hiraṇyagarbha is one; the knower of the field, who rules the eyes, the seer in the right eye and lord of the body, is another. No: no difference in themselves is admitted, from the śruti ‘one god, hidden in all beings’ (Śvetāśvatara 6.11) and the smṛti ‘know me as the knower of the field in all fields’, ‘undivided, yet standing as if divided in beings’ (Gītā 13.2, 13.16). Though he is in all the organs alike, keenness of perception is seen in the right eye, so Viśva is pointed out there in particular.

Bhāṣya On Taijasa and Prājña: having seen a form while in the right eye, he closes his eyes and, remembering it, sees that same thing within the mind, as in dream, manifested in the form of impressions. As here, so in dream. So Taijasa, within the mind, is Viśva himself. In the space in the heart, when the activity called memory ceases, he is Prājña, become one, a mass of consciousness, for the activity of the mind is absent. Seeing and remembering are the stirrings of the mind; when they are absent he stays in the heart itself, without distinction, as prāṇa: ‘prāṇa indeed absorbs all these’ (Chāndogya 4.3.3). Taijasa is Hiraṇyagarbha, being seated in the mind: ‘the mind is the mark’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.6), ‘this person consists of mind’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 5.6.1).

Bhāṣya On prāṇa as the unmanifest: objection: in deep sleep prāṇa is manifest, and the organs become of its nature; how is it the unmanifest? No fault: the unmanifest is without distinction of place and time. Though prāṇa is indeed manifest while there is identification with prāṇa, in deep sleep the identification with the particular bounded by the body ceases and one becomes prāṇa; so for those who identify with the bounded, prāṇa in deep sleep is the unmanifest. As prāṇa is unmanifest at the dissolution of prāṇa for those who identify with the bounded, so for one who identifies with prāṇa too, on reaching non-distinction, the unmanifestness is the same, and its being the seed of production; and its overseer is one, in the unmanifest state. Those who identify with the bounded, and their overseers, are one with him; so the epithets given before, ‘become one, a mass of consciousness’ and the rest, are fitting.

Bhāṣya On why the unmanifest is called prāṇa: from the śruti ‘the mind, dear one, is tied to prāṇa’ (Chāndogya 6.8.2). Objection: there it is Brahman, the existent of ‘the existent alone, dear one’ (6.2.1), that is named prāṇa. No fault: the existent is admitted to be of the nature of a seed. Though Brahman is there named prāṇa, it is named prāṇa, and named the existent, without giving up its being the seed from which the jīvas are produced. If Brahman without seed had been meant, it would have said ‘not this, not this’, ‘from which words turn back’ (Taittirīya 2.9), ‘other than the known, and above the unknown’ (Kena 1.3); and the smṛti says ‘it is called neither existent nor non-existent’ (Gītā 13.12). If they merged in the seedless, those merged in the existent in deep sleep and at dissolution could not rise again; the liberated would be born again, the absence of seed being the same; and with no seed for knowledge to burn, knowledge would be without purpose. Therefore it is only as admitted to have a seed that the existent is spoken of as prāṇa, and as the cause in all the śrutis. Hence it is described by the removal of its having a seed in ‘higher than the high imperishable’, ‘without and within, unborn’ (Muṇḍaka 2.1.2), ‘from which words turn back’ and ‘not this, not this’. That ultimately real state of the same one who is named Prājña, free of connexion with body and the rest and of waking and the rest, he will state separately as the fourth. The seed state too is experienced in the body, from the cognition of one who has risen, ‘I knew nothing’; so it is said, ‘in three ways he is stationed in the body’.

Introduction The fourth quarter, come to in its turn, is to be told. Since it is empty of every ground for the use of a word, it cannot be named by words; so he sets out to point to the fourth (turīya) only by denying particulars. Then is it a mere void? No: a false imagining cannot be without a ground; silver, a snake, a man, a mirage cannot be imagined on nothing, without mother-of-pearl, a rope, a post, a salt waste. Then, as the ground of all the imaginings, prāṇa and the rest, the fourth can be expressed by a word, as a pot is as the holder of water, and need not be made known by denials? No: the imagining of prāṇa and the rest is unreal, like the silver in mother-of-pearl, and between the real and the unreal there is no relation that could be the ground for the use of a word, for such a relation is no real thing. Nor is the Self in its own form the object of another means of knowledge, as a cow is, for it has no adjuncts; nor has it a class, as a cow has, for, being without a second, it has neither general nor particular; nor action, as a cook has, for it is changeless; nor quality, as blue, for it is without qualities. So it cannot be pointed to by a name.

Bhāṣya Then is it without purpose, like a hare's horn? No: when the fourth is known as the Self, the thirst for what is not the Self turns back, as the thirst for silver does when the mother-of-pearl is known; once the fourth is known as the Self, the faults, ignorance, thirst and the rest, cannot be. And nothing prevents the fourth being known as the Self, for all the Upaniṣads end in that: ‘you are that’, ‘this Self is Brahman’, ‘that is the true, that is the Self’, ‘the Brahman that is immediate and direct’, ‘he is without and within, unborn’, ‘the Self alone is all this’. This Self, with a real and an unreal form, was said to have four quarters. Its unreal form, made by ignorance, like the snake in the rope, the three quarters, which stand as seed and sprout, has been told. Now its real nature, which is not seed, which stands as the rope, he tells by setting aside the three states told, which stand as the snake.

Bhāṣya Objection: once the Self has been declared to have four quarters and three have been told, it is settled that the fourth is other than ‘conscious of what is within’ and the rest; the denial is pointless. No: as the rope's own nature is known by the denial of the imagined snake, so it is the very Self of the three states that is to be taught as the fourth, as in ‘you are that’. If the fourth were something else, unlike the Self of the three states, there would be no door to knowing it, and the teaching of the scripture would be pointless, or a void would follow. When the one Self is imagined in the three states as conscious within and the rest, as a rope is imagined as a snake, then, at the very time of the valid knowledge that is the denial of ‘conscious within’ and the rest, the fruit, the ceasing of the manifold of harm in the Self, is complete; no other means of knowledge and no other means need be sought for reaching the fourth, just as, when rope and snake are told apart and the snake has ceased in the rope, none is needed for knowing the rope.

Bhāṣya For those who hold that the means of knowledge works on the knowing of the pot over and above the removing of the darkness, it would follow that the act of cutting works on one of the parts as well, over and above the parting of the parts of the thing cut. When the means of knowledge that is set to tell pot and darkness apart ends in its fruit, the removal of the unwanted darkness, as cutting, set to part the joined parts of the thing cut, ends in their being two, then the knowledge of the pot follows of itself; it is not the fruit of the means of knowledge. In the same way the valid knowledge that is denial, set to tell apart the ‘conscious within’ and the rest that are laid on the Self, cannot work on the fourth over and above the removal of the unwanted ‘conscious within’ and the rest; for at the very time these cease, the difference of knower and the rest ceases. So it will be said: ‘when it is known, there is no duality’. Knowledge does not stay for another moment after the moment in which duality ceases; if it stayed there would be a regress, and duality would not cease. So it is settled that the harm laid on the Self, ‘conscious within’ and the rest, ceases at the very time the valid knowledge that is denial does its work.

Bhāṣya ‘not conscious of what is within’ denies Taijasa; ‘not conscious of what is outside’, Viśva; ‘not conscious of both’, the state between waking and dream; ‘not a mass of consciousness’, the state of deep sleep, which is the undistinguished state of seed; ‘not conscious’, the being a knower of all objects at once; ‘not unconscious’, the absence of consciousness. How is it known from the denial that ‘conscious within’ and the rest, which are perceived in the Self, are unreal, like the snake in the rope? Though their nature as the knower is the same, they fail one another, like the snake, the streak of water and the rest imagined in the rope; the nature of the knower, which nowhere fails, is real. If it is said that it fails in deep sleep: no, for deep sleep is experienced; ‘there is no loss of the knowing of the knower’.

Bhāṣya Hence it is unseen; because unseen, beyond dealings; not to be grasped by the organs of action; without mark, that is, without an inferential sign, not to be inferred; hence unthinkable; hence not to be named by words. ekātmapratyayasāra: to be traced by the cognition, which never fails, that in the states of waking and the rest this Self is one; or, that for the reaching of which the one cognition of the Self is the proof (sāra); ‘one should meditate on it as the Self only’. The properties of those who have the states, ‘conscious within’ and the rest, have been denied; by prapañcopaśama the absence of the properties of the states, waking and the rest, is told. Hence it is calm, changeless; auspicious, since it is non-dual, free of imagined difference. They hold it to be the fourth because it is unlike the three quarters that appear. ‘That is the Self, that is to be known’: as the rope is other than the snake, the crack in the ground, the stick and the rest that appear, so is the Self that is the sense of sentences such as ‘you are that’, spoken of in ‘the unseen seer’, ‘there is no loss of the seeing of the seer’. ‘To be known’ is said from the standpoint of what went before; when it is known there is no duality.

Introduction What Viśva and the others have in common and what is particular to each is now determined, in order to ascertain the true nature of the fourth.

Bhāṣya Kārya, what is made, is the state of fruit; kāraṇa, what makes, is the state of seed. Viśva and Taijasa, as described, are held to be bound, held fast, by the states of seed and fruit: the non-grasping of the truth and the grasping of it otherwise. Prājña is bound by the state of seed alone, for mere non-awakening to the truth is the seed, the cause of his being Prājña. So those two, the states of seed and fruit, the non-grasping of the truth and the grasping of it otherwise, do not hold good in the fourth: they are not found there, they are not possible.

So he is bound by the non-grasping of the truth, the darkness that is the seed of grasping it otherwise. The fourth is always the seer of all: since nothing other than the fourth exists, it is always; and it is all, and the seer of that. Therefore the seed, marked by the non-grasping of the truth, is not there; and for the same reason the grasping otherwise that is born of it is absent. In the sun, whose nature is always light, there cannot be what is opposed to it, not shining, or shining otherwise: ‘there is no loss of the seeing of the seer’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.3.23). Or else: in waking and dream it is the fourth itself that abides in all beings and appears as the seer of all things; so it is ‘always the seer of all’: ‘there is no other seer than this’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 3.8.11).

Since the fourth is always of the nature of seeing, the sleep marked by non-awakening to the truth is not found in it; so there is no bond of cause in it.

Kārikā 15

Padaccheda

Introduction When does one become certain in the fourth?

Bhāṣya In dream and in waking there is ‘dream’ for one who grasps the truth otherwise, like a snake in a rope. Sleep, which is his who does not know the truth, is the same in the three states. Since dream and sleep are common to them, Viśva and Taijasa make one class; and since grasping otherwise predominates there, sleep is subordinate, so that in them the error is dream. In the third state the error is sleep alone, marked by ignorance of the truth. So when the error in these two, the states of effect and cause, marked by grasping otherwise and by non-grasping, and having the form of the bond of effect and cause, has wasted away through awakening to the highest truth, one attains the fourth state. Then, not seeing in it the bond of either mark, one becomes certain in the fourth.

Bhāṣya This transmigrating jīva is asleep: through the dream marked by illusion, at work from beginningless time and of two marks, the seed that is non-awakening to the truth and the grasping of it otherwise, he sees in both states dreams of this kind: ‘this is my father, this my son, this my grandson; mine are the field and the cattle; I am their master; I am happy, I am unhappy; by this I am diminished, by that increased.’ When a supremely compassionate teacher, who knows the truth that the Vedānta means, awakens him with ‘you are not thus, made of cause and effect; rather, that thou art’, then he awakes thus. How? In it there is no change of state, birth and the rest, outer or inner; so it is unborn, that is, free of all changes of state, without and within. Since sleep, the seed that is the darkness of ignorance and the cause of birth and the rest, is not found in it, it is sleepless. The fourth is sleepless, and for that very reason dreamless, grasping otherwise being caused by that. And since it is sleepless and dreamless, he then knows the unborn, non-dual fourth as the Self.

Bhāṣya That would be so if the manifold existed; but, being imagined like the snake in the rope, it does not exist. If it existed it would cease, no doubt. Kārikā 20
तैजसस्योत्वविज्ञान उत्कर्षो दृश्यते स्फुटम् । मात्रासंप्रतिपत्तौ स्यादुभयत्वं तथा विधम् ॥ २० ॥
taijasasyotvavijñāna utkarṣo dṛśyate sphuṭam | mātrāsaṃpratipattau syād ubhayatvaṃ tathā vidham || 20 ||
Padaccheda
तैजसस्य उत्व-विज्ञाने उत्कर्षः दृश्यते स्फुटम् मात्रा-संप्रतिपत्तौ स्यात् उभयत्वम् तथा-विधम्
taijasasya utva-vijñāne utkarṣaḥ dṛśyate sphuṭam mātrā-saṃpratipattau syāt ubhayatvam tathā-vidham
Word by word
When Taijasa is known as the letter ‘u’, his being higher is clearly seen; and, when he is understood as that measure, his belonging to both is (clear) in the same way.

Bhāṣya When Taijasa's being ‘u’ is meant, his being higher is seen clearly, plainly; and his belonging to both is just as clear. All is as before.

Kārikā 21
मकारभावे प्राज्ञस्य मानसामान्यमुत्कटम् । मात्रासंप्रतिपत्तौ तु लयसामान्यमेव च ॥ २१ ॥
makārabhāve prājñasya mānasāmānyam utkaṭam | mātrāsaṃpratipattau tu layasāmānyam eva ca || 21 ||

मकार-भावे प्राज्ञस्य मान-सामान्यम् उत्कटम् मात्रा-संप्रतिपत्तौ तु लय-सामान्यम् एव च
makāra-bhāve prājñasya māna-sāmānyam utkaṭam mātrā-saṃpratipattau tu laya-sāmānyam eva ca

In Prājña's being the letter ‘m’, the likeness of measuring is prominent; and, when he is understood as that measure, the likeness of merging too.

Kārikā 22
त्रिषु धामसु यत्तुल्यं सामान्यं वेत्ति निश्चितः । स पूज्यः सर्वभूतानां वन्द्यश्चैव महामुनिः ॥ २२ ॥
triṣu dhāmasu yat tulyaṃ sāmānyaṃ vetti niścitaḥ | sa pūjyaḥ sarvabhūtānāṃ vandyaś caiva mahāmuniḥ || 22 ||
Padaccheda
त्रिषु धामसु यत् तुल्यम् सामान्यम् वेत्ति निश्चितः सः पूज्यः सर्व-भूतानाम् वन्द्यः च एव महा-मुनिः
triṣu dhāmasu yat tulyam sāmānyam vetti niścitaḥ saḥ pūjyaḥ sarva-bhūtānām vandyaḥ ca eva mahā-muniḥ
Word by word
He who, being certain, knows the likeness that is the same in the three abodes is to be honoured and revered by all beings, a great sage.

Bhāṣya He who knows the likeness stated to be the same in the three states described, being certain that it is just so, is honoured and revered in the world as a knower of Brahman.

Kārikā 23
अकारो नयते विश्वमुकारश्चापि तैजसम् । मकारश्च पुनः प्राज्ञं नामात्रे विद्यते गतिः ॥ २३ ॥
akāro nayate viśvam ukāraś cāpi taijasam | makāraś ca punaḥ prājñaṃ nāmātre vidyate gatiḥ || 23 ||
Padaccheda
अकारः नयते विश्वम् उकारः च अपि तैजसम् मकारः च पुनर् प्राज्ञम् न अमात्रे विद्यते गतिः
akāraḥ nayate viśvam ukāraḥ ca api taijasam makāraḥ ca punar prājñam na amātre vidyate gatiḥ
Word by word
The letter ‘a’ leads to Viśva, the letter ‘u’ to Taijasa, and the letter ‘m’ to Prājña; in what is without measure there is no going.

Introduction Him who, having made the quarters of the Self one with the measures by the likenesses stated, and having understood the syllable Om as described, meditates on it, (the letter ‘a’ leads …).

Bhāṣya The letter ‘a’ leads to Viśva, brings him to Viśva: one who knows the syllable Om as resting on ‘a’ becomes Vaiśvānara. Likewise the letter ‘u’ to Taijasa, and the letter ‘m’ to Prājña; ‘leads’ is carried on because of the word ca. But when m has wasted away, the state of seed being destroyed, there is no going anywhere in the syllable Om without measure.

अमात्रश्चतुर्थोऽव्यवहार्यः प्रपञ्चोपशमः शिवोऽद्वैतः । एवमोंकार आत्मैव । संविशत्यात्मनात्मानं य एवं वेद ॥ १२ ॥
amātraś caturtho 'vyavahāryaḥ prapañcopaśamaḥ śivo 'dvaitaḥ | evam oṃkāra ātmaiva | saṃviśaty ātmanātmānaṃ ya evaṃ veda || 12 ||
Padaccheda
अमात्रः चतुर्थः अव्यवहार्यः प्रपञ्च-उपशमः शिवः अद्वैतः एवम् ओंकारः आत्मा एव संविशति आत्मना आत्मानम् यः एवम् वेद
amātraḥ caturthaḥ avyavahāryaḥ prapañca-upaśamaḥ śivaḥ advaitaḥ evam oṃkāraḥ ātmā eva saṃviśati ātmanā ātmānam yaḥ evam veda
Word by word
The fourth is without measure, beyond dealings, the stilling of the manifold, auspicious, non-dual. Thus the syllable Om is the Self alone. He who knows thus enters the Self by the self.

Bhāṣya Amātra: that which has no measure, the Om without measure, is the fourth, the Self alone. It is beyond dealings because name and named, which are speech and mind, have wasted away; it is the stilling of the manifold, auspicious, non-dual. Thus the Om of three measures, used by one who has the knowledge told, is the Self of three quarters. He who knows thus enters by his own self his own real Self. The seer of the highest truth, the knower of Brahman, having burnt the third, the state of seed, has entered the Self and is not born again, for the fourth is not a seed. When rope and snake have been told apart, the snake that has entered the rope does not rise again as before, from the impression in the mind, for those who have told them apart.

Bhāṣya For those of slow and of middling understanding, who have taken up the state of a seeker, walk the right path and are renouncers, and who know the likenesses laid down between the measures and the quarters, the syllable Om, duly meditated on, becomes a support for the knowing of Brahman; so it will be said, ‘the stages of life are of three kinds’.

Kārikā 24
अत्रैते श्लोका भवन्तिatraite ślokā bhavanti
ओंकारं पादशो विद्यात्पादा मात्रा न संशयः । ओंकारं पादशो ज्ञात्वा न किञ्चिदपि चिन्तयेत् ॥ २४ ॥
oṃkāraṃ pādaśo vidyāt pādā mātrā na saṃśayaḥ | oṃkāraṃ pādaśo jñātvā na kiñcid api cintayet || 24 ||
Padaccheda
ओंकारम् पादशः विद्यात् पादाः मात्राः न संशयः ओंकारम् पादशः ज्ञात्वा न किञ्चित् अपि चिन्तयेत्
oṃkāram pādaśaḥ vidyāt pādāḥ mātrāḥ na saṃśayaḥ oṃkāram pādaśaḥ jñātvā na kiñcit api cintayet
Word by word
One should know the syllable Om quarter by quarter; the quarters are the measures, no doubt. Having known the syllable Om quarter by quarter, one should think of nothing at all.

Introduction Introducing the kārikās: as before, on this there are these verses.

Bhāṣya By the likenesses stated, the quarters are the measures and the measures the quarters; therefore one should know the syllable Om quarter by quarter. When Om is known thus, one should not think of any purpose, seen or unseen, since one's ends are accomplished.

Kārikā 25
युञ्जीत प्रणवे चेतः प्रणवो ब्रह्म निर्भयम् । प्रणवे नित्ययुक्तस्य न भयं विद्यते क्वचित् ॥ २५ ॥
yuñjīta praṇave cetaḥ praṇavo brahma nirbhayam | praṇave nityayuktasya na bhayaṃ vidyate kvacit || 25 ||
Padaccheda
युञ्जीत प्रणवे चेतः प्रणवः ब्रह्म निर्भयम् प्रणवे नित्य-युक्तस्य न भयम् विद्यते क्वचित्
yuñjīta praṇave cetaḥ praṇavaḥ brahma nirbhayam praṇave nitya-yuktasya na bhayam vidyate kvacit
Word by word
One should fix the mind on Om; Om is Brahman, the fearless. For one ever fixed on Om there is no fear anywhere.

Bhāṣya Yuñjīta = one should settle the mind in absorption on Om, whose nature is the highest truth, as it has been explained; because Om is Brahman, the fearless. For one always fixed on it there is no fear anywhere, as the śruti says: ‘he who knows fears nothing whatever’ (Taittirīya 2.9).

Kārikā 26
प्रणवो ह्यपरं ब्रह्म प्रणवश्च परः स्मृतः । अपूर्वोऽनन्तरोऽबाह्योऽनपरः प्रणवोऽव्ययः ॥ २६ ॥
praṇavo hy aparaṃ brahma praṇavaś ca paraḥ smṛtaḥ | apūrvo 'nantaro 'bāhyo 'naparaḥ praṇavo 'vyayaḥ || 26 ||
Padaccheda
प्रणवः हि अपरम् ब्रह्म प्रणवः च परः स्मृतः अपूर्वः अनन्तरः अबाह्यः अनपरः प्रणवः अव्ययः
praṇavaḥ hi aparam brahma praṇavaḥ ca paraḥ smṛtaḥ apūrvaḥ anantaraḥ abāhyaḥ anaparaḥ praṇavaḥ avyayaḥ
Word by word
Om is the lower Brahman, and Om is thought of as the higher. Om is without a cause, without anything within it, without anything outside it, without an effect, changeless.

Bhāṣya Om is both the higher and the lower Brahman. When, in truth, the measures and quarters have wasted away, it is the higher Self alone, Brahman; so nothing before it, no cause of it, exists: apūrva. Nothing of a different kind exists within it: anantara. Likewise nothing other exists outside it: abāhya. Nothing after it, no effect of it, exists: anapara. That is, it is ‘without and within, unborn’ (Muṇḍaka 2.1.2), a mass of consciousness, like a lump of salt.

Kārikā 27
सर्वस्य प्रणवो ह्यादिर्मध्यमन्तस्तथैव च । एवं हि प्रणवं ज्ञात्वा व्यश्नुते तदनन्तरम् ॥ २७ ॥
sarvasya praṇavo hy ādir madhyam antas tathaiva ca | evaṃ hi praṇavaṃ jñātvā vyaśnute tad anantaram || 27 ||
Padaccheda
सर्वस्य प्रणवः हि आदिः मध्यम् अन्तः तथा एव च एवम् हि प्रणवम् ज्ञात्वा व्यश्नुते तत् अनन्तरम्
sarvasya praṇavaḥ hi ādiḥ madhyam antaḥ tathā eva ca evam hi praṇavam jñātvā vyaśnute tat anantaram
Word by word
Om is the beginning, the middle and likewise the end of all. Having known Om thus, one attains that immediately.

Bhāṣya Beginning, middle and end are the origin, continuance and dissolution of everything: of the manifold of space and the rest, which arises like an illusory elephant, a rope-snake, a mirage or a dream, as the conjurer and the like are (the beginning, middle and end of those). Having thus known Om, the Self, which stands in the place of the conjurer and the like, one attains at that very moment the state of being that Self.

Kārikā 28
प्रणवं हीश्वरं विद्यात्सर्वस्य हृदि संस्थितम् । सर्वव्यापिनमोंकारं मत्वा धीरो न शोचति ॥ २८ ॥
praṇavaṃ hīśvaraṃ vidyāt sarvasya hṛdi saṃsthitam | sarvavyāpinam oṃkāraṃ matvā dhīro na śocati || 28 ||
Padaccheda
प्रणवम् हि ईश्वरम् विद्यात् सर्वस्य हृदि संस्थितम् सर्व-व्यापिनम् ओंकारम् मत्वा धीरः न शोचति
praṇavam hi īśvaram vidyāt sarvasya hṛdi saṃsthitam sarva-vyāpinam oṃkāram matvā dhīraḥ na śocati
Word by word
One should know Om as the Lord seated in the heart of all. Having thought on the all-pervading syllable Om, the wise man does not grieve.

Bhāṣya One should know Om as the Lord who stands in the heart, the seat of memory and cognition, of all living beings. The wise man, the man of understanding, having thought on the syllable Om, all-pervading like space, as the Self that does not transmigrate, does not grieve, since no cause of grief is possible; as the śrutis say, ‘the knower of the Self crosses over grief’ (Chāndogya 7.1.3).

Kārikā 29
अमात्रोऽनन्तमात्रश्च द्वैतस्योपशमः शिवः । ओंकारो विदितो येन स मुनिर्नेतरो जनः ॥ २९ ॥
amātro 'nantamātraś ca dvaitasyopaśamaḥ śivaḥ | oṃkāro vidito yena sa munir netaro janaḥ || 29 ||
Padaccheda
अमात्रः अनन्त-मात्रः च द्वैतस्य उपशमः शिवः ओंकारः विदितः येन सः मुनिः न इतरः जनः
amātraḥ ananta-mātraḥ ca dvaitasya upaśamaḥ śivaḥ oṃkāraḥ viditaḥ yena saḥ muniḥ na itaraḥ janaḥ
Word by word
Without measure and of endless measure, the stilling of duality, auspicious: he by whom the syllable Om is known (thus) is a sage, and no other person.

Bhāṣya Without measure is the fourth, the syllable Om. A mātrā is that by which something is measured, a limit; that whose measure is endless is anantamātra: its ‘so much’ cannot be delimited. It is auspicious just because it is the stilling of all duality. He by whom the syllable Om, as it has been explained, is known is a muni, from his thinking (manana) on the highest truth; no other person is, even one learned in the śāstras. (Here the Āgama-prakaraṇa ends.)
