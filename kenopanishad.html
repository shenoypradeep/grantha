Kenopaniṣad

Kena Upaniṣad

Sāmaveda · meanings following Śaṅkara's pada-bhāṣya

Four khaṇḍas, 35 mantras (Talavakāra, or Jaiminīya, Upaniṣad-Brāhmaṇa 4.18–21): by whom the mind and the senses are directed; Brahman, known to him to whom it is unknown; the story of Brahman and the gods; and the teaching by lightning and by the mind. Meanings and translation follow Śaṅkara's pada-bhāṣya.

प्रथमः खण्डः
Prathamaḥ khaṇḍaḥ · First khaṇḍa
केनेषितं पतति प्रेषितं मनः केन प्राणः प्रथमः प्रैति युक्तः । केनेषितां वाचमिमां वदन्ति चक्षुः श्रोत्रं क उ देवो युनक्ति ॥ १ ॥
keneṣitaṃ patati preṣitaṃ manaḥ kena prāṇaḥ prathamaḥ praiti yuktaḥ | keneṣitāṃ vācam imāṃ vadanti cakṣuḥ śrotraṃ ka u devo yunakti || 1 ||
Padaccheda
केन इषितम् पतति प्रेषितम् मनः केन प्राणः प्रथमः प्रैति युक्तः केन इषिताम् वाचम् इमाम् वदन्ति चक्षुः श्रोत्रम् कः उ देवः युनक्ति
kena iṣitam patati preṣitam manaḥ kena prāṇaḥ prathamaḥ praiti yuktaḥ kena iṣitām vācam imām vadanti cakṣuḥ śrotram kaḥ u devaḥ yunakti
Word by word
Willed by whom does the mind go, sent forth, (to its object)? Directed by whom does prāṇa, the first, go forth (to its work)? Willed by whom do people utter this speech? What god directs the eye and the ear?

Introduction Introducing the Upaniṣad: with keneṣitam the ninth adhyāya (of the Brāhmaṇa) begins, to teach the Upaniṣad, whose subject is the supreme Brahman. Before this the rites have been set out to the end, and the meditations on prāṇa, the support of all rites, and on the sāmans that are parts of rites; and after them the vision of the Gāyatra sāman, ending with the line of teachers. All this, rite and knowledge, rightly done, serves to purify the mind of one who seeks liberation without desire. For one who has desire and no knowledge, rites alone, Vedic and traditional, lead to the southern path and to return. From natural activity not ruled by scripture there is the downward course, from animals to the immovable (Chāndogya 5.10.8; Aitareya Āraṇyaka 2.1.1). But in one whose mind is pure and without desire, who has turned from the outer, impermanent connection of means and ends, through a particular impression that has arisen from what he has done here or before, the desire to know the inner Self arises. This is what the śruti shows, in the form of question and answer (so also Kaṭha 2.1.1, Muṇḍaka 1.2.12). Only one so dispassionate is able to hear, reflect on and know the inner Self.

Introduction Introducing the Upaniṣad (continued): by this knowledge of the inner Self as Brahman, ignorance, the seed of the round of birth and death and the cause of desire and action, ceases without remainder (Īśa 7; Chāndogya 7.1.3; Muṇḍaka 2.2.9). If it is said that this comes about from knowledge joined with rites: no. The Vājasaneyaka says that those are the cause of something else: beginning ‘may I have a wife’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.4.17), it says that this world is won by a son, the world of the ancestors by rites, the world of the gods by meditation (1.5.16): three worlds other than the Self. And there the reason for the wandering life is given: ‘what shall we do with offspring, we whose world is this Self?’ (4.4.22). Its sense: what shall we do with offspring, rites and the meditation joined to them, the means to the three worlds, which lead to a world that is not the Self? Our world is natural, unborn, undecaying, immortal, fearless; it neither grows by action nor lessens; and being eternal it is not to be brought about by any means other than the ceasing of ignorance. So the renunciation of all desires, resting on the knowledge of the inner Self as Brahman, is what is to be done. Moreover that knowledge cannot go together with rites: a rite takes for granted the difference of agents, means and fruits; the knowledge is of that in which every difference has set; and it depends on the thing, not on a person's will. The telling by way of a pupil's question and a teacher's answer makes a subtle matter easy to grasp, and shows that it is not to be reached by reasoning alone.

Bhāṣya Some one, having duly approached a teacher established in Brahman, seeing no refuge but the inner Self and wishing for the fearless, the eternal, the auspicious, the unmoving, asked this. iṣitam: wished, intended, by what agent; the root iṣ cannot here mean repeating or going, so the form is from iṣ ‘to wish’, with a Vedic iṭ. preṣitam is the same root with pra, in the sense of directing. With preṣitam alone one would ask by what sender and by what kind of sending; iṣitam removes both: sent by whose mere wish. If so, keneṣitam alone would do, and more words should give more sense: sent by wish, by act or by speech? No: from the force of the question it is one who has turned from the impermanent aggregate of body and senses and wants to know another thing, unchanging and eternal, who asks; that the aggregate is moved by wish, speech and act is well known, and the question would be idle. It is the question of one in doubt: is the mover the aggregate itself, as commonly held, or something apart from it, independent, moving the mind and the rest by its mere wish? The two words show this. And the mind is not independent: if it were, no one would think what he does not want; yet the mind wills what it knows to be harmful, and goes on to what brings sharp pain though held back. yuktaḥ: directed. ‘The first’ qualifies prāṇa, since the working of all the senses depends on it. devaḥ: the shining one.

श्रोत्रस्य श्रोत्रं मनसो मनो यद्वाचो ह वाचं स उ प्राणस्य प्राणः । चक्षुषश्चक्षुरतिमुच्य धीराः प्रेत्यास्माल्लोकादमृता भवन्ति ॥ २ ॥
śrotrasya śrotraṃ manaso mano yad vāco ha vācaṃ sa u prāṇasya prāṇaḥ | cakṣuṣaś cakṣur atimucya dhīrāḥ pretyāsmāl lokād amṛtā bhavanti || 2 ||
Padaccheda
श्रोत्रस्य श्रोत्रम् मनसः मनः यत् वाचः ह वाचम् सः उ प्राणस्य प्राणः चक्षुषः चक्षुः अतिमुच्य धीराः प्रेत्य अस्मात् लोकात् अमृताः भवन्ति
śrotrasya śrotram manasaḥ manaḥ yat vācaḥ ha vācam saḥ u prāṇasya prāṇaḥ cakṣuṣaḥ cakṣuḥ atimucya dhīrāḥ pretya asmāt lokāt amṛtāḥ bhavanti
Word by word
Since it is the ear of the ear, the mind of the mind, the speech of speech, he is also the prāṇa of prāṇa, the eye of the eye. (Knowing this,) the wise, giving up (the sense of self in the ear and the rest) and turning away from this world, become immortal.

Introduction To the fit pupil who asked thus the teacher said: hear what you ask, which god directs the mind and the other organs to their objects, and how.

Bhāṣya The ear is the organ that makes sound known; he about whom you ask is the ear of that. Is ‘the ear of the ear’ a fitting answer, where one expects ‘such a one directs the ear’? Yes, for he cannot be made known otherwise. If the director were known by a work of his own apart from the work of the ear and the rest, as one who wields a sickle, the answer would not fit. But he is known only through the work of the organs acting together, seeing, intending and determining, which ends in a result: there is one not combined with them, for whose purpose the group acts, as with a house; for what is combined exists for another. And the sense is not that the ear needs another ear, as one light another. The ear is seen to be able to make its object known; it has that power only when the light of the Self, consciousness, eternal, uncombined and inmost, is there, not otherwise (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.3.6; Muṇḍaka 2.2.10; Gītā 15.12, 13.33; Kaṭha 2.2.13). The common belief that the ear and the rest are the conscious self is set aside here.

Bhāṣya Manas is the inner organ, understanding and mind taken as one; without the ray of the light of consciousness it could not intend or determine. yat is ‘since’, and goes with each. The accusative vācam is to be turned into the nominative, as prāṇasya prāṇaḥ shows; the many forms are followed, and what is asked about is fitly named in the nominative. prāṇa is the function so called; its power to breathe is from him (Taittirīya 2.7.1; Kaṭha 2.2.3). Among the senses one expects smell, not prāṇa: the śruti holds smell to be included in prāṇa; what is meant is that Brahman is that for whose sake the whole group of organs acts.

Bhāṣya ‘having known’ is to be supplied, since the questioner wants to know and since immortality is named as the fruit: immortality is reached by knowledge. atimucya: one who takes the ear and the rest to be the self is born, dies and wanders as that; knowing that Brahman, the ear of the ear, is the Self, they give up that sense of self. dhīrāḥ: those with understanding, without which it cannot be given up. pretya asmāl lokāt: turning away from this world of dealings of ‘I’ and ‘mine’ in sons, friends, wife and kin, that is, having given up all desires (Taittirīya Nārāyaṇa 28; Kaṭha 2.1.1, 2.3.14). Or, since atimucya already gives the giving up of desires: having left this body, having died.

न तत्र चक्षुर्गच्छति न वाग्गच्छति नो मनः । न विद्मो न विजानीमो यथैतदनुशिष्यात् ॥ ३ ॥
na tatra cakṣur gacchati na vāg gacchati no manaḥ | na vidmo na vijānīmo yathaitad anuśiṣyāt || 3 ||
Padaccheda
न तत्र चक्षुः गच्छति न वाक् गच्छति नो मनः न विद्मः न विजानीमः यथा एतत् अनुशिष्यात्
na tatra cakṣuḥ gacchati na vāk gacchati no manaḥ na vidmaḥ na vijānīmaḥ yathā etat anuśiṣyāt
Word by word
The eye does not go there, nor speech, nor the mind. We do not know (it); we do not understand how one should teach this.

Bhāṣya Since Brahman is the very self of the ear and the rest, the eye does not go to it, for there is no going to oneself. Speech is said to go to a thing when a word, uttered, makes known what it names; Brahman is the self of that word and of the organ that produces it, so speech does not go there, as fire, which burns and lights, does not burn or light itself. The mind, which intends and determines other things, does not intend or determine itself; Brahman is its self too. A thing is known by the senses and the mind; being beyond their range, we do not know ‘Brahman is such’, and so do not understand how one should teach it to a pupil. What is within the range of the organs can be taught to another by its class, quality and action; Brahman has none of these. The mantra shows how great an effort is needed in teaching it and in grasping what is taught.

अन्यदेव तद्विदितादथो अविदितादधि । इति शुश्रुम पूर्वेषां ये नस्तद्व्याचचक्षिरे ॥ ४ ॥
anyad eva tad viditād atho aviditād adhi | iti śuśruma pūrveṣāṃ ye nas tad vyācacakṣire || 4 ||
Padaccheda
अन्यत् एव तत् विदितात् अथो अविदितात् अधि इति शुश्रुम पूर्वेषाम् ये नः तत् व्याचचक्षिरे
anyat eva tat viditāt atho aviditāt adhi iti śuśruma pūrveṣām ye naḥ tat vyācacakṣire
Word by word
It is other indeed than the known, and also above the unknown: thus we have heard from the ancients, who explained it to us.

Introduction ‘we do not understand how one should teach this’ might be taken to deny every way of teaching; this is the exception. True, another cannot be brought to know it by perception and the other means of knowledge; but he can by the received teaching, and that is now given.

Bhāṣya ‘that’ is what was called the ear of the ear and is beyond the range of the organs. The known is whatever is reached by the act of knowing as its object; everything manifest is known somewhere, to some one, in some measure; Brahman is other than that. Then is it unknown? It is other also than the unknown, the opposite of the known, the unmanifest, which is ignorance, the seed of the manifest. adhi means ‘above’, and by implication ‘other’. What is known is small, mortal and painful, so to be rejected: ‘other than the known’ says that Brahman is not to be rejected. ‘Above the unknown’ says that it is not to be taken up, for a cause is taken up by another for the sake of an effect, and the knower has nothing other than himself to take up for any purpose. By denying both what is rejected and what is taken up, the pupil's wish to know Brahman is brought to rest in what is not other than himself: nothing but one's own Self can be other than both the known and the unknown. So the meaning of the sentence is that the Self is Brahman (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.5, 3.4.1; Chāndogya 8.7.1).

Bhāṣya Iti śuśruma says that this meaning has come down through the succession of teachers. Brahman is to be known only so, not by reasoning, nor by exposition, intelligence, much learning, austerity or sacrifice.

यद्वाचानभ्युदितं येन वागभ्युद्यते । तदेव ब्रह्म त्वं विद्धि नेदं यदिदमुपासते ॥ ५ ॥
yad vācānabhyuditaṃ yena vāg abhyudyate | tad eva brahma tvaṃ viddhi nedaṃ yad idam upāsate || 5 ||
Padaccheda
यत् वाचा अनभ्युदितम् येन वाक् अभ्युद्यते तत् एव ब्रह्म त्वम् विद्धि न इदम् यत् इदम् उपासते
yat vācā anabhyuditam yena vāk abhyudyate tat eva brahma tvam viddhi na idam yat idam upāsate
Word by word
That which is not uttered by speech, by which speech is uttered: know that alone to be Brahman, not this that people worship.

Introduction When the Self has been taught as Brahman, the hearer doubts. The self is the one who wanders in birth and death, who has a claim to rites and meditation and by them wants to reach the gods or heaven; so another, the one worshipped, Viṣṇu, the Lord, Indra or prāṇa, should be Brahman, not the Self, for that goes against what all hold: the logicians say the self is other than the Lord, and the ritualists worship other deities. Seeing this doubt in the pupil's look or from his words, the teacher says: do not doubt so.

Bhāṣya ‘that’ is what has its being as consciousness alone. Speech is the organ, of the nature of fire, seated in eight places beginning with the root of the tongue, which makes the sounds manifest; and the sounds, fixed by the convention of a meaning, so many and in such an order; the word that they manifest is also called speech (‘the sound ‘a’ is all speech’, Aitareya Āraṇyaka 2.3.6). Not uttered: not made known by that speech. By which speech, with its organ, is uttered: is lit by the light of consciousness and put to use (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.4.7, 3.7.10, 4.3.26). Know that, the Self, to be Brahman, the unsurpassed, called the Vast (bhūman), Brahman because it is great. eva: set aside the adjuncts, speech and the rest, through which such ways of speaking as ‘the speech of speech’, ‘agent, enjoyer, knower, ruler’ are used of the supreme, in which there is no dealing and no distinction, and know the Self itself, without distinction, as Brahman. ‘Not this’: not what is marked by adjuncts and is not the Self, the Lord and the like, on which people meditate. Though ‘know that alone’ has been said, it is said again that what is not the Self is not Brahman, to restrict the sense, or to exclude the thought that anything else is Brahman.

यन्मनसा न मनुते येनाहुर्मनो मतम् । तदेव ब्रह्म त्वं विद्धि नेदं यदिदमुपासते ॥ ६ ॥
yan manasā na manute yenāhur mano matam | tad eva brahma tvaṃ viddhi nedaṃ yad idam upāsate || 6 ||
Padaccheda
यत् मनसा न मनुते येन आहुः मनः मतम् तत् एव ब्रह्म त्वम् विद्धि न इदम् यत् इदम् उपासते
yat manasā na manute yena āhuḥ manaḥ matam tat eva brahma tvam viddhi na idam yat idam upāsate
Word by word
That which one does not think with the mind, by which, they say, the mind is thought: know that alone to be Brahman, not this that people worship.

Bhāṣya Manas is the inner organ, understanding and mind as one; it is common to all the organs, since it reaches every object, and has desire and the rest for its modes (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.5.3). With that mind people do not intend or determine the light of consciousness that lights the mind, for as its illuminer it rules the mind; and being the inner self of every object, the inner organ does not act upon its own self. The mind has the power to think only when lit by the light of consciousness within. matam: the knowers of Brahman say that the mind with its modes is made an object, is pervaded, by Brahman.

यच्चक्षुषा न पश्यति येन चक्षूंषि पश्यति । तदेव ब्रह्म त्वं विद्धि नेदं यदिदमुपासते ॥ ७ ॥
yac cakṣuṣā na paśyati yena cakṣūṃṣi paśyati | tad eva brahma tvaṃ viddhi nedaṃ yad idam upāsate || 7 ||
Padaccheda
यत् चक्षुषा न पश्यति येन चक्षूंषि पश्यति तत् एव ब्रह्म त्वम् विद्धि न इदम् यत् इदम् उपासते
yat cakṣuṣā na paśyati yena cakṣūṃṣi paśyati tat eva brahma tvam viddhi na idam yat idam upāsate
Word by word
That which one does not see with the eye, by which one sees the eyes: know that alone to be Brahman, not this that people worship.

Bhāṣya With the eye joined to a mode of the inner organ people do not make it an object. ‘The eyes’ are the modes of the eye, which differ as the modes of the inner organ differ; one sees them, makes them objects and pervades them, by the light of the Self, consciousness.

यच्छ्रोत्रेण न शृणोति येन श्रोत्रमिदं श्रुतम् । तदेव ब्रह्म त्वं विद्धि नेदं यदिदमुपासते ॥ ८ ॥
yac chrotreṇa na śṛṇoti yena śrotram idaṃ śrutam | tad eva brahma tvaṃ viddhi nedaṃ yad idam upāsate || 8 ||
Padaccheda
यत् श्रोत्रेण न शृणोति येन श्रोत्रम् इदम् श्रुतम् तत् एव ब्रह्म त्वम् विद्धि न इदम् यत् इदम् उपासते
yat śrotreṇa na śṛṇoti yena śrotram idam śrutam tat eva brahma tvam viddhi na idam yat idam upāsate
Word by word
That which one does not hear with the ear, by which this ear is heard: know that alone to be Brahman, not this that people worship.

Bhāṣya The ear is presided over by the deity of the quarters, is a product of space, and works joined to a mode of the mind; with it people do not make that an object. ‘Heard’: this well-known ear is made an object by the light of the Self, consciousness.

यत्प्राणेन न प्राणिति येन प्राणः प्रणीयते । तदेव ब्रह्म त्वं विद्धि नेदं यदिदमुपासते ॥ ९ ॥
yat prāṇena na prāṇiti yena prāṇaḥ praṇīyate | tad eva brahma tvaṃ viddhi nedaṃ yad idam upāsate || 9 ||
Padaccheda
यत् प्राणेन न प्राणिति येन प्राणः प्रणीयते तत् एव ब्रह्म त्वम् विद्धि न इदम् यत् इदम् उपासते
yat prāṇena na prāṇiti yena prāṇaḥ praṇīyate tat eva brahma tvam viddhi na idam yat idam upāsate
Word by word
That which one does not smell with (the organ of) smell, by which smell is led (to its object): know that alone to be Brahman, not this that people worship.

Bhāṣya Prāṇa here is the organ of smell, of the nature of earth, seated within the nostrils, working together with the modes of the inner organ and of prāṇa; with it one does not make that an object as one does a thing that has odour. By it, as what is lit by the light of the Self, consciousness, the organ of smell is led to its object.

द्वितीयः खण्डः
Dvitīyaḥ khaṇḍaḥ · Second khaṇḍa
यदि मन्यसे सु वेदेति दभ्रमेवापि नूनं त्वं वेत्थ ब्रह्मणो रूपम् । यदस्य त्वं यदस्य देवेष्वथ नु मीमांस्यमेव ते मन्ये विदितम् ॥ १ ॥
yadi manyase su vedeti dabhram evāpi nūnaṃ tvaṃ vettha brahmaṇo rūpam | yad asya tvaṃ yad asya deveṣv atha nu mīmāṃsyam eva te manye viditam || 1 ||
Padaccheda
यदि मन्यसे सु वेद इति दभ्रम् एव अपि नूनम् त्वम् वेत्थ ब्रह्मणः रूपम् यत् अस्य त्वम् यत् अस्य देवेषु अथ नु मीमांस्यम् एव ते मन्ये विदितम्
yadi manyase su veda iti dabhram eva api nūnam tvam vettha brahmaṇaḥ rūpam yat asya tvam yat asya deveṣu atha nu mīmāṃsyam eva te manye viditam
Word by word
(The teacher:) If you think ‘I know it well’, surely you know only a little of the form of Brahman: what of it you (know in yourself) and what of it (you know) among the gods. So it is still to be reflected on by you. (The pupil:) I think it is known.

Introduction The pupil has been brought to understand ‘you, the Self, are Brahman, which is neither to be rejected nor taken up’. Lest he take this as ‘I know well that I am Brahman’, the teacher speaks to unsettle his understanding. Is not the settled understanding ‘I know it well’ what is wanted? A settled understanding is wanted, but not ‘I know it well’. What becomes an object can be known well, as what can be burnt can be burnt by fire; but fire cannot burn its own nature. The settled meaning of all the Vedānta is that Brahman is the own self of every knower; it has been taught here by ‘the ear of the ear’ (1.2), fixed in particular by 1.5, given as the conviction of the tradition in 1.4, and will be concluded in 2.3. The knower cannot be known by the knower, as fire cannot be burnt by fire; and there is no other knower for whom Brahman would be an object (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 3.8.11). So the understanding ‘I know Brahman well’ is false.

Bhāṣya The teacher says ‘if’ with doubt, because of a thing hard to know one man, whose faults are worn away and whose mind is good, understands what he has heard, and another does not. When told ‘the person seen in the eye is the Self’ (Chāndogya 8.7.4), Virocana, king of the asuras, a son of Prajāpati and learned, took it through the fault of his nature in the opposite sense, that the body is the Self; and Indra, king of the gods, not understanding though told once, twice and thrice, understood at the fourth turn, when the fault of his nature had worn away, the Brahman taught at first. In the world too, of those who hear from one teacher one understands rightly, one not rightly, one the opposite and one not at all; how much more with the truth of the Self, which is beyond the senses, and on which all the logicians, who hold to being and to non-being, disagree.

Bhāṣya Dabhram: little. Has Brahman many forms, great and small? Yes: many, made by the adjuncts of name and form, not of itself; of itself, forms are denied together with sound and the rest (Kaṭha 1.3.15). But is not that by which a thing is marked off its own form? Consciousness is not a property of earth and the other elements, of one or of all of them transformed, nor of the senses or the inner organ; so it is the form of Brahman, and Brahman is marked off by it (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 3.9.28, 2.4.12; Taittirīya 2.1; Aitareya 3.1.3). True; yet even so it is pointed to by words such as ‘knowledge’ only through the adjuncts of inner organ, body and senses, since it seems to follow them in the growth, shrinking, cutting and perishing of the body; not of itself. Of itself it is as 2.3 will settle. ‘What of it’ goes with ‘the form of Brahman’: not only the form of Brahman limited by the adjuncts in the body, but also the form limited by the divine adjuncts, which you know among the gods, you know as little; whatever is limited by an adjunct does not cease to be small. But Brahman with every adjunct and distinction destroyed, at peace, endless, one, non-dual, called the Vast, eternal, is not a thing well known. Since this is so, I think that Brahman is still to be inquired into by you.

Bhāṣya So addressed, the pupil sat down alone, collected; he pondered the meaning of the teaching as the teacher had given it, settled it by reasoning, made it his own experience, came back to the teacher and said: I think that Brahman is now known.

नाहं मन्ये सु वेदेति नो न वेदेति वेद च । यो नस्तद्वेद तद्वेद नो न वेदेति वेद च ॥ २ ॥
nāhaṃ manye su vedeti no na vedeti veda ca | yo nas tad veda tad veda no na vedeti veda ca || 2 ||
Padaccheda
न अहम् मन्ये सु वेद इति नो न वेद इति वेद च यः नः तत् वेद तत् वेद नो न वेद इति वेद च
na aham manye su veda iti no na veda iti veda ca yaḥ naḥ tat veda tat veda no na veda iti veda ca
Word by word
I do not think ‘I know it well’; nor (do I think) ‘I do not know’; and I know. He among us who knows that (saying) knows that (Brahman): ‘nor that I do not know; and I know’.

Bhāṣya How is it known? Hear. ‘I do not think that I know Brahman well.’ Then it is not known by you? ‘Nor that I do not know; and I know’; and by the word ‘and’, also ‘I do not know’. Is this not contradictory? If you do not think ‘I know well’, how do you think ‘I know’? That one thing is known by a man and not well known by the same man is a contradiction, unless there is doubt or error; and it cannot be laid down that Brahman is to be known as doubtful or wrongly, for doubt and error are known everywhere to do harm. Though the teacher tried in this way to unsettle him, the pupil was not unsettled, by the strength of the received teaching the teacher had given (1.4) and by the strength of reasoning and experience; and he roared, showing his own firm conviction in the knowledge of Brahman: whoever among us, fellow students, knows in truth that saying of mine knows Brahman. Which saying? ‘Nor that I do not know; and I know.’ It is the very thing said in 1.4, ‘other than the known and above the unknown’, joined with inference and experience and so made certain, which he said in another sentence, to show that his understanding agrees with the teacher's and to keep off the understanding of the dull.

यस्यामतं तस्य मतं मतं यस्य न वेद सः । अविज्ञातं विजानतां विज्ञातमविजानताम् ॥ ३ ॥
yasyāmataṃ tasya mataṃ mataṃ yasya na veda saḥ | avijñātaṃ vijānatāṃ vijñātam avijānatām || 3 ||
Padaccheda
यस्य अमतम् तस्य मतम् मतम् यस्य न वेद सः अविज्ञातम् विजानताम् विज्ञातम् अविजानताम्
yasya amatam tasya matam matam yasya na veda saḥ avijñātam vijānatām vijñātam avijānatām
Word by word
He by whom it is not known, by him it is known; he by whom it is known does not know. It is unknown to those who know, known to those who do not know.

Bhāṣya Turning from the talk of pupil and teacher, the śruti in its own person teaches what the whole talk has established. The knower of Brahman whose conviction is that Brahman is not known, by him Brahman is rightly known; he whose conviction is ‘Brahman is known by me’ does not know Brahman. The second half fixes the two positions, of the knower and of the one who does not know. ‘To those who know’: to those who have known rightly. ‘To those who do not know’: to those who do not see rightly, who see the Self in the senses, mind and understanding; not to those whose minds are wholly untaught, for they have no thought ‘Brahman is known by us’. Those who see the Self in these adjuncts do not discern Brahman from the adjunct, and the adjunct, the understanding and the rest, is known; so the error ‘Brahman is known’ can arise in them, and their wrong view is set down as the opposing side. Or the second half gives the reason for the first.

प्रतिबोधविदितं मतममृतत्वं हि विन्दते । आत्मना विन्दते वीर्यं विद्यया विन्दतेऽमृतम् ॥ ४ ॥
pratibodhaviditaṃ matam amṛtatvaṃ hi vindate | ātmanā vindate vīryaṃ vidyayā vindate 'mṛtam || 4 ||
Padaccheda
प्रतिबोध-विदितम् मतम् अमृतत्वम् हि विन्दते आत्मना विन्दते वीर्यम् विद्यया विन्दते अमृतम्
pratibodha-viditam matam amṛtatvam hi vindate ātmanā vindate vīryam vidyayā vindate amṛtam
Word by word
It is (rightly) known when it is known in each cognition, for (so) one gains immortality. By the Self one gains strength; by knowledge one gains immortality.

Introduction It has been settled that Brahman is unknown to those who know. If it were wholly unknown, the knowers of Brahman would be no different from worldly men; and ‘unknown to those who know’ contradicts itself. How then is Brahman rightly known?

Bhāṣya Pratibodha-viditam: known with regard to cognition after cognition. bodha means the cognitions of the understanding. The Self, to which all cognitions are objects, is awake to every cognition; the seer of all cognitions, of the nature of the power of consciousness alone, it is marked by the cognitions themselves, as undistinguished in them; there is no other door to the knowledge of the inner Self. When Brahman is known as the inner self of the cognitions, that is right seeing. And as the seer of all cognitions it is established as sight that neither arises nor passes away, eternal, pure, the Self, without distinction and one in all beings, since there is no difference of mark, as with space in a jar, a mountain or a cave. So the meaning of the received teaching, that Brahman is other than the known and the unknown, is brought to its conclusion purified (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 3.4.2).

Bhāṣya On other explanations: (1) If it is explained as ‘known by the mark of the act of knowing, as its agent’, as one knows wind as that which moves the branches, then the Self is a substance that has the power to know, not knowing itself; knowing arises and perishes; when it arises the Self is distinguished by it, and when it perishes the Self is bare substance: it would be changing, with parts, impermanent and impure, and these faults cannot be avoided. (2) For the followers of Kaṇāda, knowing is born of the contact of the self and the mind and inheres in the self, so that the self is a knower without changing, a bare substance like a jar in which colour inheres. Then Brahman is an unconscious substance, and the śrutis ‘Brahman is knowledge, bliss’, ‘consciousness is Brahman’ are set aside; as the self has no parts and so no places, and the mind is always joined to it, there can be no rule for the arising of memory; and that the Self has contact is against śruti and smṛti (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 3.9.26; Gītā 13.14) and against reason, for what has qualities joins what has qualities and is of its kind, not what is without quality and unlike everything. So only if the Self is the knower of every cognition is it established as Brahman, the light whose nature is knowledge never lost. (3) If the sense is taken as ‘known to itself’: where the Self has adjuncts one may speak of it as knowing itself by itself, a difference being imagined through the understanding (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.23; Gītā 10.15); but the Self without adjunct, being one, can be known neither by itself nor by another, and being awareness itself needs no other awareness, as light needs no other light. On the Buddhist view of self-awareness, cognition would be momentary and without a self, against the śrutis (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.3.30, 4.4.25; Muṇḍaka 1.1.6). (4) Some take pratibodha as a knowing without cause, as of one who wakes from sleep, others as a knowing once for all: with cause or without, once or more than once, it is still pratibodha.

Bhāṣya Because from that knowing one gains immortality, which is deathlessness, abiding in one's own Self, liberation, it is the right view. That the knowing has the inner Self for its object is the reason for immortality: the Self's being what is not the Self is not immortality; because it is the Self, its immortality has no cause, and its mortality is only the taking of it, through ignorance, as what is not the Self. How does one gain immortality by this knowledge? By the Self, by one's own nature, one gains strength. Strength made by wealth, allies, mantras, herbs, austerity or yoga cannot overcome death, being made by impermanent things; the strength made by the knowledge of the Self is gained by the Self alone, not by anything else, and so it can overcome death. Hence by knowledge, of the Self, one gains immortality (Muṇḍaka 3.2.4).

इह चेदवेदीदथ सत्यमस्ति न चेदिहावेदीन्महती विनष्टिः । भूतेषु भूतेषु विचित्य धीराः प्रेत्यास्माल्लोकादमृता भवन्ति ॥ ५ ॥
iha ced avedīd atha satyam asti na ced ihāvedīn mahatī vinaṣṭiḥ | bhūteṣu bhūteṣu vicitya dhīrāḥ pretyāsmāl lokād amṛtā bhavanti || 5 ||
Padaccheda
इह चेत् अवेदीत् अथ सत्यम् अस्ति न चेत् इह अवेदीत् महती विनष्टिः भूतेषु भूतेषु विचित्य धीराः प्रेत्य अस्मात् लोकात् अमृताः भवन्ति
iha cet avedīt atha satyam asti na cet iha avedīt mahatī vinaṣṭiḥ bhūteṣu bhūteṣu vicitya dhīrāḥ pretya asmāt lokāt amṛtāḥ bhavanti
Word by word
If one has known (it) here, then there is the true; if one has not known (it) here, great is the ruin. The wise, having discerned (the one Self) in being after being, turn away from this world and become immortal.

Bhāṣya Hard indeed, and from ignorance, is the meeting with birth, old age, death and disease among the hosts of living beings, gods, men, animals and ghosts, full of the pain of the round of birth and death. ‘Here’: if a man, who has the claim and the ability, has known the Self as described. Then ‘there is the true’: in this human birth there is imperishableness, or meaning, or real being, or the highest truth. If, living here and having the claim, he has not known, there is great, long, endless ruin: the course of birth and death, an unbroken chain of birth, old age and death. So, knowing the gain and the loss, the wise, having discerned, known and seen directly the one truth of the Self, Brahman, in all beings, moving and unmoving, turn away from this world, which is ignorance, marked by ‘I’ and ‘mine’, and having reached oneness with the Self of all, the non-dual, become immortal, that is, become Brahman itself (Muṇḍaka 3.2.9).

तृतीयः खण्डः
Tṛtīyaḥ khaṇḍaḥ · Third khaṇḍa
ब्रह्म ह देवेभ्यो विजिग्ये तस्य ह ब्रह्मणो विजये देवा अमहीयन्त । त ऐक्षन्तास्माकमेवायं विजयोऽस्माकमेवायं महिमेति ॥ १ ॥
brahma ha devebhyo vijigye tasya ha brahmaṇo vijaye devā amahīyanta | ta aikṣantāsmākam evāyaṃ vijayo 'smākam evāyaṃ mahimeti || 1 ||
Padaccheda
ब्रह्म ह देवेभ्यः विजिग्ये तस्य ह ब्रह्मणः विजये देवाः अमहीयन्त ते ऐक्षन्त अस्माकम् एव अयम् विजयः अस्माकम् एव अयम् महिमा इति
brahma ha devebhyaḥ vijigye tasya ha brahmaṇaḥ vijaye devāḥ amahīyanta te aikṣanta asmākam eva ayam vijayaḥ asmākam eva ayam mahimā iti
Word by word
Brahman, it is told, won a victory for the gods. In that victory of Brahman the gods attained greatness. They thought: ‘Ours alone is this victory, ours alone this greatness.’

Introduction Introducing the khaṇḍa: hearing ‘unknown to those who know, known to those who do not know’ (2.3), the dull may be misled: what exists is known by the means of knowledge, and what does not exist is unknown, like a hare's horn; so Brahman, being unknown, does not exist. The story is begun so that this may not be. For that Brahman is the ruler in every way, the supreme god even of the gods, the supreme lord even of lords, hard to know, the cause of the gods' victory and of the asuras' defeat: how should it not exist? The words that follow agree with this purpose. Or the story is to praise the knowledge of Brahman: by knowing Brahman Agni and the others became the foremost of the gods, and Indra even more. Or it shows that Brahman is hard to know, since even Agni and the others, of great splendour, came to know it only with trouble, and so Indra, though lord of the gods. Or the whole is for the sake of the meditations that are to be enjoined. Or it is to show that, apart from the knowledge of Brahman, the conceit of living beings that they are agents and enjoyers is false, as was the gods' conceit of victory.

Bhāṣya Brahman, the supreme, as described. In the battle of the gods and the asuras it conquered the asuras, the enemies of the world, who break the bounds set by the Lord, and gave the victory and its fruit to the gods, for the stability of the world. The gods, Agni and the others, attained greatness. Not knowing that the victory and the greatness belonged to the Lord, the inner Self seated in themselves, all-knowing, who joins living beings to the fruit of every act, all-powerful, who wished the world to stand, they thought: this victory was won by ourselves, limited as Agni and the rest; this greatness, the fruit of the victory, of being Agni, Vāyu and Indra, is ours, not the work of the Lord who is our inner Self.

तद्धैषां विजज्ञौ तेभ्यो ह प्रादुर्बभूव । तन्न व्यजानत किमिदं यक्षमिति ॥ २ ॥
tad dhaiṣāṃ vijajñau tebhyo ha prādurbabhūva | tan na vyajānata kim idaṃ yakṣam iti || 2 ||
Padaccheda
तत् ह एषाम् विजज्ञौ तेभ्यः ह प्रादुर्बभूव तत् न व्यजानत किम् इदम् यक्षम् इति
tat ha eṣām vijajñau tebhyaḥ ha prādurbabhūva tat na vyajānata kim idam yakṣam iti
Word by word
It knew this (false thought) of theirs and appeared to them. They did not know it: ‘What is this venerable being?’

Bhāṣya Brahman knew the false view of those who looked on things with this false conceit, for it sees all, being that which directs the organs of all beings. Lest the gods be overcome through false conceit, as the asuras were, out of compassion for them and to favour them by removing it, it appeared for their sake within the range of the gods' senses, in a most wonderful, astonishing form fashioned by the greatness of its own power. yakṣam: a great being worthy of worship.

तेऽग्निमब्रुवन्जातवेद एतद्विजानीहि किमेतद्यक्षमिति तथेति ॥ ३ ॥

They said to Agni: ‘Jātavedas, find out what this venerable being is.’ ‘So be it’, (he said).

Bhāṣya The gods, not knowing it, afraid within and wishing to know it, spoke to Agni, who goes before them, Jātavedas, almost all-knowing: you are the brilliant one among us; find out in particular what this being is that stands within our sight.

तदभ्यद्रवत्तमभ्यवदत्कोऽसीति । अग्निर्वा अहमस्मीत्यब्रवीज्जातवेदा वा अहमस्मीति ॥ ४ ॥
tad abhyadravat tam abhyavadat ko 'sīti | agnir vā aham asmīty abravīj jātavedā vā aham asmīti || 4 ||
Padaccheda
तत् अभ्यद्रवत् तम् अभ्यवदत् कः असि इति अग्निः वै अहम् अस्मि इति अब्रवीत् जातवेदाः वै अहम् अस्मि इति

He hastened to it. It said to him: ‘Who are you?’ ‘I am Agni’, he said; ‘I am Jātavedas.’

Bhāṣya Agni went to it wishing to ask, but stood silent near it, for want of boldness; the being spoke to him. Asked by Brahman, he answered ‘I am the well-known Agni, and Jātavedas’, praising himself as famed by two names.

तस्मिंस्त्वयि किं वीर्यमिति । अपीदं सर्वं दहेयं यदिदं पृथिव्यामिति ॥ ५ ॥

Padaccheda
तस्मिन् त्वयि किम् वीर्यम् इति अपि इदम् सर्वम् दहेयम् यत् इदम् पृथिव्याम् इति
tasmin tvayi kim vīryam iti api idam sarvam daheyam yat idam pṛthivyām iti
Word by word
‘What power is there in you, being such?’ ‘I could burn even all this, whatever there is on the earth.’

Bhāṣya In you who have such famed qualities and names. ‘I could burn to ashes all this world, the immovable and the rest.’ ‘On the earth’ stands for the rest too, since what is in the mid-region is also burnt by fire.

तस्मै तृणं निदधावेतद्दहेति । तदुपप्रेयाय सर्वजवेन तन्न शशाक दग्धुम् । स तत एव निववृते नैतदशकं विज्ञातुं यदेतद्यक्षमिति ॥ ६ ॥

It placed a blade of grass before him: ‘Burn this.’ He went up to it with all his speed; he could not burn it. He turned back from there: ‘I could not find out what this venerable being is.’

Bhāṣya Before him who had such conceit Brahman placed a blade of grass and said: burn this mere blade of grass in front of me; if you cannot burn it, give up everywhere the conceit that you are a burner. sarvajavena: with the speed of all his eagerness. Unable to burn the grass, ashamed, his boast broken, Jātavedas went back in silence from the being to the gods.

अथ वायुमब्रुवन्वायवेतद्विजानीहि किमेतद्यक्षमिति तथेति ॥ ७ ॥

In that very space he came upon a woman, greatly shining, Umā Haimavatī. He said to her: ‘What is this venerable being?’

Bhāṣya In the region of space where the being had shown itself and vanished, and where Indra was when it vanished, there he stayed, pondering what the being was; he did not turn back like Agni and Vāyu. Knowing his devotion to the being, Knowledge appeared in the form of Umā, as a woman. ‘Greatly shining’: of all that shines knowledge is the most beautiful, so the word fits. haimavatī: as one adorned with ornaments of gold (hema), that is, greatly shining. Or she is Umā herself, the daughter of Himavat; thinking that she, who is always with the all-knowing Lord, would be able to know, he approached her and asked what the being was that had shown itself and vanished.

चतुर्थः खण्डः

ब्रह्मेति होवाच ब्रह्मणो वा एतद्विजये महीयध्वमिति । ततो हैव विदांचकार ब्रह्मेति ॥ १ ॥

Padaccheda
ब्रह्म इति ह उवाच ब्रह्मणः वै एतत् विजये महीयध्वम् इति ततः ह एव विदांचकार ब्रह्म इति

‘It is Brahman’, she said. ‘It is in Brahman's victory that you attain greatness thus.’ From that alone he knew that it was Brahman.

Bhāṣya In the victory of the Lord alone: the asuras were conquered by the Lord, and you were only the occasion; in his victory you attain greatness. etat qualifies the verb. Your conceit, ‘ours alone is this victory, ours alone this greatness’, was false. tato ha eva: from Umā's words alone Indra knew it to be Brahman; the stress shows that it was not by himself.

तस्माद्वा एते देवा अतितरामिवान्यान्देवान्यदग्निर्वायुरिन्द्रः । ते ह्येनन्नेदिष्ठं पस्पर्शुस्ते ह्येनत्प्रथमो विदांचकार ब्रह्मेति ॥ २ ॥
tasmād vā ete devā atitarām ivānyān devān yad agnir vāyur indraḥ | te hy enan nediṣṭhaṃ pasparśus te hy enat prathamo vidāṃcakāra brahmeti || 2 ||
Padaccheda
तस्मात् वै एते देवाः अतितराम् इव अन्यान् देवान् यत् अग्निः वायुः इन्द्रः ते हि एनत् नेदिष्ठम् पस्पर्शुः ते हि एनत् प्रथमः विदांचकार ब्रह्म इति

Therefore these gods, Agni, Vāyu and Indra, surpass, as it were, the other gods; for they touched it nearest, and they were the first to know it as Brahman.

Bhāṣya Because these gods came near to Brahman, by speaking with it, seeing it and so on, they surpass the other gods in power, quality and great good fortune. iva has no sense here, or is for emphasis. nediṣṭham: nearest, dearest. prathamaḥ stands for the plural, ‘being the foremost’, and vidāṃcakāra for vidāṃcakruḥ.

तस्माद्वा इन्द्रोऽतितरामिवान्यान्देवान् । स ह्येनन्नेदिष्ठं पस्पर्श स ह्येनत्प्रथमो विदांचकार ब्रह्मेति ॥ ३ ॥

तस्मात् वै इन्द्रः अतितराम् इव अन्यान् देवान् सः हि एनत् नेदिष्ठम् पस्पर्श सः हि एनत् प्रथमः विदांचकार ब्रह्म इति
tasmāt vai indraḥ atitarām iva anyān devān saḥ hi enat nediṣṭham pasparśa saḥ hi enat prathamaḥ vidāṃcakāra brahma iti

Therefore Indra surpasses, as it were, the other gods; for he touched it nearest, and he was the first to know it as Brahman.

Bhāṣya Because even Agni and Vāyu came to know it only from Indra's words, and it was Indra who first heard from Umā's words that it was Brahman.

तस्यैष आदेशो यदेतद्विद्युतो व्यद्युतदा३इतीन्न्यमीमिषदा३इत्यधिदैवतम् ॥ ४ ॥
tasyaiṣa ādeśo yad etad vidyuto vyadyutad ā3 itīn nyamīmiṣad ā3 ity adhidaivatam || 4 ||
Padaccheda
तस्य एषः आदेशः यत् एतत् विद्युतः व्यद्युतत् आ इति इत् न्यमीमिषत् आ इति अधिदैवतम्
tasya eṣaḥ ādeśaḥ yat etat vidyutaḥ vyadyutat ā iti it nyamīmiṣat ā iti adhidaivatam
Word by word
This is the teaching of it (by a likeness): it is like this flashing of lightning; and it is like the winking (of the eye). This is with regard to the gods.

Bhāṣya Ādeśa is teaching by a likeness: the likeness by which Brahman, which has no like, is taught. ‘Of lightning … has flashed’ does not construe, so it is taken as ‘the flashing of lightning’. ā has the sense of likeness: like the flashing of lightning (so ‘like a single flash of lightning’, Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.3.6); for Brahman showed itself once to the gods, like lightning, and vanished. Or ‘the light’ is to be supplied: the light of lightning flashed once, as it were. iti points back to the teaching: ‘this is the teaching’. it means ‘and’: here is another teaching of it. nyamīmiṣat: winked, as the eye does; the causative has the sense of the simple verb, and ā again marks the likeness: like the eye's showing and hiding with regard to its object. adhidaivatam: this is the showing of a likeness for Brahman in what concerns the deities.

अथाध्यात्मं यदेतद्गच्छतीव च मनोऽनेन चैतदुपस्मरत्यभीक्ष्णं संकल्पः ॥ ५ ॥
athādhyātmaṃ yad etad gacchatīva ca mano 'nena caitad upasmaraty abhīkṣṇaṃ saṃkalpaḥ || 5 ||
Padaccheda
अथ अध्यात्मम् यत् एतत् गच्छति इव च मनः अनेन च एतत् उपस्मरति अभीक्ष्णम् संकल्पः
atha adhyātmam yat etat gacchati iva ca manaḥ anena ca etat upasmarati abhīkṣṇam saṃkalpaḥ
Word by word
Now with regard to the self: that the mind, as it were, goes to this (Brahman); that by it (the seeker) constantly remembers this; and (the mind's) intention (toward it).

Bhāṣya Next the teaching in what concerns the inner self. The mind, as it were, approaches this Brahman, makes it an object; by this mind the seeker remembers Brahman closely and intensely; and there is the mind's intention with Brahman for its object. Since Brahman has the mind for its adjunct, it is manifested by the cognitions of the mind, intention, memory and the like, as if it were being made an object. That is the teaching of Brahman with regard to the self. With regard to the gods it is of the nature of a swift showing, like lightning and winking; with regard to the self it is manifested together with the cognitions of the mind. Taught so, Brahman can be reached by those of slow understanding, who cannot grasp Brahman without any adjunct.

तद्ध तद्वनं नाम तद्वनमित्युपासितव्यम् । स य एतदेवं वेदाभि हैनं सर्वाणि भूतानि संवाञ्छन्ति ॥ ६ ॥
tad dha tadvanaṃ nāma tadvanam ity upāsitavyam | sa ya etad evaṃ vedābhi hainaṃ sarvāṇi bhūtāni saṃvāñchanti || 6 ||
Padaccheda
तत् ह तत्-वनम् नाम तत्-वनम् इति उपासितव्यम् सः यः एतत् एवम् वेद अभि ह एनम् सर्वाणि भूतानि संवाञ्छन्ति
tat ha tat-vanam nāma tat-vanam iti upāsitavyam saḥ yaḥ etat evam veda abhi ha enam sarvāṇi bhūtāni saṃvāñchanti
Word by word
That (Brahman) is called Tadvana, what is dear to all; it is to be meditated on as Tadvana. All beings long for him who knows it thus.

Bhāṣya Tadvana is tasya vanam: being the inner self of the whole host of living beings, it is what they are to desire and resort to. Since Brahman is known as Tadvana, it is to be meditated on by this very name, which states its quality. The fruit of meditating by this name: all beings pray to that meditator, as they do to Brahman.

उपनिषदं भो ब्रूहीत्युक्ता त उपनिषत् । ब्राह्मीं वाव त उपनिषदमब्रूमेति ॥ ७ ॥
upaniṣadaṃ bho brūhīty uktā ta upaniṣat | brāhmīṃ vāva ta upaniṣadam abrūmeti || 7 ||
Padaccheda
उपनिषदम् भोः ब्रूहि इति उक्ता ते उपनिषत् ब्राह्मीम् वाव ते उपनिषदम् अब्रूम इति
upaniṣadam bhoḥ brūhi iti uktā te upaniṣat brāhmīm vāva te upaniṣadam abrūma iti
Word by word
(The pupil:) ‘Sir, tell me the Upaniṣad.’ (The teacher:) ‘The Upaniṣad has been told to you; we have indeed told you the Upaniṣad concerning Brahman.’

Bhāṣya Upaniṣad: the secret that is to be reflected on. brāhmī: of Brahman, the supreme Self, for the knowledge taught above has the supreme Self for its subject. ‘We have told’ confirms that the Upaniṣad already given is the one concerning the supreme Self, with a view to what follows.

Bhāṣya On the pupil's question: what does one who has heard the Upaniṣad mean by asking for it? If he asks about what he has heard, the question is a useless repetition, like grinding what is ground. If the Upaniṣad had been told with something left over, it would not have been closed with the statement of its fruit (2.5); so the question is not about a remainder either. His meaning is this: does the Upaniṣad already told need some further means, as a part of it or as a help to it, or does it need nothing? If it needs something, tell me the Upaniṣad about that; if it needs nothing, confirm it, as Pippalāda did, ‘there is nothing beyond this’ (Praśna 6.7). So the teacher's confirmation, ‘the Upaniṣad has been told to you’, fits.

Bhāṣya On the same: it may be said that this is no confirmation, since he goes on to say more (4.8). Something more is said, but not as a part of the Upaniṣad or as a means that works with it; it is said as the way to attain the knowledge of Brahman, for austerity and the rest are put on a level with the Vedas and their limbs by being named together with them, and the Vedas and their limbs cannot be parts of the knowledge of Brahman or work together with it. May not things named together be applied apart, as each fits, as the mantras of the sūktavāka are divided according to their deities: austerity, restraint, rites and truth as parts of the knowledge or helps to it, the Vedas and their limbs, which make meanings known, as means to rites and to the knowledge of the Self? No: the division does not hold. The knowledge of Brahman, which sets aside every notion of the difference of act, means and fruit, cannot need a part or be joined to a helping means; it and its fruit, the highest good, rest in the inner Self, turned away from every object. So action cannot work together with knowledge, nor knowledge need action as a part. The question and answer are therefore for confirmation: this much is the Upaniṣad, and it needs nothing else for immortality.

तस्यै तपो दमः कर्मेति प्रतिष्ठा वेदाः सर्वाङ्गानि सत्यमायतनम् ॥ ८ ॥
tasyai tapo damaḥ karmeti pratiṣṭhā vedāḥ sarvāṅgāni satyam āyatanam || 8 ||
Padaccheda
तस्यै तपः दमः कर्म इति प्रतिष्ठा वेदाः सर्व-अङ्गानि सत्यम् आयतनम्
tasyai tapaḥ damaḥ karma iti pratiṣṭhā vedāḥ sarva-aṅgāni satyam āyatanam
Word by word
Austerity, restraint, rites (and the like) are its footing, (and so are) the Vedas and all their limbs; truth is its abode.

Bhāṣya Tasyai: of the Upaniṣad concerning Brahman just spoken of; austerity and the rest are the means of attaining it. tapas: the collecting of body, senses and mind. dama: quieting. karma: the agnihotra and other rites. Knowledge of the truth is seen to arise, through purity of mind, in one refined by these; and one whose stain is not rubbed away is seen not to understand, or to understand wrongly, even when Brahman is taught, as Indra and Virocana. So knowledge arises, as it is taught, in one whose mind has been purified by austerity and the rest, here or in many past births (Śvetāśvatara 6.23; Mahābhārata, Śāntiparvan 204.8). iti shows that these stand for others too that help knowledge to arise, such as humility and freedom from pretence (Gītā 13.7). pratiṣṭhā: its two feet, as it were; when these are there the knowledge of Brahman stands firm and goes forward, as a man on his feet.

Bhāṣya The four Vedas and all the six limbs, phonetics and the rest, are also its footing, the Vedas because they make rites and knowledge known, the limbs because they guard the Vedas. Or, since pratiṣṭhā pictures the feet, the Vedas are all its other limbs, the head and the rest; on this view phonetics and the rest are included in the word ‘Vedas’. satyam: freedom from deceit, straightness of speech, mind and body. āyatanam: where the Upaniṣad dwells; knowledge rests in those who are without deceit and good, not in the deceitful, whose nature is demonic (Praśna 1.16). Truth is already included in the footing with austerity and the rest; it is named again as the abode to show that it is a means above the others (‘a thousand horse sacrifices and truth were weighed in the balance; truth alone outweighs the thousand’).

यो वा एतामेवं वेदापहत्य पाप्मानमनन्ते स्वर्गे लोके ज्येये प्रतितिष्ठति प्रतितिष्ठति ॥ ९ ॥
yo vā etām evaṃ vedāpahatya pāpmānam anante svarge loke jyeye pratitiṣṭhati pratitiṣṭhati || 9 ||
Padaccheda
यः वै एताम् एवम् वेद अपहत्य पाप्मानम् अनन्ते स्वर्गे लोके ज्येये प्रतितिष्ठति प्रतितिष्ठति
yaḥ vai etām evam veda apahatya pāpmānam anante svarge loke jyeye pratitiṣṭhati pratitiṣṭhati
Word by word
He who knows this (knowledge) thus, having struck off evil, is established in the endless, the highest, world of bliss (Brahman); he is established.

Bhāṣya This knowledge of Brahman, taught from keneṣitam onward and praised by the story beginning ‘Brahman won a victory for the gods’, the ground of all knowledge. The fruit of the knowledge, though stated at 2.4, is summed up at the end. pāpman: the seed of the round of birth and death, which is ignorance, desire and action. svarge loke: in Brahman, whose nature is happiness. Because it is called endless it is not heaven; and lest ‘endless’ be taken there in a secondary sense, the mantra adds jyeye: in what is greater than all, his own Self, in the primary sense. He is established there: he does not come again to the round of birth and death.

oṃ āpyāyantu mamāṅgāni vākprāṇaścakṣuḥ śrotramatho balamindriyāṇi ca sarvāṇi | sarvaṃ brahmaupaniṣadaṃ māhaṃ brahma nirākuryāṃ mā mā brahma nirākarodanirākaraṇamastvanirākaraṇaṃ me'stu | tadātmani nirate ya upaniṣatsu dharmāste mayi santu te mayi santu || oṃ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ ||
Padaccheda
ॐ आप्यायन्तु मम अङ्गानि वाक् प्राणः चक्षुः श्रोत्रम् अथो बलम् इन्द्रियाणि च सर्वाणि सर्वम् ब्रह्म औपनिषदम् मा अहम् ब्रह्म निराकुर्याम् मा मा ब्रह्म निराकरोत् अनिराकरणम् अस्तु अनिराकरणम् मे अस्तु तत्-आत्मनि निरते ये उपनिषत्सु धर्माः ते मयि सन्तु ते मयि सन्तु ॐ शान्तिः शान्तिः शान्तिः
oṃ āpyāyantu mama aṅgāni vāk prāṇaḥ cakṣuḥ śrotram atho balam indriyāṇi ca sarvāṇi sarvam brahma aupaniṣadam mā aham brahma nirākuryām mā mā brahma nirākarot anirākaraṇam astu anirākaraṇam me astu tat-ātmani nirate ye upaniṣatsu dharmāḥ te mayi santu te mayi santu oṃ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ
Word by word
Om. May my limbs grow strong: speech, prāṇa, eye, ear, strength, and all the senses. All is the Brahman of the Upaniṣads. May I not reject Brahman; may Brahman not reject me. Let there be no rejecting; let there be no rejecting on my part. May the dharmas taught in the Upaniṣads be in me, who delight in that Self; may they be in me. Om. Peace, peace, peace.

Note The peace invocation of the Upaniṣads of the Sāmaveda, printed in a frame before the Kenopaniṣad in the 1910 edition (vol. 4, scan leaf 50) and again before and after the vākya-bhāṣya. Śaṅkara's bhāṣya does not comment on it; the translation gives the plain sense of the words.

Sanskrit text: The Works of Sri Sankaracharya, vol. 4 (Upaniṣad-bhāṣya, vol. 1), Sri Vani Vilas Press, Srirangam, 1910, where the mantras are printed in the Kenopaniṣad-pada-bhāṣya (pp. 33–82) and again in the Kenopaniṣad-vākya-bhāṣya (pp. 83–121). The edition is in the public domain; Vedic Space typed the mantras from the Internet Archive scan (The Free Indological Collection). The print is worn: most anusvāras and many visargas are missing (the first mantra prints केनेषित पतति प्रेषित मन). These were restored by Claude from the sense while typing, without collation against another edition; the edition's readings and its division into 35 mantras are otherwise kept.

Commentary followed: Śaṅkara's Kenopaniṣad-pada-bhāṣya (printed in the same volume of the 1910 edition, pp. 33–82), read in Advaita Śāradā (advaitasharada.sringeri.net), the e-text of the Dakṣiṇāmnāya Śrī Śāradā Pīṭham, Śṛṅgerī. The English is Vedic Space's own.
