सनत्सुजातीयम्

Sanatsujātīyam

Sanatsujātīya

Mahābhārata, Udyogaparvan · meanings following Śaṅkara's bhāṣya

The teaching of the sage Sanatsujāta to King Dhṛtarāṣṭra on the eve of the war (Mahābhārata, Udyogaparvan, in the vulgate text Śaṅkara comments on): death and deathlessness, the faults and virtues of the seeker, brahmacarya, and Brahman seen by yogins. Meanings and translation follow Śaṅkara's bhāṣya.

SourceSrirangam edition, 1910
प्रथमोऽध्यायः
Prathamo 'dhyāyaḥ · Adhyāya 1
वैशम्पायन उवाचvaiśampāyana uvāca
ततो राजा धृतराष्ट्रो मनीषी संपूज्य वाक्यं विदुरेरितं तत् । सनत्सुजातं रहिते महात्मा पप्रच्छ बुद्धिं परमां बुभूषन् ॥ १ ॥
tato rājā dhṛtarāṣṭro manīṣī saṃpūjya vākyaṃ vidureritaṃ tat | sanatsujātaṃ rahite mahātmā papraccha buddhiṃ paramāṃ bubhūṣan || 1 ||
Padaccheda
ततः राजा धृतराष्ट्रः मनीषी संपूज्य वाक्यम् विदुर-ईरितम् तत् सनत्सुजातम् रहिते महात्मा पप्रच्छ बुद्धिम् परमाम् बुभूषन्
tataḥ rājā dhṛtarāṣṭraḥ manīṣī saṃpūjya vākyam vidura-īritam tat sanatsujātam rahite mahātmā papraccha buddhim paramām bubhūṣan
Word by word
Then King Dhṛtarāṣṭra, wise and great-minded, honouring those words spoken by Vidura, asked Sanatsujāta, in a secluded place, about the highest understanding, wishing to become (his own Self again).

Introduction Introducing the text: after a verse of homage to Viṣṇu, the ancient Puruṣa, full bliss, cause of the universe, and homage to the teachers who handed down the knowledge of Brahman, he begins a short explanation of the Sanatsujātīya, for the easy understanding of those who wish to know Brahman. The Self, though by nature Brahman — consciousness, being, bliss, without a second — through its own ignorance, which rests on it, has it for its object, is known in its own experience and carries a reflection (of consciousness), falls from that natural being as Brahman and takes the not-self, the body and the rest, as itself. Every human end then escapes it and every evil comes to it; it seeks the wanted and avoids the unwanted by means that ignorance and action have imagined, and even by worldly and Vedic means duly performed it does not gain the highest end, called liberation. Dragged here and there by passion, aversion and the like, as by sea-monsters, it turns about in many births, among gods, men, animals and the rest, deluded, in saṃsāra. Then somehow, by merit, through action done for the Lord as the Veda enjoins, the stain of passion and the rest goes; from seeing the faults of impermanence and the like, dispassion arises for enjoying fruits here and hereafter; wishing to know the oneness with Brahman that the Vedānta teaches, and having calm, self-restraint and the other means the Veda names, he approaches a teacher who knows Brahman, and by hearing the Vedānta and the rest, as the teacher directs, he knows the truth of the Self as Brahman, ‘I am Brahman’; ignorance and its effects ended, he abides as Brahman. ‘This is the bound of the Vedānta’; the blessed Sanatsujāta will show all of it in order.

Bhāṣya On the setting: Dhṛtarāṣṭra, burning with grief and delusion, had heard the Vedānta's word ‘the knower of the Self crosses over grief’ (Chāndogya 7.1.3) and, thinking grief cannot be removed without the knowledge of Brahman, asked Vidura to tell him anything he had not yet told. Vidura, though he had heard the teaching, all-knowing and most compassionate, held that the knowledge of Brahman belongs to those specially qualified, and said ‘Born of a śūdra womb, I do not venture to say more’, that is, he was not entitled to the Upaniṣadic knowledge of the Self as Brahman. Wishing all the same to establish the king, by that knowledge, in the supreme state, the Supreme Self, full bliss, his own kingdom, he remembered the story known from the Chāndogya Upaniṣad (Sanatkumāra's teaching of the bhūman), thought that no one but Sanatsujāta could show the king the Supreme Self beyond darkness, called him by the power of yoga, honoured him by rising to meet him and the like, and asked him to resolve the doubt in Dhṛtarāṣṭra's mind, so that, hearing him, the king would pass beyond all sorrow, bear gain and loss, the loved and the hated, old age and death, intoxication, hunger and thirst, fear, discontent, sloth, desire and anger, decline and rise, and be freed from dharma and adharma, the causes of saṃsāra, liberated, beyond pleasure and pain.

Bhāṣya Tat vākyam: the words Vidura spoke to Sanatsujāta just before. Sanatsujāta: ‘sanat’ is the eternal Brahman called Hiraṇyagarbha; born well (sujāta), with knowledge, dispassion and the rest, from that eternal Brahman's mind, he is the blessed Sanatkumāra. rahite: in a secluded place, free of common people. mahātmā: of great understanding. buddhiṃ paramām: the highest understanding, whose object is the full bliss without a second. bubhūṣan: wishing to become, that is, wishing to gain, by the knowledge of Brahman, the Self that had been taken from him.

धृतराष्ट्र उवाचdhṛtarāṣṭra uvāca
सनत्सुजात यदिदं शृणोमि मृत्युर्हि नास्तीति तवोपदेशम् । देवासुरा आचरन्ब्रह्मचर्यममृत्यवे तत्कतरन्नु सत्यम् ॥ २ ॥
sanatsujāta yad idaṃ śṛṇomi mṛtyur hi nāstīti tavopadeśam | devāsurā ācaran brahmacaryam amṛtyave tat kataran nu satyam || 2 ||
Padaccheda
सनत्सुजात यत् इदम् शृणोमि मृत्युः हि न अस्ति इति तव उपदेशम् देव-असुराः आचरन् ब्रह्मचर्यम् अमृत्यवे तत् कतरत् नु सत्यम्
sanatsujāta yat idam śṛṇomi mṛtyuḥ hi na asti iti tava upadeśam deva-asurāḥ ācaran brahmacaryam amṛtyave tat katarat nu satyam
Word by word
O Sanatsujāta, I hear this teaching of yours, that there is no death; (yet) the gods and asuras lived the life of a brahmacārin for deathlessness. Of these, which then is true?

Bhāṣya Vidura says that you taught your disciples ‘there is no death’; yet the gods and asuras, Indra, Virocana and the rest, lived the life of a brahmacārin, dwelling with the teacher, for deathlessness, to gain deathlessness, as the Chāndogya tells: ‘both the gods and the asuras came to know of it’, ‘the two lived as brahmacārins for thirty-two years’ (8.7.2–3), and ‘Maghavan lived with Prajāpati as a brahmacārin for a hundred and one years’ (8.11.3). If your view is that there is no death, why did the gods and asuras live so for deathlessness? Of the two views, that death is and that it is not, tell which is true.

श्रीसनत्सुजात उवाचśrīsanatsujāta uvāca
अमृत्युः कर्मणा केचिन्मृत्युर्नास्तीति चापरे । शृणु मे ब्रुवतो राजन्यथैतन्मा विशङ्किथाः ॥ ३ ॥
amṛtyuḥ karmaṇā kecin mṛtyur nāstīti cāpare | śṛṇu me bruvato rājan yathaitan mā viśaṅkithāḥ || 3 ||
Padaccheda
अमृत्युः कर्मणा केचित् मृत्युः न अस्ति इति च अपरे शृणु मे ब्रुवतः राजन् यथा एतत् मा विशङ्किथाः
amṛtyuḥ karmaṇā kecit mṛtyuḥ na asti iti ca apare śṛṇu me bruvataḥ rājan yathā etat mā viśaṅkithāḥ
Word by word
Some (say) deathlessness (comes) by action; others, that there is no death. Hear me, O king, as I tell how this (stands); do not doubt (what I say).

Bhāṣya Some, set in ignorance and holding death to be real, think deathlessness comes by the action the Veda enjoins, and perform it for deathlessness. Others, blinded by the poison of sense-objects and not admitting a liberation without objects, describe deathlessness as reaching the state of a god and the like by action alone, and quote a passionate man's verse: ‘Let him rather wish to be a jackal in the empty Vṛndāvana, but never an objectless liberation, Gautama.’ Others again, seeing a second beside the Supreme Self, describe deathlessness as coming from knowledge and action together. Others still, who see the Self without a second, see nothing second to it and say ‘there is no death’. Hear from me, O king, how the two views do not conflict; mā viśaṅkithāḥ: have no doubt about what I say.

उभे सत्ये क्षत्रियाद्यप्रवृत्ते मोहो मृत्युः संमतो यः कवीनाम् । प्रमादं वै मृत्युमहं ब्रवीमि सदाप्रमादममृतत्वं ब्रवीमि ॥ ४ ॥
ubhe satye kṣatriyādyapravṛtte moho mṛtyuḥ saṃmato yaḥ kavīnām | pramādaṃ vai mṛtyum ahaṃ bravīmi sadāpramādam amṛtatvaṃ bravīmi || 4 ||
Padaccheda
उभे सत्ये क्षत्रिय आद्य-प्रवृत्ते मोहः मृत्युः संमतः यः कवीनाम् प्रमादम् वै मृत्युम् अहम् ब्रवीमि सदा-अप्रमादम् अमृतत्वम् ब्रवीमि
ubhe satye kṣatriya ādya-pravṛtte mohaḥ mṛtyuḥ saṃmataḥ yaḥ kavīnām pramādam vai mṛtyum aham bravīmi sadā-apramādam amṛtatvam bravīmi
Word by word
Both are true, O warrior, current from the first (creation). Delusion is held to be death by (some) sages; but I call heedlessness death, and constant heedfulness I call deathlessness.

Note The edition prints kṣatriyādyapravṛtte as one word; Śaṅkara divides it as kṣatriya (vocative) and ādyapravṛtte, or, second, as kṣatriyādya (vocative, ‘first of warriors’) and pravṛtte (‘current’).

Bhāṣya Ubhe: both, the being and the not-being of death spoken of before; ādyapravṛtte: current since the first creation. How can two contradictory things both be true? There would be a contradiction if death were something real. What then is death? Delusion, false knowledge, the taking of the not-self as self, is held to be death by some sages; I do not call death that. I call heedlessness death: pramāda is the falling away from one's natural being as Brahman; that heedlessness, the failure to grasp the Self, ignorance of the Self, the cause even of false knowledge, the seed of every evil, birth, death and the rest, I call death. Constant heedfulness, abiding in one's own nature, I call deathlessness. So the śruti shows liberation to be abiding in one's own nature: ‘having reached the highest light, he appears in his own form’ (Chāndogya 8.3.4); and the Anugītā says it plainly: there is one sacrifice, no second, the one that lies in the heart, in whom mortals, having swallowed all this, stand in their own nature.

Bhāṣya Since liberation is abiding in one's own nature, and so unlike the four kinds of result an act can have, deathlessness is not produced by action, nor by knowledge and action together; the view ‘some (say) deathlessness (comes) by action’ (verse 3) is therefore untenable. Sanatsujāta himself refutes it below, in verse 9 (Śaṅkara quotes it with a second half: by knowledge the knower goes to the eternal light; for him there is no other path).

प्रमादाद्वा असुराः पराभवन्नप्रमादाद्ब्रह्मभूताः सुराश्च । न वै मृत्युर्व्याघ्र इवात्ति जन्तून्नाप्यस्य रूपमुपलभ्यते हि ॥ ५ ॥
pramādād vā asurāḥ parābhavann apramādād brahmabhūtāḥ surāś ca | na vai mṛtyur vyāghra ivātti jantūn nāpy asya rūpam upalabhyate hi || 5 ||
Padaccheda
प्रमादात् वै असुराः पराभवन् अप्रमादात् ब्रह्म-भूताः सुराः च न वै मृत्युः व्याघ्रः इव अत्ति जन्तून् न अपि अस्य रूपम् उपलभ्यते हि
pramādāt vai asurāḥ parābhavan apramādāt brahma-bhūtāḥ surāḥ ca na vai mṛtyuḥ vyāghraḥ iva atti jantūn na api asya rūpam upalabhyate hi
Word by word
Through heedlessness the asuras were defeated, and through heedfulness the gods became Brahman. Death does not eat creatures like a tiger; for its form is not even perceived.

Introduction How is it known that heedlessness is death and heedfulness deathlessness?

Bhāṣya Through heedlessness, the falling away from one's natural being as Brahman and taking the not-self, the body and the rest, as self, the asuras, Virocana and the others, were defeated, as the Chāndogya tells from ‘not having found the Self’ to ‘they will be defeated’ (8.8.4). Through heedfulness, abiding as Brahman, consciousness, being, bliss without a second, the gods, Indra and the rest, became Brahman: ‘the gods worship that Self; therefore all worlds and all desires are theirs’ (Chāndogya 8.12.6). Or: asuras are those who delight in the asus, the breaths and the senses: creatures who do not know the Self and live for objects. Going beyond their natural being as Brahman and taking the body as self, they were defeated, falling into animal and other births, as the Aitareya Āraṇyaka says from ‘therefore let one not be heedless’ to ‘those three peoples that went beyond became these birds …’ (2.1.1). And suras are those who delight in their own Self, the knowers of the Self: ‘those whose delight is in the Self, in their own unmoving Brahman, are called suras; the wise (sūri) are held to be suras.’ Through heedfulness, abiding as Brahman, false knowledge and its effects ended, they became Brahman itself.

Bhāṣya On the second half: the objection is that another death, the destroyer of all creatures, is well known; how then can heedlessness be called death? He answers: death does not eat creatures like a tiger; if it did, its form would be perceived like a tiger's; it is not perceived; so there is no (such) death.

यमं त्वेके मृत्युमतोऽन्यमाहुरात्मावासममृतं ब्रह्मचर्यम् । पितृलोके राज्यमनुशास्ति देवः शिवः शिवानामशिवोऽशिवानाम् ॥ ६ ॥
yamaṃ tv eke mṛtyum ato 'nyam āhur ātmāvāsam amṛtaṃ brahmacaryam | pitṛloke rājyam anuśāsti devaḥ śivaḥ śivānām aśivo 'śivānām || 6 ||
Padaccheda
यमम् तु एके मृत्युम् अतः अन्यम् आहुः आत्म-आवासम् अमृतम् ब्रह्मचर्यम् पितृ-लोके राज्यम् अनुशास्ति देवः शिवः शिवानाम् अशिवः अशिवानाम्
yamam tu eke mṛtyum ataḥ anyam āhuḥ ātma-āvāsam amṛtam brahmacaryam pitṛ-loke rājyam anuśāsti devaḥ śivaḥ śivānām aśivaḥ aśivānām
Word by word
But some call Yama death, other than this (heedlessness): dwelling in the self (the intellect), deathless, established in Brahman. The god rules a kingdom in the world of the fathers, kind to the good, unkind to the wicked.

Introduction But Yama's form is perceived, in the story of Sāvitrī: ‘Then from Satyavān's body Yama drew out by force the thumb-sized person, bound by the noose, in his power.’ How then is it said that death's form is not perceived?

Bhāṣya It is true that (Yama's form) is perceived; yet he is not death directly. Death is the ignorance called heedlessness, for it is the direct cause of destruction: ‘if one has not known here, great is the loss’ (Kena 2.5); and the Bṛhadāraṇyaka shows that the ignorance called heedlessness is death itself: ‘death is darkness, light is the deathless’ (1.3.28). Since heedlessness is the direct seed of every evil, let one not be heedless: let one abide as Brahman, consciousness, being, bliss without a second (Gītā 5.15: knowledge is covered by ignorance, by that creatures are deluded). Then ‘by the way’ he sets out the meaning of the whole śāstra in brief, as outlined in the next six notes.

Bhāṣya Śaṅkara (1), knowledge alone liberates: since heedlessness is death and heedfulness deathlessness, deathlessness is neither produced nor reached by action, being ever accomplished and ever attained (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.23: this eternal greatness of the brāhmaṇa neither grows nor lessens by action). Knowledge alone is the means: ‘knowing him alone one goes beyond death; there is no other path’ (Śvetāśvatara 3.8), ‘knowing him alone, let the wise brāhmaṇa make wisdom’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.21), ‘not by the eye … not by austerity or action; by the clearness of knowledge, pure in mind, he sees him’ (Muṇḍaka 3.1.8). Sanatsujāta says the same below (the worlds won by action end; ‘knowing death thus as it arises’, verse 16); so the Mokṣadharma (a creature is bound by action, freed by knowledge; so the renouncers who see the far shore do no action; knowledge, not sacrifice, crosses the hard way); Manu (12.92: leaving even the prescribed actions, the best of the twice-born should strive in Self-knowledge, calm and the study of the Veda); and the Supreme Lord (knowledge alone gives liberation; the one Self, all-pervading, pure consciousness, bliss, is to be known — ‘this is the Sāṃkhya view’; the renouncers devoted to it reach me).

Bhāṣya Śaṅkara (2), actions are not for the knower: are actions then not to be done? Not so; they are not to be done by the knower: ‘for him who delights in the Self alone … there is nothing to be done’ (Gītā 3.17); the Kāvaṣeyas in the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa (what is study to us, why should we sacrifice; we offer prāṇa in speech and speech in prāṇa); the Aitareya (‘for what shall we study?’); the Bṛhadāraṇyaka, which shows the knower's renunciation of action (4.4.22: the knowers of old, rising above the desire for sons, for wealth and for the worlds, lived as mendicants); the Liṅga Purāṇa (for the yogin content with the nectar of knowledge nothing remains to be done; if anything does, he is not a knower of the truth); an Ātharvaṇa śruti. By whom then? By the ignorant who wish to ascend, all actions always; not by the knower (Gītā 3.3, 6.3; Vyāsa: there are two paths on which the Vedas rest, dharma marked by activity and dharma that stands in withdrawal).

Bhāṣya Śaṅkara (3), actions purify, they do not liberate: if action binds, should even those who wish to ascend not act? True, it binds; yet done for the Lord, without regard to the fruit, it does not (Gītā 3.9); it is done for the purity of the mind (Gītā 5.11, 18.5, 4.23; ‘actions ripen the impurities; knowledge is the highest goal’). Objection: the śruti makes action a cause of liberation too (Īśa 11 and 2; Manu: ‘austerity and knowledge both bring the brāhmaṇa the highest good’). No: that error comes from not reading what goes before and after. Īśa 11 goes on: ‘by ignorance crossing death, by knowledge one gains the deathless’, so the two have different objects and are not combined; Manu himself explains: ‘by austerity he destroys sin, by knowledge gains the deathless’, so austerity, the obligatory and occasional rites, serves only the purity of the inner organ. Īśa 1 says all this is (the Lord) and enjoins protecting oneself by renunciation for one who sees it; for one who does not know that Self, Īśa 2 says ‘doing works here, let one wish to live a hundred years’: works lifelong, for the purity of the ignorant man who thinks himself a mere man. So these texts too teach purification, not liberation; and the ‘and’ of ‘by it goes the knower of Brahman, the doer of merit and the luminous’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.9) is to be construed by the established use of the śruti. The Anugītā says plainly that actions lead to liberation only through purity: by obligatory and occasional rites, pure and without attachment to fruit, one gains purity of mind, ascends to yoga, and reaches the highest place of Viṣṇu; spotless knowledge arises from devotion to the teacher, firmness, devotion to dharma, learning and devotion to Viṣṇu. Sanatsujāta too says it (adhyāya 2: by austerity and sacrifice the knower gains merit; by merit destroying sin he is born lit by knowledge; by knowledge he reaches the Self).

Bhāṣya Śaṅkara (4), why purity is needed: liberation comes by knowledge alone, even without purity? True, liberation is by knowledge alone; but that knowledge does not arise without purity of mind: ‘knowledge arises in men from the wearing away of sinful action’; while the heap of sin gathered in many births is not worn away, the mind does not turn to Govinda; devotion to Kṛṣṇa arises in those whose sins are worn away by austerity, meditation and absorption over thousands of births; Gītā 6.45, 6.46, 18.45–46; Yājñavalkya: one whose instrument is unripe is not fit for Self-knowledge. Since knowledge, the means of liberation, comes only to the pure in mind, through discerning the eternal from the transient, one should do all actions, of speech, mind and body, enjoined by śruti and smṛti, for the Lord of all, until, pure in mind and without desire for enjoyment here or hereafter, one has ascended to yoga (Gītā 6.3, 5.6, 6.4); for him who has so ascended, calm alone is the means, not action.

Bhāṣya Śaṅkara (5), the practice: so, with calm, self-restraint and the other means, with hearing and the rest, ‘the yogin should constantly join himself, alone, in a secluded place’ (Gītā 6.10). How? On a level, clean place pleasing to the mind, free of gravel, fire, sand, noise, water and the like, setting a firm seat neither too high nor too low, of cloth, skin and kuśa grass, seated in the svastika or the lotus posture, holding body, head and neck straight, withdrawing Viśva and the rest in order into the full Self free of effect and cause, abiding as the full Self, he should meditate, as Om, on the deity dwelling in the body, full bliss, stainless, Brahman without before or after, marked by ‘not this, not this’, untouched by hunger and the rest, abiding as knowledge that neither rises nor sets: the Supreme Self (the Kāvaṣeya-gītā: strive for liberation; take to Om, Brahman, the void, the highest, the chief, pure bliss, the praṇava). When, so joining himself, he knows himself directly as the Supreme Self, ignorance and its effects are cast off, he is free of sorrow, his work done (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.12; Īśa 7; Kaṭha 1.2.12, 1.3.15; Taittirīya 2.9; Muṇḍaka 2.2.8; Gītā 2.25, 15.20; the Kāvaṣeya-gītā: he is not born, does not die, is neither bound nor freed; when the noose of ignorance is broken and the seeds of birth are burnt he goes to the highest bliss; Manu 12.85, 12.93). Since the highest end comes from that knowledge alone, one should know the Supreme Self, full bliss, as oneself, ‘I am (he)’, and think of nothing else (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.21; Muṇḍaka 2.2.5; Śvetāśvatara 1.12; Gītā 6.24–25). ‘So, by the way, the meaning of the whole śāstra has been shown in brief.’

Bhāṣya Śaṅkara (6), on the verse itself: since heedlessness alone is the seed of every evil, I call heedlessness death, not Yama. But some, blinded by the poison of objects, set in ignorance, seeing a second beside their own Self, call another than the death called heedlessness, Vaivasvata, death. ātmāvāsam: he who dwells in the self, in the intellect (Manu 8.92: Yama Vaivasvata, the king who stands in your heart). amṛtam: not subject to death. brahmacaryam: moving, delighting, in Brahman, his own Self; established in Brahman (Kaṭha 1.2.21: who but I can know that god, rejoicing and not rejoicing?). Further, the god rules a kingdom in the world of the fathers: śivaḥ, giving happiness, to the śivas, those of good deeds; aśivaḥ, giving unhappiness, to the aśivas, those of evil deeds.

Introduction So far the nature of death has been settled by ‘I call heedlessness death’; now he shows it standing as its effects.

Bhāṣya This death of ignorance called heedlessness first turns into the ‘mouth’: āsya is the sense of ‘I’ (ahaṃkāra), whose nature is pride (a verse: the dreadful ahaṃkāra, a great guṇa, is called āsya, because it gathers in all objects and is joined with the root as). Standing as the sense of ‘I’, it goes forth from it as desire; desire, moving toward its objects and checked, becomes anger, heedlessness and delusion. Then, through the ignorance become ‘I’, standing as the sense of ‘I’ and the rest, a man takes himself to be that — ‘I am a brāhmaṇa, I am a kṣatriya, I am lean, I am stout, the son of so-and-so, the grandson of this one’ — and, with passion, aversion and the rest, going in wrong ways contrary to śruti and smṛti, he does not reach even a little the union (yoga) with the Self, the Supreme Self, which is absorption (samādhi).

Bhāṣya Second explanation: ignorance, desire and action set saṃsāra going. Ignorance, the not grasping and the wrong grasping (of the Self), was named in ‘delusion is held to be death’ (verse 4); action will be named in ‘when action arises’ (verse 9); here desire is named: āsya is desire, because by it creatures are cast (as) into saṃsāra, or it is a mouth because it devours everything (Gītā 3.37: it is desire, it is anger). This death, standing as desire, turns into anger (Gītā 2.62: from desire anger is born); then, through ignorance become ‘I’ — the reflection of consciousness mounted on the board of ‘I’ and ‘mine’ — going in wrong ways, he does not reach union with the Self at all.

Then, when he takes a body, the gods, the senses, following his actions, float all about him: they circle all round. Therefore, from following the qualities of the senses, he goes to death, dying; from dying he goes on to another birth, and from it to death again. So, mounted on the chain of births and deaths, he is never freed. Since saṃsāra has ignorance of the Self for its cause, as long as he does not directly know the Supreme Self as himself he remains in saṃsāra, overcome by the three afflictions, dragged here and there by passion and the rest as by sea-monsters, ever more deluded.

Introduction So far ignorance and desire have been shown to be causes of bondage; now he shows that actions are too.

Bhāṣya This refutes the other view set out in verse 3, that deathlessness comes by action. Not only does deathlessness not come by action: when actions arise, men attached to their fruits follow there, into that fruit; since they follow there, they do not cross death: they turn again and again in saṃsāra, which is birth and death. Why? sadarthayogānavagamāt: sadarthayoga is union with the Real, with the Supreme Self, oneness; from not knowing it, that is, from not knowing one's own Self as Brahman, consciousness and bliss without a second, the embodied one goes on everywhere by union with enjoyment, with the thought that objects have savour. As a blind man wanders over low and high ground and thorny places, so he, without discernment, wanders everywhere in longing for the pleasure of objects.

Bhāṣya The moving of the senses among objects, in one overcome by passion, is a great delusion. That is: when a man holds objects to be unreal, his senses do not move toward them; not moving toward objects, they move toward the inward Self, and delusion ceases. When a man holds objects to be real, his senses have gone out to outward objects, and he does not know directly, as himself, the Supreme Self, real, without a second, inward (‘those whose minds are soiled by contact with women's bodies, blinded by the poison of objects, do not know Brahman’). So the great delusion is moving toward objects again and again (Manu 2.94: desire is never appeased by enjoying desires; like fire with ghee it only grows). Then there is union with false objects, sound and the rest, imagined by ignorance; in that union the embodied one's course, the course of saṃsāra, is constant, fixed. For it is well known that those who move toward objects without knowing the Supreme Self as their own, turned outward, reach animal and other births (Aitareya Āraṇyaka 2.1.1; and verse 13 below). Why is it constant? His inner self struck, his natural being as Brahman struck, by union with false objects, remembering women and other objects, he attends on them, not on the Supreme Self, on every side.

Bhāṣya Abhidhyā, brooding on objects, first strikes him, makes him fall from his own nature. Introduction How do the wise, conquering objects by steadfastness, cross death?

Introduction By what means, then, is death destroyed?

Bhāṣya Knowing thus the death called heedlessness, the seed of every evil, birth, death and the rest, as it arises in the form of anger and the rest, abandoning anger and the other faults that burn beings and gaining freedom from anger and the rest, standing in knowledge, as Brahman, consciousness, being, bliss without a second, one does not fear death (Taittirīya 2.9: knowing the bliss of Brahman, one fears nothing). As a mortal who has come into the range of death, saṃsāra, is overcome by death and destroyed, so the death of ignorance, come into the range of the knower of the Self, is destroyed (the Nāmamahodadhi: he who stands in knowledge, the fire of knowledge, bearing the thunderbolt of knowledge, is known as the slayer of death).

Bhāṣya Closing: so, by ‘when action arises’ (verse 9) and what follows, actions have been shown to be causes of bondage, and by ‘standing in knowledge one does not fear death’ knowledge alone has been shown to be the means of liberation. On this Dhṛtarāṣṭra raises an objection.

Bhāṣya How can actions be causes of bondage, when they speak of worlds won by sacrifice, by the Jyotiṣṭoma and the rest: worlds of the good, reached by the righteous, most holy, pure, everlasting? Here, in this round of saṃsāra, the Vedas declare those worlds, up to the world of Brahmā, to be the highest human end. Knowing this, how does the knower not go to action, the means to them, not perform it? Or: how does the knower, the knower of Brahman, not go to action, the means to the worlds up to the world of Brahmā?

Word by word
Thus the man without knowledge goes about there, and so too the Vedas speak of the range of ends (for him). He (the knower) does not come here: being the supreme Self, he goes to the highest; by the path (of knowledge) he strikes down the wrong paths.

Bhāṣya True. Thinking thus that the happiness to be won in the world of Brahmā and the rest (is worth having), blinded by the poison of objects, the ignorant man goes about there, in the action that is the means to those worlds; not the knower, for he sees the faults of ignorance and the rest (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.11: joyless are those worlds, covered by blind darkness; to them, after death, go the ignorant). So too the Vedas speak of the whole range of ends for that same ignorant man. Since they speak of them for the ignorant, not for the knower, the knower does not come here, does not engage in the transient happiness of the world of Brahmā or the action that is its means. What then does he do? Knowing the Supreme Self as himself, being the supreme Self, being Brahman itself, he goes to the highest; and by the path he strikes down the wrong paths, the paths opposed to the Self, causes of saṃsāra, which are dharma, adharma and worship (upāsanā).

Bhāṣya On another reading, evaṃ hi vidvān pariyāti tatra: the knower of the qualified Brahman reaches there, in the world of Brahmā and the rest, the fruit of his worship, and the Vedas speak of his range of ends: he does not come here, is not born again as the man of rites is, but by the worship of Brahman strikes down the wrong paths; having gone there and struck them down, being the supreme Self, in time he goes to the highest Brahman.

Bhāṣya Summing up: so far it has been shown that the ignorance called heedlessness is death and heedfulness, abiding in one's own nature, deathlessness (verse 4); from ‘from the mouth this death goes forth’ (verse 7) to ‘that is death, this that is in your body’ (verse 15), that this death, turned into its effects, is the cause of every evil; then, asking how it is destroyed, that it is destroyed by knowledge of the Self (verse 16); then the doubt whether the world of Brahmā and the rest are the human end (verse 17), answered by showing that they belong to the ignorant and are no human end (verse 18); and liberation by the path of knowledge was taught in ‘being the supreme Self he goes to the highest’, which states the oneness of the jīva and the Supreme. Not bearing this, Dhṛtarāṣṭra objects.

Word by word
If he (created) all this in order (and entered it), who is it that directs him, the unborn, the ancient? What is his purpose, or else what unhappiness (is there for him in it)? Tell me all that, O wise one, as it is.

Bhāṣya If the Supreme Self, marked by truth and the rest, having created in order the world from space to earth, entered it and, standing as the self made of food and the rest, transmigrates, who is it that directs, impels, him, the unborn, the ancient, marked by truth and the rest, in saṃsāra? If it is no other but himself, what purpose has he in moving in many births? Or, if he rests still in his own glory, not moving in births, what unhappiness, what evil, would there be in his entering saṃsāra? Tell me all this as it is. Yājñavalkya, the lotus among the knowers of Brahman, having told the creation of all from him and granted that he himself is the jīva, says the same: ‘if so, how is Brahman born in sinful births? how is the Lord joined with unwanted states?’ By ‘who is it that directs’ Dhṛtarāṣṭra states plainly the quibbler's objection to the view that Brahman is the jīva.

Padaccheda
दोषः महान् अत्र विभेद-योगे हि अनादि-योगेन भवन्ति नित्याः तथा अस्य न आधिक्यम् अपैति किंचित् अनादि-योगेन भवन्ति पुंसः

Word by word
Great is the fault here, in (the view of) difference. (The jīvas and the rest) are eternal by the beginningless union (with māyā); so nothing of his pre-eminence departs at all. By the beginningless union (with māyā) the persons (the jīvas, many) come to be.

Note puṃsaḥ: Śaṅkara takes the word first as nominative plural (pumāṃsaḥ, the jīvas), then, second, as genitive singular: by union with the beginningless māyā of the Puruṣa, the full Supreme Self, (the many) come to be.

Bhāṣya The objector's thought is this: from seeing a difference of director and directed, impossible in one changeless being, there must be difference. If plurality is granted to Brahman itself, then in this vibhedayoga, Brahman's being many, the fault is great: the non-dualists would be speakers of untruth and outside the Veda, the non-dual supreme truth, the heart of the Veda, would be sublated; and being turned into many forms, Brahman would be impermanent and the like, against ‘not gross …’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 3.8.8). If instead it is said that not Brahman's plurality but a difference between the jīva and the Supreme is held, there too the fault is great: destruction follows (Taittirīya 2.7: when he makes the least difference in it, fear comes to him; Kaṭha 2.1.10: from death to death goes he who sees difference here; Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.4.10: he who worships another deity, thinking ‘he is one, I another’, does not know, he is like a beast; Chāndogya 7.25.2: those who know otherwise have perishable worlds; Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.4.6). Or, with difference between the jīva and the Supreme, one speaks against ‘that thou art’, ‘I am Brahman’, ‘this Self is Brahman’, ‘the Self within all’, ‘this is your Self, the inner controller, the deathless’, ‘all this is that Self’, ‘I am you, O deity, you are I’, ‘this great unborn Self … is Brahman, the fearless’, and the like in śruti, smṛti, itihāsa and purāṇa: the great fault of being outside the Veda.

Bhāṣya How then, on your view, do the dealings of jīva, Lord and the rest come about, and how are they eternal? ‘By the beginningless union they are eternal’: the beginningless is māyā, called ignorance (Gītā 13.19: know Prakṛti and Puruṣa both to be beginningless; a verse: she is the one womb of the world, the self of all, ruler of all, the beginningless power of Maheśvara). By union with that beginningless māyā the jīvas and the rest are eternal: the Supreme Self, though without a second, can well have many forms by māyā. The śruti tells of the one having many forms by māyā: ‘he became the counterpart of every form’, ‘Indra by his māyās goes in many forms’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.5.19), ‘the one god hidden in all beings’ (Śvetāśvatara 6.11), ‘the sages call the one being by many names’ (Ṛgveda 1.164.46), ‘unborn, he is born in many ways’, and more; so the Mokṣadharma (the one self of beings is seen as one and many, like the moon in water), Yājñavalkya (as the one space is separate in pots, so the one Self is many, like the sun in vessels of water), the Kāvaṣeya-gītā (Brahman in all bodies, within and without, like space in jars) and the Supreme Lord (the eternal Self, all-pervading, changeless, one, is divided by the power that is māyā).

Bhāṣya Since the one has many forms by māyā alone, that same Supreme Lord, the self as cause, directs the self as effect, the jīva, by māyā, according to the effort the jīva has made; in truth he neither transmigrates nor makes another transmigrate (the Kāvaṣeya-gītā: he is not born, does not die, is neither slain nor slayer, neither bound nor binder, neither freed nor freeing; what is other than the Supreme Self is unreal; the Lord: ‘I am the ruler of all, by a nature beyond māyā; this lord neither transmigrates nor makes transmigrate’). Moreover, with a difference caused by māyā granted, the Supreme Self, though standing as effect and cause, loses nothing of his pre-eminence, the pre-eminence of his own nature, for saṃsāra is of the nature of māyā: he stays changeless as before. Therefore by union with beginningless ignorance the persons, the jīvas, come to be many; or, by union with the beginningless māyā of the Puruṣa, the full Supreme Self, they come to be many.

Bhāṣya The authorities he then quotes: the Anugītā (the Self, standing as ignorance and the guṇas; endowed with the power of ignorance it moves in all births; that delusion is to be abandoned; when the sense of ‘I’ is conquered the Self is accomplished; the devotee, by the grace of the Supreme, with the fire of knowledge, burns up all action and reaches the deathless called Viṣṇu); and Parāśara (the Viṣṇu Purāṇa, at length): knowledge, utterly pure, is the reality, and by error it stands as objects; the unwise see the world, which is knowledge, as objects and wander in a flood of delusion, while the pure-minded see it as knowledge; mountains, seas and earth are the play of consciousness; earth becomes a pot, the pot sherds, the sherds powder, the powder atoms — ‘tell me, what here is a thing?’; there is nothing at any time but knowledge, one, seen as many by minds divided by their own actions; the one All is Acyuta, ‘he is I, he is you, he is all this; give up the delusion of difference’; hearing this the king gave up difference and was freed in that birth; when ignorance, which makes difference, is wholly destroyed, who will make the unreal difference of the self and Brahman?; knowledge alone is the supreme Brahman.

Bhāṣya On renunciation: Sanatsujāta himself says all this plainly in the Kāvaṣeya-gītā of the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa. The Kāvaṣeyas, told to study the Vedas, marry, sacrifice, beget sons and at the end leave the body by austerity, answer that study and sacrifice serve them nothing, their work being done (‘we offer prāṇa in speech and speech in prāṇa’); he shows the world won by sacrifice to be faulty, impermanent (heaven ends in a fall when merit is spent, like a courtesan's house). The Yajurveda Upaniṣad (the Mahānārāyaṇa), after praising truth and the rest, ranks renunciation (nyāsa) above all, as the means to Brahman, eternal and unsurpassed bliss: ‘not by action, not by offspring or wealth; by renunciation some reached deathlessness … the renouncers, pure in mind, whose meaning is fixed by the knowledge of the Vedānta, by the yoga of renunciation are all freed in the worlds of Brahmā at the end’. So too the Bṛhadāraṇyaka (4.4.22), Vāsudeva (Gītā 4.21, 12.16, 12.17, 14.25, 18.49, 18.66), the Anugītā (knowledge is marked by renunciation; so, putting knowledge first, let the wise man renounce), Vyāsa to Śuka in the Śāntiparvan (a creature is bound by action, freed by knowledge; so the renouncers do no action; let him go forth to the life of the wandering mendicant), Nārada (renouncing all actions, austerity and learning, with a lump of food for support one can wander everywhere; giving up truth and untruth one becomes free of the guṇas), Bṛhaspati (only one restrained, free of passion, gains knowledge), and Brahmā in the Āśvamedhika (the householder, the brahmacārin or the forest-dweller who wishes for liberation should take to the highest way). Hence for both the knower and the seeker of liberation, the renunciation of all action alone is fitting.

Bhāṣya Closing: so far the one Supreme Self's having many forms by union with beginningless māyā has been told; now he says that the Lord's being the cause of the world is also due to the adjunct, māyā.

Bhāṣya That the Lord, real in truth, endowed with lordship and the rest, the Supreme Lord, always makes the universe by union with modification, preceded by seeing (īkṣaṇa) and the rest — all that is so; but it is his power, the power of the Supreme Self, māyā alone, that makes it, not the Supreme Self, who is without before or after: so I think. Causality does not belong to him, consciousness and bliss without a second, by himself, but by his entering into māyā. What proves that he is joined with such a power? ‘In his union with that the Vedas are’: for the existence of māyā, the material cause of the world, the Vedas are the means of knowledge (Ṛgveda: ‘Indra by his māyās goes in many forms’; Śvetāśvatara 4.9: from this the master of māyā creates the universe; 4.10: know the great Lord as the master of māyā; 1.3: the power of the god, hidden by its own guṇas; Gītā 7.14, 4.6, 9.10; and verses: this māyā of yours deludes those who do not know things, by it the fool mounts the knowledge of self on the not-self; hard to cross is this deep māyā of Viṣṇu, by which deluded the mind does not know the Supreme Lord).

Bhāṣya Closing: so far, by ‘I call heedlessness death’ and what follows, the nature of death, its standing as its effects and the many evils it causes have been shown; asked how it is destroyed, he showed that fearlessness comes from knowledge of the Self alone (verse 16); and the two incidental objections (verses 17 and 19) have been answered. Now Dhṛtarāṣṭra speaks, to learn the nature of action.

yeṣām dharmeṣu vispardhā bale balavatām iva te brāhmaṇāḥ itaḥ pretya svarge yānti prakāśatām
Word by word
Those who have rivalry in dharmas, like the strong in strength — those brāhmaṇas, departing from here, go to brightness in heaven.

Bhāṣya Śaṅkara comments on verses 25 and 26 together.

Bhāṣya Those given to objects, hearing of the enjoyment of Urvaśī and the like in the dwelling of heaven, feel rivalry, contention, over the dharmas that are its means, the Jyotiṣṭoma and the rest: ‘doing a better dharma than this man, may I be happier than he’. ‘Like the strong in strength’: as a strong king, seeing a strong king, contends, ‘gaining more strength than he and conquering him, may I be happier than he’. Those brāhmaṇas, entitled to sacrifice and attached to its fruit, departing from here by the path of smoke and the rest, reach brightness in heaven, in the form of stars and the like (Chāndogya 5.10.3–5: those who worship sacrifice, works of merit and gifts go to smoke … this is King Soma, the food of the gods; having stayed there until it is spent, they return by the same way).

Note The edition: the transcript notes that prakāśatām reads like prakāśanām in the scan; the bhāṣya has prakāśatām.

येषां धर्मे न च स्पर्धा तेषां तज्ज्ञानसाधनम् । ते ब्राह्मणा इतो मुक्ताः स्वर्गं यान्ति त्रिविष्टपम् ॥ २६ ॥
yeṣāṃ dharme na ca spardhā teṣāṃ tajjñānasādhanam | te brāhmaṇā ito muktāḥ svargaṃ yānti triviṣṭapam || 26 ||
Padaccheda
येषाम् धर्मे न च स्पर्धा तेषाम् तत् ज्ञान-साधनम् ते ब्राह्मणाः इतः मुक्ताः स्वर्गम् यान्ति त्रिविष्टपम्
yeṣām dharme na ca spardhā teṣām tat jñāna-sādhanam te brāhmaṇāḥ itaḥ muktāḥ svargam yānti triviṣṭapam
Word by word
Those who have no rivalry in dharma — for them that (action) is a means to knowledge. Those brāhmaṇas, freed from here, go to heaven, Triviṣṭapa (Brahman, which is full bliss).

Note The edition prints tajjñānasādhanam as one word; Śaṅkara's gloss (tat yajñādikaṃ karma … jñānasādhanam) divides it as tat and jñānasādhanam.

Bhāṣya Those whose minds are not drawn by objects have no rivalry, no contention, over dharma such as the Jyotiṣṭoma, the means to transient fruit. For them, doing action for the Lord without regard to the fruit, that action, sacrifice and the rest, is the means to knowledge, through purification (as he says below: by merit destroying sin, he is then born lit by knowledge). Those brāhmaṇas who, pure in mind through sacrifice and the rest, know the Supreme Self as themselves, departing from this world of effect and cause, freed, go to svarga, which is happiness: Brahman, full bliss. Its difference from the other heaven is told by triviṣṭapa: it protects (pāti) one who has entered his own nature, freed from the three, the afflictions from oneself and the rest, or sattva and the rest, or waking and the rest; or it protects the qualified man who has entered (viṣṭa) it, (freed) by those three.

Introduction Introducing the next verse: now he tells the conduct of the knower, the paramahaṃsa-parivrājaka-ācārya, who has renounced all actions from (the stage of) brahmacarya itself, has heard the Vedānta and the rest, and abides in his own glory.

tasya samyaksamācāram āhur vedavido janāḥ | nainaṃ manyeta bhūyiṣṭhaṃ bāhyam ābhyantaraṃ janam || 27 ||
Padaccheda
तस्य सम्यक्-समाचारम् आहुः वेद-विदः जनाः न एनम् मन्येत भूयिष्ठम् बाह्यम् आभ्यन्तरम् जनम्
tasya samyak-samācāram āhuḥ veda-vidaḥ janāḥ na enam manyeta bhūyiṣṭham bāhyam ābhyantaram janam
Word by word
Those who know the Veda tell his right conduct: (he should live so that) people, outer and inner, do not think much of him.

Bhāṣya The knowers of the Veda tell the right conduct of that dispassionate knower: let (people) not think much of him, this yogin; people, inner and outer: the inner are sons, friends, wife and the like, the others the outer. That is, as sons, friends and the rest do not grasp him, so he lives beyond their range.

Note The construction is loose: Śaṅkara takes janam, though accusative, as those who are not to think much of the knower.

यत्र मन्येत भूयिष्ठं प्रावृषीव तृणोदकम् । अन्नपानं च ब्राह्मणस्तज्जीवेन्नानुसंज्वरेत् ॥ २८ ॥
yatra manyeta bhūyiṣṭhaṃ prāvṛṣīva tṛṇodakam | annapānaṃ ca brāhmaṇas taj jīven nānusaṃjvaret || 28 ||
Padaccheda
यत्र मन्येत भूयिष्ठम् प्रावृषि इव तृण-उदकम् अन्न-पानम् च ब्राह्मणः तत् जीवेत् न अनुसंज्वरेत्
yatra manyeta bhūyiṣṭham prāvṛṣi iva tṛṇa-udakam anna-pānam ca brāhmaṇaḥ tat jīvet na anusaṃjvaret
Word by word
Where the brāhmaṇa would find food and drink plentiful, as grass and water in the rains — (staying there) he should live on that, and not fret.

Introduction In what kind of place does he live?

Bhāṣya In a place free of trouble from wild beasts and the like, where he thinks food, drink and the rest are plentiful, as grass and water are plentiful in the rainy season. Some read tṛṇolapa, quoting ‘tṛṇolapa is known among the rice and other foods of munis’; others take it as a kind of sacred dūrvā grass. Staying there he should live on that food and drink, and not fret, not be troubled: otherwise, in a place without food and drink, he would be troubled how the life of the body could go on, and then there would be no success in yoga.

यत्राकथयमानस्य प्रयच्छत्यशिवं भयम् । अतिरिक्तमिवाकुर्वन्स श्रेयान्नेतरो जनः ॥ २९ ॥
yatrākathayamānasya prayacchaty aśivaṃ bhayam | atiriktam ivākurvan sa śreyān netaro janaḥ || 29 ||
Padaccheda
यत्र अकथयमानस्य प्रयच्छति अशिवम् भयम् अतिरिक्तम् इव अकुर्वन् सः श्रेयान् न इतरः जनः
yatra akathayamānasya prayacchati aśivam bhayam atiriktam iva akurvan saḥ śreyān na itaraḥ janaḥ
Word by word
Where (a man) gives unkind fear (insult) to one who does not speak (of himself), not doing, as it were, anything more (than for any brāhmaṇa) — he is better (for the knower), not the other man.

Introduction And (he should live) near people of this kind.

Bhāṣya In the place where a man gives unkind fear, insult and the like, to the paramahaṃsa-parivrājaka-ācārya who does not speak, who is silent, hiding his greatness, clothed by anything, fed by anything, sleeping anywhere, seeing only the Self as the world, moving like one dull, dumb, a child or a goblin; ‘not doing anything more’: as one who knows the marks of the man of firm wisdom, knowing him to be a knower of Brahman, worships him as the Lord with prostration, salutation and the like — not doing, from not knowing him, more than the honour due to mere birth as a brāhmaṇa, the worship fitting a knower of Brahman, but rather insulting him: that man is better for this knower, not the other who worships him as the Lord with prostration and the rest (Manu 2.162: a learned brāhmaṇa should shrink from honour as from poison and always long for dishonour as for nectar; Parāśara: honour does the greatest harm to the wealth of yoga; the yogin despised by men gains success in yoga).

यो वाकथयमानस्य ह्यात्मानं नानुसंज्वरेत् । ब्रह्मस्वं नोपहन्याद्वा तदन्नं संमतं सताम् ॥ ३० ॥
yo vākathayamānasya hy ātmānaṃ nānusaṃjvaret | brahmasvaṃ nopahanyād vā tad annaṃ saṃmataṃ satām || 30 ||
Padaccheda
यः वा अकथयमानस्य हि आत्मानम् न अनुसंज्वरेत् ब्रह्म-स्वम् न उपहन्यात् वा तत् अन्नम् संमतम् सताम्
yaḥ vā akathayamānasya hi ātmānam na anusaṃjvaret brahma-svam na upahanyāt vā tat annam saṃmatam satām
Word by word
He who does not vex the self of one who does not speak (of himself), or does not harm a brāhmaṇa's property — his food is approved by the good.

Introduction Whose food, then, is to be eaten?

Bhāṣya He who does not vex the self of the one who does not speak, who is silent, who, having withdrawn all, abides as full bliss; or does not harm his brahmasva, the cloth, skin, kuśa grass, books and the like that are the means of his steadfastness in Brahman (‘jewels, gold and the like are not called the yogin's own; kuśa, bark, cloth and the like they know as the yogin's brahmasva’), or any other property of a brāhmaṇa: his food is approved by the good as fit to eat.

नित्यमज्ञातचर्या म इति मन्येत ब्राह्मणः । ज्ञातीनां तु वसन्मध्ये नैव विन्देत किंचन ॥ ३१ ॥
nityam ajñātacaryā ma iti manyeta brāhmaṇaḥ | jñātīnāṃ tu vasan madhye naiva vindeta kiṃcana || 31 ||
Padaccheda
नित्यम् अज्ञात-चर्या मे इति मन्येत ब्राह्मणः ज्ञातीनाम् तु वसन् मध्ये न एव विन्देत किंचन
nityam ajñāta-caryā me iti manyeta brāhmaṇaḥ jñātīnām tu vasan madhye na eva vindeta kiṃcana
Word by word
‘An unknown way of life is always to be mine’: so the brāhmaṇa should think. Living in the midst of kinsmen, he should take nothing at all (to himself).

Introduction Again he tells the conduct of that same (knower).

Bhāṣya Nityam: without fail. ‘An unknown way of life, a hidden way of life, is to be mine’: so should the brāhmaṇa, the knower of Brahman, think. Living near kinsmen, sons, friends, wife and the like, he should not take, not accept, anything at all (some read kaścana). The yogin should not take up sons, friends, wife and the rest; abandoning them, devoted to his own Self alone, he should live hidden (a śruti: giving up family, sons and wife, the Vedāṅgas, sacrifice and the sacred thread, the muni should move hidden; Vasiṣṭha: he of whom no one knows whether he is good or not, learned or unlearned, of good or bad conduct, is a brāhmaṇa; though knowing, the wise man should act in the world like one dull). Liberation, the gaining of steadfastness in knowledge, is for such a man only, not for another, because of the many distractions.

Bhāṣya Second explanation: ajñātacaryā me: in the unknown — Brahman, not an object of the eye and the rest, beyond the reach of speech, abiding as knowledge that neither rises nor sets, untouched by hunger and the rest, whose nature is full bliss, within all, inward — conduct, steadfastness in the form of absorption (samādhi), is to be mine; not in the outward body, senses, sons, friends, wife and the rest, as ‘I am stout, I am lean, I go, I stand, impotent, one-eyed, dumb, deaf, the son of so-and-so, the grandson of this one, I am a brāhmaṇa, a kṣatriya, my wife, my son, my wealth, my dear kin and friends’: so should the brāhmaṇa, the knower of Brahman, think (Kena 1: what one does not see with the eye, but by which the eyes see, that alone is Brahman). Since steadfastness must be in the unknown Brahman, the kinsmen are the senses and the rest (a verse: anger, pride and the other faults, the objects and the senses, these are called the kinsmen of the embodied one); living among them — seeing, hearing, touching, smelling, going, eating, thinking, knowing — he should not take the Self to be the knower and the rest, for the Self is their witness (Chāndogya 8.12.4: he who knows ‘let me smell this’ is the Self). That is, he should not take the two bodies and their properties as the Self.

को ह्येवमन्तरात्मानं ब्राह्मणो मन्तुमर्हति । निर्लिङ्गमचलं शुद्धं सर्वद्वन्द्वविवर्जितम् ॥ ३२ ॥
ko hy evam antarātmānaṃ brāhmaṇo mantum arhati | nirliṅgam acalaṃ śuddhaṃ sarvadvandvavivarjitam || 32 ||
Padaccheda
कः हि एवम् अन्तरात्मानम् ब्राह्मणः मन्तुम् अर्हति निर्लिङ्गम् अचलम् शुद्धम् सर्व-द्वन्द्व-विवर्जितम्
kaḥ hi evam antarātmānam brāhmaṇaḥ mantum arhati nirliṅgam acalam śuddham sarva-dvandva-vivarjitam
Word by word
For what brāhmaṇa ought to think thus (as the body and its ways) of the inner Self, without marks, unmoving, pure, free of all pairs of opposites?

Introduction Why is (the Self) not to be taken so?

Bhāṣya Nirliṅgam: free of the gross and the subtle (bodies); acalam: empty of action, agent and the rest; śuddham: free of the fault of ignorance; sarvadvandvavivarjitam: free of hunger, thirst, grief, delusion, old age, death, cold, heat, pleasure, pain and the other properties. What brāhmaṇa can think thus, in the way said, as the two bodies and their properties — ‘I am stout, I am lean, I go, I see, dumb, deaf, one-eyed, I am happy, I am unhappy’ — of the inner Self, within all, the witness of the knower and the rest, not an object of the means of knowledge? Were he to do so, his very being a brāhmaṇa would be lost (as he says below: he who does not depart from truth is to be known as a brāhmaṇa).

योऽन्यथा सन्तमात्मानमन्यथा प्रतिपद्यते । किं तेन न कृतं पापं चोरेणात्मापहारिणा ॥ ३३ ॥
yo 'nyathā santam ātmānam anyathā pratipadyate | kiṃ tena na kṛtaṃ pāpaṃ coreṇātmāpahāriṇā || 33 ||
Padaccheda
यः अन्यथा सन्तम् आत्मानम् अन्यथा प्रतिपद्यते किम् तेन न कृतम् पापम् चोरेण आत्म-अपहारिणा
yaḥ anyathā santam ātmānam anyathā pratipadyate kim tena na kṛtam pāpam coreṇa ātma-apahāriṇā
Word by word
He who takes the Self, which is in one way, to be in another — what sin is not done by that thief, the stealer of the Self?

Introduction He who thinks so is the worst of sinners.

Bhāṣya He who takes his Self, which is in one way — as knowledge, without marks, stainless, pure, free of all pairs, as Brahman, consciousness, being, bliss without a second — to be in another way, as the two bodies and their properties, ‘doer, enjoyer, happy, unhappy, I am stout, I am lean, the son of so-and-so, the grandson of this one, I am a brāhmaṇa, I am a kṣatriya’: what sin is not done by that fool who does not know the Self, the thief who steals the Self? All, the great sins and the rest, are done by him alone (Īśa 3: sunless are those worlds, covered by blind darkness; to them go after death those who slay the self; a verse: he who, having gained the state of a brāhmaṇa in this world, does not pass out of saṃsāra, is a slayer of Brahman). So, giving up taking as self the body, senses and the rest, which are objects, one should be steadfast in the Supreme Self, the unknown, beyond the reach of speech and the rest.

अश्रान्तः स्यादनादाता संमतो निरुपद्रवः । शिष्टो न शिष्टवत्स स्याद्ब्राह्मणो ब्रह्मवित्कविः ॥ ३४ ॥
aśrāntaḥ syād anādātā saṃmato nirupadravaḥ | śiṣṭo na śiṣṭavat sa syād brāhmaṇo brahmavit kaviḥ || 34 ||
Padaccheda
अश्रान्तः स्यात् अनादाता संमतः निरुपद्रवः शिष्टः न शिष्टवत् सः स्यात् ब्राह्मणः ब्रह्म-वित् कविः
aśrāntaḥ syāt anādātā saṃmataḥ nirupadravaḥ śiṣṭaḥ na śiṣṭavat saḥ syāt brāhmaṇaḥ brahma-vit kaviḥ
Word by word
He who does not take (the not-self to himself) would be unwearied (by saṃsāra), esteemed, free from troubles. Though cultured, he should not be like a cultured man — that brāhmaṇa, the knower of Brahman, the sage.

Introduction What comes to one who does not see the body, the senses and their properties (as self)?

Bhāṣya Anādātā: he who does not take up the not-self, the body, the senses and their properties, as self; that man is unwearied, not tired by saṃsāra, since hunger and the rest belong to the body and the rest (a śruti: hunger and thirst belong to prāṇa, grief and delusion to the mind, old age and death to the body); for when the two bodies are superimposed (on the Self), their properties are superimposed too. So, being unwearied, he is nirupadrava: the troubles are the obstacles, anger, joy, greed, delusion and the rest, and he is without them. saṃmataḥ: esteemed as cultured by the wise. He should not be like a cultured man, not behave so, but move like one dull: the brāhmaṇa, the knower of Brahman, the sage.

Padaccheda
ये यथा वान्तम् अश्नन्ति बालाः नित्यम् अभूतये एवम् ते वान्तम् अश्नन्ति स्व-वीर्यस्य उपभोजनात्
ye yathā vāntam aśnanti bālāḥ nityam abhūtaye evam te vāntam aśnanti sva-vīryasya upabhojanāt
Word by word
As children (dogs and fools) eat vomit, always to their ruin, so those (who make their greatness known) eat vomit, by making use of their own power.

Introduction Now, censuring the one who does not move hidden, he says this.

Bhāṣya Bāla is used of a fool and of a dog (‘the fool is called bāla, the dog too is known as bāla’). As children, dogs or fools eat vomit, what has been thrown up, so those cultured knowers of Brahman who proclaim their own greatness and do not move hidden eat vomit, because they make use of their own power; this eating of vomit is to their ruin, for evil only. So he should be hidden and behave as one not cultured.

anāḍhyā mānuṣe vitte āḍhyā vedeṣu ye dvijāḥ | te durdharṣā duṣprakampyā vidyāt tān brahmaṇas tanum || 36 ||
Padaccheda
अनाढ्याः मानुषे वित्ते आढ्याः वेदेषु ये द्विजाः ते दुर्धर्षाः दुष्प्रकम्प्याः विद्यात् तान् ब्रह्मणः तनुम्
anāḍhyāḥ mānuṣe vitte āḍhyāḥ vedeṣu ye dvijāḥ te durdharṣāḥ duṣprakampyāḥ vidyāt tān brahmaṇaḥ tanum
Word by word
The twice-born who are not rich in human wealth, (but) rich in the Vedas — they are unassailable, not to be shaken; one should know them as the body of Brahman.

Introduction Now, praising the yogin, he says this.

Bhāṣya Anāḍhyāḥ: not esteemed, unattached in mind, in human wealth, wife, sons, riches and the like; rich in the Vedas: in what the Vedas teach, non-violence, truth, not stealing, non-possession, brahmacarya and the means to absorption (samādhi). Those twice-born are unassailable, not to be shaken; one should know them as the body of Brahman, of the very nature of Brahman.

सर्वान्स्विष्टकृतो देवान्विद्याद्य इह कश्चन । न समानो ब्राह्मणस्य यस्मिन्प्रयतते स्वयम् ॥ ३७ ॥

Padaccheda
सर्वान् स्विष्टकृतः देवान् विद्यात् यः इह कश्चन न समानः ब्राह्मणस्य यस्मिन् प्रयतते स्वयम्
sarvān sviṣṭakṛtaḥ devān vidyāt yaḥ iha kaścana na samānaḥ brāhmaṇasya yasmin prayatate svayam
Word by word
Whoever here would know all the gods who make the offering well (and sacrifice to them) is not equal to the brāhmaṇa (who knows Brahman); nor is (the god) toward whom he himself strives (with his offering).

Introduction Further, this is the greatness of the knower of Brahman.

Bhāṣya Sarvān: Agni and the rest; sviṣṭakṛtaḥ: those who make (the offering) well offered (a śruti: making it well offered, he is Sviṣṭakṛt). Whoever here would know the gods, each one, for the giving (of the oblation), even one who sacrifices to all the deities, is not equal to the brāhmaṇa, the knower of Brahman. Nor is this a wonder: even the particular deity toward which the sacrificer himself strives for the fruit, giving the oblation with ‘this to Agni, this to Indra’, the deity that is the counterpart of the oblation, is not equal to the knower of Brahman; how much less the sacrificer, a beast of the gods (the Mokṣadharma: even (the god) to whom the best of the twice-born sacrifices with the mantra does not come near the likeness of the brāhmaṇa, how much less (the sacrificer); Manu: there is no being here higher than the knowers of Brahman).

Bhāṣya Honour and silence never dwell together, in one place. Introduction Now he shows that for one who dwells among the things of honour there is no liberation.

Bhāṣya From dwelling in the field of honour: for one who moves in the manifold that is the range of honour and performs the action that is the means to heaven, cattle, food and the like, blinded by the poison of objects, there is fortune. And that fortune is an obstacle, opposed to the path of the highest good (the Mokṣadharma: the delight of one dwelling in the village is a binding rope; cutting it the good go on, the evil do not cut it). By one so overcome by fortune, deluded, engaged in objects, lacking wisdom, lacking knowledge, the fortune of Brahman, which is the bliss of Brahman, is very hard to gain (the Hairaṇyagarbha: that which is eternal, a mass of consciousness and bliss, free of guṇa and form, called bliss, supreme, pure, is called the fortune of Brahman). Word by word
The good rightly declare the doors (to the fortune of Brahman), of many kinds, hard to practise: truth and uprightness, modesty, restraint, purity and knowledge — six, which bar honour and delusion.

Introduction Now he shows the doors by which one enters the fortune of Brahman.

Bhāṣya The doors are the doors for entering the fortune of Brahman; the good declare them rightly; of many kinds, hard to practise. Which are they? satya: speaking of things as they are, and the good of beings; ārjava: freedom from crookedness; hrī: shame at doing what should not be done; dama: the quieting of the inner organ (some say: of the outer organs); śauca: the washing away of impurity; vidyā: the knowledge of Brahman. These six bar honour and delusion.

Bhāṣya Since the Vedas with the mind do not enter this Supreme Self (Taittirīya 2.4: ‘from which words turn back, not reaching it, with the mind’), for that very reason the Supreme Self, beyond the reach of speech, is silence. Rites and meditations are taught by the Veda because they are of use to man step by step: they purify the inner organ, which serves knowledge, the means of reaching liberation. So the Veda shows the Supreme Self as the highest end: it shows that the worlds of heaven are not the end of man and are joyless, the lot of the ignorant (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.11 ‘joyless are those worlds, covered by blind darkness’); that the knower of the Self has done all there is to do (4.4.12 ‘if a man should know the Self as “I am this”, wishing what, for whose desire, should he suffer after the body?’); that the knower of the Self reaches deathlessness and the one who does not know it perishes and meets evil (4.4.14 ‘while we are here we may know it; if not, great is the loss’); and with passages such as 4.4.15 (‘when one sees him directly as the Self, the god, lord of what was and will be, one shrinks from nothing’) it shows again and again his nature, the seeing of him and its fruit. Then, as if thinking ‘how shall I free this sinner, dragged this way and that by passion and the rest as by crocodiles, overcome by objects, and set him in the highest place, the Supreme Self, full bliss, self-rule, called liberation?’, it applies sacrifice and the rest as means to the desire to know, which leads to knowledge, the means of reaching it (4.4.22 ‘him the brahmins seek to know by the recitation of the Veda, by sacrifice, by giving, by austerity, by fasting’). So sacrifice and the rest are of use to man only as serving that; directed to heaven and the like they are of no more use than the śyena rite, being causes of the evil of saṃsāra (Muṇḍaka 1.2.7 ‘unsteady boats are these forms of sacrifice … the fools who delight in this as the good go again to old age and death’). Since the Vedas, teaching liberation and its means, put an end to the evil of saṃsāra, they can protect the knower of the Veda: with all this in mind he speaks three verses, and in the first shows the Supreme Self, ever immediate, the highest human end.

Bhāṣya By the particular forms, name and the rest, of that Supreme Self alone, imagined by māyā, this world appears. How is this known? Having pointed out the many forms made by māyā (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.5.19 ‘Indra by his powers goes in many forms’), the Vedas declare his true form (the same passage: ‘this Brahman, without before or after, without inside or outside; this Self is Brahman, perceiving all’); having pointed out with ‘there are two forms of Brahman, the formed and the formless’ (2.3.1) the world of the formed and the formless as his, they declare his true form with ‘not this, not this’; having pointed out ether and the rest down to the earth as his effect (Taittirīya 2.1), they declare his true form by way of the sheaths, ‘from which words turn back’ (2.4); having pointed out name and the rest up to prāṇa as the world caused by his māyā (Chāndogya 7.1.1 ‘teach me, sir’), they declare his true form, the plenitude beyond darkness standing in its own greatness (7.24.1 ‘where one sees nothing else … that is the plenitude’). Not the Vedas alone: the sages too describe that Brahman as viśvavairūpya, of a form contrary to the form of the universe (Parāśara, Viṣṇu Purāṇa 6.7.53–54: ‘that in which all difference has set, mere being, beyond the reach of the mind, known by the self, is the knowledge called Brahman; and that is the highest form of Viṣṇu, called the formless, … marked by being unlike the form of the universe’).

Introduction Introducing verses 8–9: now he shows in two verses that rites done for the Lord are of use to man step by step, through the purity that knowledge, the means of reaching him, requires; and that the others, being causes of the evil of saṃsāra, are of no use to man.

Bhāṣya For the sake of that Brahman whose form is contrary to the form of the universe the Veda teaches austerity, the kṛcchra, the cāndrāyaṇa and the like, and sacrifice, the jyotiṣṭoma and the like. By these two the knowing man, who knows the application just stated and does the rites for the Lord, gains merit, that is, is joined with the unseen result (apūrva) born of the rite. Having destroyed sin by that merit, afterwards, all his taint destroyed, he becomes one whose self is lit up by knowledge: his own nature, Brahman, consciousness, being and bliss, without a second, is revealed by knowledge.

Introduction Now he states the faults of pride.

Introduction It was said ‘renunciation is sixfold’ (2.24); on this he says:

Bhāṣya The sixfold renunciation is better; among these six renunciations the third is hard to do, hard to accomplish. By that third renunciation they cross sorrow, divided into the kinds that arise from oneself and the rest; when that renunciation is made, all is conquered.

Padaccheda

Introduction He now shows the six kinds of renunciation (tyāga) named in 2.24–25.

Bhāṣya Arhate: to one who is fit. Giving sons and wealth to a fit man who asks is a pair of renunciations, the first among the six. iṣṭa is giving in the rites of the Veda (śrauta), pūrta giving in the rites of the smṛti; some say iṣṭa is what is given to the gods, pūrta what is given to the ancestors: this pair is the second. nityaṃ vairāgyayogataḥ goes with the next verse: the giving up of wealth and the rest by one whose mind is pure and who, seeing in things the fault of impermanence and the rest, has turned from them, and the giving up of desire, together are the third.

and the renunciation of desire, O lord of kings: that is held to be the third. By these one becomes heedful; and that (heedfulness) too is considered to have eight qualities.

Bhāṣya The giving up of wealth and the rest out of dispassion (26) and the giving up of desire make the third pair. Asked what comes of these, he says: in the heedful desire perishes; he quotes the Rājadharma: ‘the stain of dharma is ill-will, the stain of wealth is hoarding; desire has heedfulness as its stain, and it increases good qualities further’. Introduction What are those (eight qualities)? He shows them.

Bhāṣya Satya: speaking as things are. dhyāna: the flow of the mind upon some auspicious support, such as the Puruṣa in the orb (of the sun), unbroken like a stream of oil. samādhāna: having withdrawn Viśva and the rest by the praṇava, abiding as the non-dual Brahman, the Self, whose own nature is consciousness, being and bliss. codya: the questioning ‘who am I, whose, whence?’ and the like. vairāgya: freedom from thirst for objects seen and heard of (in the Veda). asteya: not stealing, either of the self or of goods; stealing the self is named in 1.33 (‘he who takes the self, which is one way, to be another’). brahmacarya: giving up the eightfold union, as it is said: ‘remembering, speaking of, play, looking, talk in secret, intention, resolve and the act itself: this the wise call the eightfold union; brahmacarya is the opposite, with these same eight marks’. asaṃgraha: not taking possession of sons, wife, fields and the rest. indriyebhyaś ca pañcabhyo manasaś caiva bhārata | atītānāgatebhyaś ca muktaś cet sa sukhī bhavet || 34 ||
Padaccheda
इन्द्रियेभ्यः च पञ्चभ्यः मनसः च एव भारत अतीत-अनागतेभ्यः च मुक्तः चेत् सः सुखी भवेत्
indriyebhyaḥ ca pañcabhyaḥ manasaḥ ca eva bhārata atīta-anāgatebhyaḥ ca muktaḥ cet saḥ sukhī bhavet
Word by word
If one is free from the five senses (with their objects, now present) and from the mind, O Bhārata, and from past and future (ones), he would be happy (and free).

Introduction What is that? To this he says:

Bhāṣya Indriyebhyaḥ: from the five senses with their objects, those present; likewise from the past and the future. If one is free of them, he, being happy, is free indeed.

धृतराष्ट्र उवाचdhṛtarāṣṭra uvāca
आख्यानपञ्चमैर्वेदैर्भूयिष्ठं कत्थ्यते जनः । तथा चान्ये चतुर्वेदास्त्रिवेदाश्च तथापरे ॥ ३५ ॥
ākhyānapañcamair vedair bhūyiṣṭhaṃ katthyate janaḥ | tathā cānye caturvedās trivedāś ca tathāpare || 35 ||
Padaccheda
आख्यान-पञ्चमैः वेदैः भूयिष्ठम् कत्थ्यते जनः तथा च अन्ये चतुः-वेदाः त्रि-वेदाः च तथा अपरे
ākhyāna-pañcamaiḥ vedaiḥ bhūyiṣṭham katthyate janaḥ tathā ca anye catuḥ-vedāḥ tri-vedāḥ ca tathā apare
Word by word
A man prides himself greatly on the Vedas with the narratives as the fifth; so too others (are) men of four Vedas, and others again men of three Vedas,

Bhāṣya Ākhyānapañcamāḥ: the Vedas whose fifth is the narrative, the Purāṇa; so the Chāndogya speaks of ‘the Itihāsa and Purāṇa as the fifth’ (7.1.2). By these Vedas a man thinks very highly of himself, ‘I am above all’ (katthyate = ślāghyate, bahumanyate).

Bhāṣya Some read kathyate: a man is called ‘one of five Vedas’ by the Vedas with the narratives as fifth.

द्विवेदाश्चैकवेदाश्च अनृचश्च तथापरे । एतेषु मेऽधिकं ब्रूहि यमहं वेद ब्राह्मणम् ॥ ३६ ॥
dvivedāś caikavedāś ca anṛcaś ca tathāpare | eteṣu me 'dhikaṃ brūhi yam ahaṃ veda brāhmaṇam || 36 ||
Padaccheda
द्वि-वेदाः च एक-वेदाः च अनृचः च तथा अपरे एतेषु मे अधिकम् ब्रूहि यम् अहम् वेद ब्राह्मणम्
dvi-vedāḥ ca eka-vedāḥ ca anṛcaḥ ca tathā apare eteṣu me adhikam brūhi yam aham veda brāhmaṇam
Word by word
men of two Vedas and of one Veda, and others again without the Ṛk. Tell me who among these is the greater, whom I may know as a brāhmaṇa.

Bhāṣya Anṛcaḥ: others, who have given up the Ṛk and the other Vedas. eteṣu: among these men; adhikam: the best; veda: vidyām, I may know.

एकवेदस्य चाज्ञानाद्वेदास्ते बहवोऽभवन् । सत्यस्यैकस्य राजेन्द्र सत्ये कश्चिदवस्थितः ॥ ३७ ॥
ekavedasya cājñānād vedās te bahavo 'bhavan | satyasyaikasya rājendra satye kaścid avasthitaḥ || 37 ||
Padaccheda
एक-वेदस्य च अज्ञानात् वेदाः ते बहवः अभवन् सत्यस्य एकस्य राजेन्द्र सत्ये कश्चित् अवस्थितः
eka-vedasya ca ajñānāt vedāḥ te bahavaḥ abhavan satyasya ekasya rājendra satye kaścit avasthitaḥ
Word by word
From not knowing the one Veda, (Brahman,) the Vedas came to be many: (from not knowing) the one True, O lord of kings. (Only) some one stands in the True.

Introduction To show that he alone is a brāhmaṇa who abides as the non-dual Brahman, the Self, whose own nature is consciousness, being and bliss, he first shows that all else is rooted in not knowing that.

Bhāṣya Ekavedasya: of the one Veda: the knowable has the form ‘this’, (knowing) is not of the form ‘this’; vedana, knowing, is the Veda: so ‘of the one, the non-dual, whose form is consciousness’. From not understanding that one Veda, Brahman, the Ṛk and the other Vedas became many. They inquire into it in order to make it understood, and so gained the name Veda. Or they are Vedas because they establish what is (sadbhāva); or because they are the means of knowing; or because by them one gains oneself as dependent on Brahman; or because they are the cause of gaining Brahman as the Self: they are called Vedas because they bear on the senses of the roots vid ‘to consider’, vid ‘to be’, vid ‘to know’ and vid (vidḷ) ‘to gain’.

Bhāṣya What is the one Veda? ‘Brahman is truth, knowledge, the infinite’ (Taittirīya 2.1). So from not understanding the True, Brahman, the one Veda, the Vedas were expounded as many: all of them are means to seeing its meaning. ‘O king, have you too, knowing this, come to stand in the True, Brahman?’ And: only some one is established in the True.

य एनं वेद तत्सत्यं प्राज्ञो भवति नित्यदा । दानमध्ययनं यज्ञो लोभादेव प्रवर्तते ॥ ३८ ॥
ya enaṃ veda tat satyaṃ prājño bhavati nityadā | dānam adhyayanaṃ yajño lobhād eva pravartate || 38 ||
Padaccheda
यः एनम् वेद तत् सत्यम् प्राज्ञः भवति नित्यदा दानम् अध्ययनम् यज्ञः लोभात् एव प्रवर्तते
yaḥ enam veda tat satyam prājñaḥ bhavati nityadā dānam adhyayanam yajñaḥ lobhāt eva pravartate
Word by word
He who knows this, that True, becomes wise for ever. (Of him who does not,) giving, study and sacrifice go on from greed alone.

Introduction Hear me further. He gives no gloss on the verse itself, and introduces the next: ‘Why? If one does not (know the True), hear what happens then.’

सत्यात्प्रच्यवमानानां संकल्पा वितथाभवन् । ततः कर्म प्रतायेत सत्यस्यानवधारणात् ॥ ३९ ॥
satyāt pracyavamānānāṃ saṃkalpā vitathābhavan | tataḥ karma pratāyeta satyasyānavadhāraṇāt || 39 ||
Padaccheda
सत्यात् प्रच्यवमानानाम् संकल्पाः वितथाः अभवन् ततः कर्म प्रतायेत सत्यस्य अनवधारणात्
satyāt pracyavamānānām saṃkalpāḥ vitathāḥ abhavan tataḥ karma pratāyeta satyasya anavadhāraṇāt
Word by word
The resolves of those who fall from the True became vain; then action is spread out, from not ascertaining the True.

Bhāṣya Satyāt: from Brahman, marked by truth, knowledge and the rest. Those who fall from it, giving up their natural being as Brahman and taking the non-self, the body and the rest, as the self: their resolves become vain, that is, their natural true resolve and the rest do not come about. Then action, sacrifice and the rest, is spread out. All this comes from not understanding the True, Brahman marked by truth and the rest, since saṃsāra is caused by ignorance of the Self: so long as one does not know the supreme Self directly as oneself, he, overcome by the three afflictions, dragged this way and that by passion and the rest as by crocodiles, deluded again and again, with resolves that are not true, busies himself with means to heaven, cattle, food and the like, which are all to be given up.

विद्याद्बहुपठं तं तु बहुवागिति ब्राह्मणम् । य एव सत्यान्नापैति स ज्ञेयो ब्राह्मणस्त्वया ॥ ४० ॥
vidyād bahupaṭhaṃ taṃ tu bahuvāg iti brāhmaṇam | ya eva satyān nāpaiti sa jñeyo brāhmaṇas tvayā || 40 ||
Padaccheda
विद्यात् बहु-पठम् तम् तु बहु-वाक् इति ब्राह्मणम् यः एव सत्यात् न अपैति सः ज्ञेयः ब्राह्मणः त्वया
vidyāt bahu-paṭham tam tu bahu-vāk iti brāhmaṇam yaḥ eva satyāt na apaiti saḥ jñeyaḥ brāhmaṇaḥ tvayā
Word by word
One should know him who reads much as ‘a man of many words’, (not) as a brāhmaṇa. He who does not depart from the True: he is to be known by you as a brāhmaṇa.

Introduction Now he gives the mark of a brāhmaṇa.

Bhāṣya Bahupaṭham: him who studies the Vedas with the narratives as fifth one should know as a man of many words, not as a brāhmaṇa in truth. Who then is the brāhmaṇa in the chief sense? He who does not depart from the True, marked by truth and the rest, who abides as the non-dual Brahman, the Self, whose own nature is consciousness, being and bliss: him alone you should know as a brāhmaṇa, not the other, who, fallen from the True and with his end not gained, engages in action. So the śruti shows that the knower of Brahman alone is a brāhmaṇa: ‘having done with silence and non-silence, then he is a brāhmaṇa’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 3.5.1), and ‘free of sin, free of stain, free of doubt, he becomes a brāhmaṇa’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.23).

छन्दांसि नाम द्विपदां वरिष्ठ स्वच्छन्दयोगेन भवन्ति तत्र । छन्दोविदस्तेन च तानधीत्य गता हि वेदस्य न वेद्यमार्याः ॥ ४१ ॥
chandāṃsi nāma dvipadāṃ variṣṭha svacchandayogena bhavanti tatra | chandovidas tena ca tān adhītya gatā hi vedasya na vedyam āryāḥ || 41 ||
Padaccheda
छन्दांसि नाम द्विपदाम् वरिष्ठ स्व-छन्द-योगेन भवन्ति तत्र छन्दः-विदः तेन च तान् अधीत्य गताः हि वेदस्य न वेद्यम् आर्याः
chandāṃsi nāma dvipadām variṣṭha sva-chanda-yogena bhavanti tatra chandaḥ-vidaḥ tena ca tān adhītya gatāḥ hi vedasya na vedyam āryāḥ
Word by word
The Vedas, O best of men, are of their own accord (a valid means of knowledge) there (in the supreme Self). And therefore the noble knowers of the Vedas, having studied them, have reached (the nature) of the Veda (the supreme Self), not the knowable.

Introduction ‘If it were so, Brahman would be established; but it is not, for the Veda is about something else.’ To this he says:

Bhāṣya Chandāṃsi: the Vedas. svacchandayogena: svacchandatā is independence, as they will. tatra: in the supreme Self; there alone they are a valid means of knowledge. So the śruti: ‘the word that all the Vedas declare’ (Kaṭha 1.2.15), and ‘by all the Vedas I alone am to be known’ (Gītā 15.15). For the Veda ends in the goal of man, and all else, pierced by impermanence, impurity and sorrow, is no goal of man; so the Vedas are valid in teaching its nature, the knowledge of it and the means to that.

Bhāṣya Because the Vedas are of their own accord valid there, in the supreme Self, therefore the noble, the learned, the knowers of Brahman, having studied the Vedas, that is, having heard the Vedānta and the rest, have reached the nature of the Veda, the supreme Self whose form is consciousness, not the knowable, the manifold world.

न वेदानां वेदिता कश्चिदस्ति वेदेन वेदं न विदुर्न वेद्यम् । यो वेद वेदं स च वेद वेद्यं यो वेद वेद्यं न स वेद सत्यम् ॥ ४२ ॥
na vedānāṃ veditā kaścid asti vedena vedaṃ na vidur na vedyam | yo veda vedaṃ sa ca veda vedyaṃ yo veda vedyaṃ na sa veda satyam || 42 ||
Padaccheda
न वेदानाम् वेदिता कश्चित् अस्ति वेदेन वेदम् न विदुः न वेद्यम् यः वेद वेदम् सः च वेद वेद्यम् यः वेद वेद्यम् न सः वेद सत्यम्
na vedānām veditā kaścit asti vedena vedam na viduḥ na vedyam yaḥ veda vedam saḥ ca veda vedyam yaḥ veda vedyam na saḥ veda satyam
Word by word
None of the Vedas is the knower (of the supreme Self). By the Veda (Ṛk and the rest) they do not know the Veda (the supreme Self whose form is consciousness), nor the knowable. He who knows the Veda knows the knowable too; he who knows the knowable does not know the True.

Introduction ‘If it is known by the Veda, that would conflict with śrutis such as “other than the known, and above the unknown” (Kena 1.4) and “whence words turn back, not reaching it, with the mind” (Taittirīya 2.4).’ To this doubt he says:

Bhāṣya Among the Vedas, the Ṛk and the rest, not one is the knower of the supreme Self, which is beyond the reach of words and whose form is consciousness. Why? Because by the Veda in the form of the Ṛk and the rest, which is insentient, they do not know the Veda, the supreme Self whose form is consciousness; nor do they know the knowable, the manifold, since everything is established only in dependence on consciousness. Because of that, he who knows the Veda, the supreme Self whose form is consciousness, knows the knowable too, all this; so the śruti: ‘by the seeing, hearing, thinking and knowing of the Self all this is known’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.4.5). He who knows the knowable as a form does not know the True, the supreme Self marked by truth and the rest.

यो वेद वेदान्स च वेद वेद्यं न तं विदुर्वेदविदो न वेदाः । तथापि वेदेन विदन्ति वेदं ये ब्राह्मणा वेदविदो भवन्ति ॥ ४३ ॥
yo veda vedān sa ca veda vedyaṃ na taṃ vidur vedavido na vedāḥ | tathāpi vedena vidanti vedaṃ ye brāhmaṇā vedavido bhavanti || 43 ||
Padaccheda
यः वेद वेदान् सः च वेद वेद्यम् न तम् विदुः वेद-विदः न वेदाः तथापि वेदेन विदन्ति वेदम् ये ब्राह्मणाः वेद-विदः भवन्ति
yaḥ veda vedān saḥ ca veda vedyam na tam viduḥ veda-vidaḥ na vedāḥ tathāpi vedena vidanti vedam ye brāhmaṇāḥ veda-vidaḥ bhavanti
Word by word
He who knows the Vedas (the Ṛk and the rest) knows the knowable (only). The knowers of the Veda do not know Him, nor do the Vedas. Even so, by the Veda they know the Veda (the supreme Self): those brāhmaṇas who are knowers of the Veda (who know how the Vedas teach).

Introduction ‘Then, in saying “by the Veda they know neither the Veda nor the knowable”, it is said that for one who does not know the Self the manifold is not established at all.’ To this doubt he says:

Bhāṣya He who knows the Vedas, the Ṛk and the rest, he too, not knowing the Self, knows the knowable, the manifold, by a knowing distinct from it. Then would he know the supreme Self too, like the knowable? No: the knowers of the Veda do not know Him, who is beyond the reach of words; nor do the Vedas: they do not make Him their object. Then how is Brahman taught in the Upaniṣads? Though Brahman is not the object of speech and the rest, still by the Veda, the Ṛk and the rest, they know the Veda, the supreme Self whose form is consciousness. Who? Those brāhmaṇas who are knowers of the Veda, who know the way in which the Vedas teach the Veda.

यामांशभागस्य तथा हि वेदा यथा हि शाखा च महीरुहस्य । संवेदनेऽप्येवमथामनन्ति तस्मिन्हि नित्ये परमात्मनोऽर्थे ॥ ४४ ॥
yāmāṃśabhāgasya tathā hi vedā yathā hi śākhā ca mahīruhasya | saṃvedane 'py evam athāmananti tasmin hi nitye paramātmano 'rthe || 44 ||
Padaccheda
याम-अंश-भागस्य तथा हि वेदाः यथा हि शाखा च महीरुहस्य संवेदने अपि एवम् अथ आमनन्ति तस्मिन् हि नित्ये परम-आत्मनः अर्थे
yāma-aṃśa-bhāgasya tathā hi vedāḥ yathā hi śākhā ca mahīruhasya saṃvedane api evam atha āmananti tasmin hi nitye parama-ātmanaḥ arthe
Word by word
As the branch of a tree (is a means to seeing) the part of the moon's portion, so indeed are the Vedas (a means) for the consciousness of the supreme Self, for that eternal object: so they declare.

Introduction How then do the Vedas teach Brahman, which is no object at all?

Bhāṣya Yāmāṃśabhāgasya: of the part of the moon's portion, yāma standing for the moon by the śruti ‘the moon is triyāma’. As, in seeing the digit of the moon on the first day of the bright fortnight, the branch of a tree is the means, so the Vedas are means to the consciousness that is the very nature of the supreme Self, to the eternal, imperishable object (artha) that is the highest goal of man, whose form is full bliss; they do not teach directly the supreme Self, which is beyond the reach of words: so (the knowers) declare.

अभिजानामि ब्राह्मणमाख्यातारं विचक्षणम् । एवं योऽभिविजानाति स जानाति परं हि तत् ॥ ४५ ॥
abhijānāmi brāhmaṇam ākhyātāraṃ vicakṣaṇam | evaṃ yo 'bhivijānāti sa jānāti paraṃ hi tat || 45 ||
Padaccheda
अभिजानामि ब्राह्मणम् आख्यातारम् विचक्षणम् एवम् यः अभिविजानाति सः जानाति परम् हि तत्
abhijānāmi brāhmaṇam ākhyātāram vicakṣaṇam evam yaḥ abhivijānāti saḥ jānāti param hi tat
Word by word
I recognize as a brāhmaṇa the discerning expounder (of the way the Vedas teach). He who knows this thoroughly knows that supreme (Brahman).

Introduction He who, having understood the way in which the Vedas teach the Self whose form is the Veda, explains it: he too is a brāhmaṇa.

Bhāṣya I recognize as a brāhmaṇa the discerning expounder who explains the way the Veda teaches. Objection: the śruti calls him a brāhmaṇa who abides having done with learning, childlikeness and the rest (‘then he is a brāhmaṇa’, Bṛhadāraṇyaka 3.5.1); how then is it said, ‘I recognize as a brāhmaṇa the expounder’? Answer: he who thoroughly knows the way, told by me, in which the Vedas teach the Self whose form is knowing, knows that supreme Brahman. For he who abides having done with learning soon does with childlikeness and the rest, and becomes a brāhmaṇa.

नास्य पर्येषणं गच्छेत्प्रत्यर्थिषु कदाचन । अविचिन्वन्निमं वेदे ततः पश्यति तं प्रभुम् ॥ ४६ ॥
nāsya paryeṣaṇaṃ gacchet pratyarthiṣu kadācana | avicinvann imaṃ vede tataḥ paśyati taṃ prabhum || 46 ||
Padaccheda
न अस्य पर्येषणम् गच्छेत् प्रत्यर्थिषु कदाचन अविचिन्वन् इमम् वेदे ततः पश्यति तम् प्रभुम्
na asya paryeṣaṇam gacchet pratyarthiṣu kadācana avicinvan imam vede tataḥ paśyati tam prabhum
Word by word
One should never go searching (for the Self) among its adversaries. Not gathering (objects), (seeking) this (inner Self) in the Veda, he then sees that Lord.

Introduction Because only one devoted to the True is known as a brāhmaṇa, one should not be given to objects.

Bhāṣya In the light of the verse ‘objects, the senses, the body and the ego are the fierce enemies of the yogin, outer and inner’, one should not go searching about (pari-eṣaṇa) among the adversaries of this self, the body, the senses and their objects such as sound; that is, one should not be bent on seeking objects. avicinvan: not amassing objects; imam: this inner Self, in the Veda, in the Upaniṣads, in sentences such as ‘that thou art’; then, afterwards, he sees that Lord, the supreme Self: he knows Him as the Self.

Bhāṣya A second explanation: one should not search for the self (asya) in its adversaries, the body, the senses and the rest, that is, should not take the body, the senses and their properties as the self. Not gathering them as the self, taking the witness of them alone as the Self, after the meanings of the words ‘that’ and ‘thou’ have been purified, he sees this supreme Self, the witness of the knower and the rest: not taking body, senses and their properties as the self, he sees by sentences such as ‘that thou art’ the supreme Self as the Self.

तूष्णींभूत उपासीत न चेच्छेन्मनसा अपि । अभ्यावर्तेत ब्रह्मास्मै बह्वनन्तरमाप्नुयात् ॥ ४७ ॥
tūṣṇīṃbhūta upāsīta na cecchen manasā api | abhyāvarteta brahmāsmai bahv anantaram āpnuyāt || 47 ||
Padaccheda
तूष्णीम्-भूतः उपासीत न च इच्छेत् मनसा अपि अभ्यावर्तेत ब्रह्म अस्मै बहु अनन्तरम् आप्नुयात्
tūṣṇīm-bhūtaḥ upāsīta na ca icchet manasā api abhyāvarteta brahma asmai bahu anantaram āpnuyāt
Word by word
Become silent, one should worship (one's own Self), and not desire (objects) even with the mind. Brahman would turn towards him; thereupon he would reach the much (the plenitude, the supreme Self).

Bhāṣya Because the seeing of the Self comes about only by giving up all objects, therefore, become silent, having renounced all works, having given up all but one's own Self, become alone, one should worship one's own Self alone as the world; and should not desire even with the mind: should make no desire for objects. To him, the brāhmaṇa who, become silent, has withdrawn from objects and worships his own Self as the world, Brahman, marked as ‘without before’ and the rest (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.5.19), would turn towards him, would come face to face. So the śruti: ‘whom he chooses, by him he is gained; to him this Self reveals its own form’ (Kaṭha 1.2.23). Thereupon, his own form made manifest, he would reach the much (bahu), the plenitude (bhūman), the supreme Self beyond darkness.

मौनाद्धि मुनिर्भवति नारण्यवसनान्मुनिः । अक्षरं तं तु यो वेद स मुनिश्रेष्ठ उच्यते ॥ ४८ ॥
maunād dhi munir bhavati nāraṇyavasanān muniḥ | akṣaraṃ taṃ tu yo veda sa muniśreṣṭha ucyate || 48 ||
Padaccheda
मौनात् हि मुनिः भवति न अरण्य-वसनात् मुनिः अक्षरम् तम् तु यः वेद सः मुनि-श्रेष्ठः उच्यते
maunāt hi muniḥ bhavati na araṇya-vasanāt muniḥ akṣaram tam tu yaḥ veda saḥ muni-śreṣṭhaḥ ucyate
Word by word
By silence indeed one becomes a sage, not by dwelling in the forest. But he who knows that imperishable (supreme Self) is called the best of sages.

Introduction He alone is a muni too.

Bhāṣya Maunāt: by the being silent told before (47), not merely by dwelling in the forest, does one become a sage. Among those who have become silent, he who knows that imperishable, the supreme Self, directly as ‘I am this’, is called the best of sages. So the śruti: ‘knowing this very one he becomes a sage’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.22). akṣaraṃ tam: ‘that imperishable, the supreme Self’ (masculine tam by the sense).

Note The edition: the reading sa (‘he’) in the second half is doubtful in the print; the transcript's reading is kept.

sarvārthānāṃ vyākaraṇād vaiyākaraṇa ucyate | tanmūlato vyākaraṇaṃ vyākarotīti tat tathā || 49 ||
Padaccheda
सर्व-अर्थानाम् व्याकरणात् वैयाकरणः उच्यते तत्-मूलतः व्याकरणम् व्याकरोति इति तत् तथा
sarva-arthānām vyākaraṇāt vaiyākaraṇaḥ ucyate tat-mūlataḥ vyākaraṇam vyākaroti iti tat tathā
Word by word
One is called a ‘grammarian’ (vaiyākaraṇa) from making distinct all things. From that (the Imperishable) as the root is the making distinct (of name and form); he (the knower) makes that (Brahman) distinct in that way: so (he is a grammarian).

Introduction He alone is a grammarian too.

Bhāṣya One is called a grammarian (vaiyākaraṇa) from making distinct (vyākaraṇa) all things, not from analysing some part of words. Granted; what follows? tanmūlato vyākaraṇam: from the Imperishable named before (48) comes the making distinct of the whole manifold of name and form; so the śruti: ‘entering with this living self, let me make distinct name and form’ (Chāndogya 6.3.2). Hence Brahman alone is directly the ‘grammarian’. vyākarotīti tat tathā: this knower too makes that Brahman distinct in just that way, and so is a grammarian.

प्रत्यक्षदर्शी लोकानां सर्वदर्शी भवेन्नरः । सत्ये वै ब्रह्मणि तिष्ठंस्तद्विद्वान्सर्वविद्भवेत् ॥ ५० ॥
pratyakṣadarśī lokānāṃ sarvadarśī bhaven naraḥ | satye vai brahmaṇi tiṣṭhaṃs tad vidvān sarvavid bhavet || 50 ||
Padaccheda
प्रत्यक्ष-दर्शी लोकानाम् सर्व-दर्शी भवेत् नरः सत्ये वै ब्रह्मणि तिष्ठन् तत् विद्वान् सर्व-वित् भवेत्
pratyakṣa-darśī lokānām sarva-darśī bhavet naraḥ satye vai brahmaṇi tiṣṭhan tat vidvān sarva-vit bhavet
Word by word
The man who sees the worlds directly would be ‘all-seeing’ (only if he sees the supreme Self, which is all). Abiding in the True, in Brahman, knowing That (as the Self), he would be all-knowing.

Introduction He alone is all-knowing too.

Bhāṣya He who sees directly the worlds, Bhūḥ and the rest, would be the all-seeing man: he sees the supreme Self whose form is all. And he, abiding in the True, in Brahman marked by truth and the rest, fixes his mind there; knowing that Brahman as the Self, he becomes all-knowing, knows all. So he alone is directly omniscient, not one who sees only the non-self.

ज्ञानादिषु स्थितोऽप्येवं क्षत्रिय ब्रह्म पश्यति । वेदानां चारपूर्वेण चैतद्विद्वन्ब्रवीमि ते ॥ ५१ ॥
jñānādiṣu sthito 'py evaṃ kṣatriya brahma paśyati | vedānāṃ cārapūrveṇa caitad vidvan bravīmi te || 51 ||
Padaccheda
ज्ञान-आदिषु स्थितः अपि एवम् क्षत्रिय ब्रह्म पश्यति वेदानाम् चार-पूर्वेण च एतत् विद्वन् ब्रवीमि ते
jñāna-ādiṣu sthitaḥ api evam kṣatriya brahma paśyati vedānām cāra-pūrveṇa ca etat vidvan bravīmi te
Word by word
Abiding in knowledge and the rest too, O warrior, one sees Brahman in this way, by first going through the Vedas (by first hearing the Vedānta); and this, O learned one, I tell you.

Introduction The sense told at ‘he who does not leave these (twelve)’ (2.20) he shows again, to show that it must be done.

Bhāṣya Jñānādiṣu: in knowledge and the rest, told before at ‘knowledge and truth’ (2.19); abiding in them too, one sees Brahman in the same way as one abiding in the True sees Brahman (50). vedānāṃ cārapūrveṇa: preceded by the hearing of the Vedānta.

Bhāṣya A second explanation: this lays down a further quality. Even abiding in knowledge and the rest, one does not see Brahman by that alone, but sees it only in the way about to be told, preceded by inquiry into the Vedānta, by hearing the Vedānta and the rest. etat: this manner of inquiring into the Vedānta, O learned one, I tell you, that is, I shall tell you.

तृतीयोऽध्यायः

धृतराष्ट्र उवाचdhṛtarāṣṭra uvāca
सनत्सुजात यदिमां परार्थां ब्राह्मीं वाचं वदसि हि विश्वरूपाम् । परां हि कार्येषु सुदुर्लभां कथां प्रब्रूहि मे वाक्यमेवं कुमार ॥ १ ॥

Padaccheda

Sanatsujāta, since you speak this speech concerning Brahman, of the highest meaning and of many forms, a supreme discourse, very hard to come by (even to hear) in the manifold world, tell me words of this kind, O youth (for you alone are fit to tell them).

Introduction Introducing the adhyāya: now, after the means, brahmacarya and the rest, the third and fourth adhyāyas begin, to set forth Brahman, which is reached by those means. Having heard of the means, brahmacarya and the rest, and longing to know Brahman, Dhṛtarāṣṭra speaks.

Bhāṣya Yat = yasmāt, since; parārthām = of excellent meaning; brāhmīm = connected with Brahman; viśvarūpām = of many forms; kāryeṣu = among the classes of effects, the manifold world; sudurlabhām = not possible even to hear. Since you speak such speech about Brahman, the means to the highest human end, you alone are fit to speak it.

सनत्सुजात उवाचsanatsujāta uvāca

naitad brahma tvaramāṇena labhyaṃ yan māṃ pṛcchasy abhiṣaṅgeṇa rājan | buddhau pralīne manasi pracintyā vidyā hi sā brahmacaryeṇa labhyā || 2 ||
Padaccheda
न एतत् ब्रह्म त्वरमाणेन लभ्यम् यत् माम् पृच्छसि अभिषङ्गेण राजन् बुद्धौ प्रलीने मनसि प्रचिन्त्या विद्या हि सा ब्रह्मचर्येण लभ्या

This Brahman, about which you ask me so eagerly, O king, is not gained by one who is in haste. When the mind is dissolved in the understanding, that knowledge is to be contemplated; it is gained by brahmacarya (as it will be described).

Bhāṣya Buddhau = in the understanding, whose nature is resolve (adhyavasāya); pralīne manasi = when the mind, whose nature is intention and doubt (saṃkalpa-vikalpa), turns back from objects and becomes still in its own self. The knowledge to be contemplated then is gained by the brahmacarya about to be described.

ādyāṃ vidyāṃ vadasi hi satyarūpāṃ yā prāpyate brahmacaryeṇa sadbhiḥ | yāṃ prāpyainaṃ martyabhāvaṃ tyajanti yā vai vidyā guruvṛddheṣu nityā || 3 ||
Padaccheda
आद्याम् विद्याम् वदसि हि सत्य-रूपाम् या प्राप्यते ब्रह्मचर्येण सद्भिः याम् प्राप्य एनम् मर्त्य-भावम् त्यजन्ति या वै विद्या गुरु-वृद्धेषु नित्या

You speak of (and ask me to tell) the first knowledge, whose form is truth, which is attained by the good through brahmacarya, attaining which they abandon this mortal state, the knowledge that is constant in those grown (in it) through the teacher.

Bhāṣya Ādyām = whose object is Brahman, the first of all; satyarūpām = whose form is the highest truth: ‘tell me’ (you say). Or else: ādyām = not an effect, not having the unreal world for its object. Therefore it is gained only by one who is not in haste, who has the means, brahmacarya and the rest, and whose inner organ is withdrawn. guruvṛddheṣu = in disciples grown by the teacher through the giving of knowledge and the rest; nityā = fixed, certain.

The knowledge that can be known directly through brahmacarya: how is that brahmacarya (to be practised)? Tell me this, O learned one.

Introduction When this was said, Dhṛtarāṣṭra speaks in order to learn what brahmacarya is. He does not comment on the verse itself.

Those who, entering here the womb that is the teacher and becoming an embryo (his disciples), practise brahmacarya, become even here makers of treatises; leaving the body, they go to the highest, the True.

Then, weary of childhood and the rest, they become brāhmaṇas, and when the karma that has begun to bear fruit is spent they leave the body and attain the True, the supreme Self, marked by truth and the rest.

In this world they conquer desires here, holding patiently, day after day, to the standing in Brahman; by steadfastness they draw out the Self here from the body, like the inner stalk from muñja grass.

Bhāṣya Brāhmīṃ sthitim = the standing in Brahman alone; anutitikṣamāṇāḥ = bearing it day after day; ātmānam nirharanti = drawing out from body, senses and the rest their witness, pure consciousness, they set it apart. As one draws out the stalk that lies within the muñja grass, so, drawing it out from the five sheaths, they attain the Self of all; dhīrabhāvāt = by firmness. He cites Kaṭha 2.3.17 (‘the Puruṣa, the size of a thumb … one should draw him out from one’s own body with firmness, as the stalk from the muñja grass’).

शरीरमेतौ कुरुतः पिता माता च भारत । आचार्यतस्तु यज्जन्म तत्सत्यं वै तथामृतम् ॥ ७ ॥

Word by word
These two, father and mother, make the body, O Bhārata; but the birth that is from the teacher, that is true, and deathless too.

Introduction In ‘ācāryayonim iha’ (verse 5) the teacher was shown to be a womb. How can the teacher, apart from mother and father, be a womb? To this doubt he says that the teacher alone is truly the begetter.

Bhāṣya Mother and father make the body; they do not beget the self in its own nature. The birth as the two bodies is untrue; the birth from the teacher, as Brahman, being, consciousness and bliss without a second, is true, real, and deathless, free from perishing. So he alone is the begetter. He cites Praśna 6.8 (‘you are our father, who take us across to the far shore of ignorance’) and Āpastamba (‘he begets him through knowledge; that is the best birth; mother and father beget the body only’).

saḥ āvṛṇoti amṛtam saṃprayacchan tasmai na druhyet kṛtam asya jānan gurum śiṣyaḥ nityam abhivādayīta svādhyāyam icchet ca sadā apramattaḥ
Word by word
He (the teacher) fills (the disciple) with the deathless, bestowing it (as his Self); knowing what he has done (for him), one should not wrong him. The disciple should always salute the teacher (and worship him as a god), and should desire study of scripture (hearing and the rest), ever heedful.

Introduction Since the attaining of the highest human end depends on the teacher, therefore (the verse follows).

Bhāṣya Āvṛṇoti = fills; amṛtam = Brahman, full bliss, which he bestows as the disciple’s Self; tasmai na druhyet = one should do him no wrong. He cites Śvetāśvatara 6.23 (‘to the great soul who has supreme devotion to God, and to the teacher as to God, these things, when told, shine forth’) and Āpastamba (‘one should never wrong him, for he begets him through knowledge’). kṛtam asya jānan: the genitive asya stands for the instrumental: knowing the help done to oneself by him. abhivādayīta: one should worship the teacher as a god; svādhyāyam icchet = one should be intent on hearing and the rest; apramattaḥ = free from heedlessness.

Padaccheda
शिष्य-वृत्ति-क्रमेण एव विद्याम् आप्नोति यः शुचिः ब्रह्मचर्य-व्रतस्य अस्य प्रथमः पादः उच्यते

Word by word
That one who is pure gains knowledge only by the course of a disciple’s conduct (described from verse 5 on): this is called the first quarter of this vow of brahmacarya.

Introduction Introducing verses 9–12: now he states brahmacarya with four quarters, in four verses.

Bhāṣya Śiṣyavṛttikrameṇa = by the course stated from ‘ācāryayonim iha’ on; that the pure man gains knowledge by it is the first quarter of this brahmacarya.

यथा नित्यं गुरौ वृत्तिर्गुरुपत्न्यां तथाचरेत् । तत्पुत्रे च तथा कुर्वन्द्वितीयः पाद उच्यते ॥ १० ॥
yathā nityaṃ gurau vṛttir gurupatnyāṃ tathācaret | tatputre ca tathā kurvan dvitīyaḥ pāda ucyate || 10 ||
Padaccheda
यथा नित्यम् गुरौ वृत्तिः गुरु-पत्न्याम् तथा आचरेत् तत्-पुत्रे च तथा कुर्वन् द्वितीयः पादः उच्यते
yathā nityam gurau vṛttiḥ guru-patnyām tathā ācaret tat-putre ca tathā kurvan dvitīyaḥ pādaḥ ucyate
Word by word
As his conduct always is toward the teacher, so should he behave toward the teacher’s wife; doing so toward his son as well is called the second quarter.

Bhāṣya The verse is plain in meaning. He cites (Āpastamba) ‘conduct toward the teacher’s wives as toward the teacher’ and ‘conduct toward the teacher’s son as toward the teacher’.

आचार्येणात्मकृतं विजानञ्ज्ञात्वा चार्थं भावितोऽस्मीत्यनेन । यन्मन्यते तं प्रति हृष्टबुद्धिः स वै तृतीयो ब्रह्मचर्यस्य पादः ॥ ११ ॥
ācāryeṇātmakṛtaṃ vijānañ jñātvā cārthaṃ bhāvito 'smīty anena | yan manyate taṃ prati hṛṣṭabuddhiḥ sa vai tṛtīyo brahmacaryasya pādaḥ || 11 ||
Padaccheda
आचार्येण आत्म-कृतम् विजानन् ज्ञात्वा च अर्थम् भावितः अस्मि इति अनेन यत् मन्यते तम् प्रति हृष्ट-बुद्धिः सः वै तृतीयः ब्रह्मचर्यस्य पादः
ācāryeṇa ātma-kṛtam vijānan jñātvā ca artham bhāvitaḥ asmi iti anena yat manyate tam prati hṛṣṭa-buddhiḥ saḥ vai tṛtīyaḥ brahmacaryasya pādaḥ
Word by word
Recognizing what the teacher has done for him, and having understood the meaning (of the Veda), he thinks, ‘I have been brought into being by him’, and, glad in mind toward him, (holds himself to have gained his end): that is the third quarter of brahmacarya.

Bhāṣya Ātmakṛtam = the help done to oneself; artham = the meaning of the Veda, the highest human end; bhāvito ’smy anena = ‘I have been brought forth by him, rightly, as the Brahman that is my own nature, being, consciousness and bliss without a second’: so reflecting, glad in mind toward the teacher, he considers that he has gained his end (kṛtārthatva). That is the third quarter.

आचार्याय प्रियं कुर्यात्प्राणैरपि धनैरपि । कर्मणा मनसा वाचा चतुर्थः पाद उच्यते ॥ १२ ॥
ācāryāya priyaṃ kuryāt prāṇair api dhanair api | karmaṇā manasā vācā caturthaḥ pāda ucyate || 12 ||
Padaccheda
आचार्याय प्रियम् कुर्यात् प्राणैः अपि धनैः अपि कर्मणा मनसा वाचा चतुर्थः पादः उच्यते
ācāryāya priyam kuryāt prāṇaiḥ api dhanaiḥ api karmaṇā manasā vācā caturthaḥ pādaḥ ucyate
Word by word
He should do what is pleasing to the teacher, even with his life, even with his wealth, by deed, mind and speech: this is called the fourth quarter.

Bhāṣya Śaṅkara does not comment on this verse; it is the last of the four introduced before verse 9.

कालेन पादं लभते तथार्यं तथैव पादं गुरुयोगतश्च । उत्साहयोगेन च पादमृच्छेच्छास्त्रेण पादं च ततोऽभियाति ॥ १३ ॥
kālena pādaṃ labhate tathāryaṃ tathaiva pādaṃ guruyogataś ca | utsāhayogena ca pādam ṛcchec chāstreṇa pādaṃ ca tato 'bhiyāti || 13 ||
Padaccheda
कालेन पादम् लभते तथा आर्यम् तथा एव पादम् गुरु-योगतः च उत्साह-योगेन च पादम् ऋच्छेत् शास्त्रेण पादम् च ततः अभियाति
kālena pādam labhate tathā āryam tathā eva pādam guru-yogataḥ ca utsāha-yogena ca pādam ṛcchet śāstreṇa pādam ca tataḥ abhiyāti
Word by word
By time he gains a noble quarter (of knowledge), and a quarter likewise from union with the teacher; by applied energy he reaches a quarter, and then by the teaching (enquired into with fellow students) he reaches a quarter.

Introduction Now he shows knowledge (vidyā) in four quarters.

Bhāṣya No order is meant here. First (a quarter) from union with the teacher; then by applied energy, that is, by the rise of a special understanding; then by time, the ripening of understanding; then by the teaching, enquiry into the truth with fellow students. He cites a verse: ‘a quarter the disciple takes from the teacher, a quarter by his own intelligence, a quarter by time, a quarter with fellow students’.

Bhāṣya Śaṅkara does not gloss tathāryam; it is read here as tathā āryam, ‘a noble (quarter)’.

ज्ञानादयो द्वादश यस्य रूपमन्यानि चाङ्गानि तथा बलं च । आचार्ययोगे फलतीति चाहुर्ब्रह्मार्थयोगेन च ब्रह्मचर्यम् ॥ १४ ॥
jñānādayo dvādaśa yasya rūpam anyāni cāṅgāni tathā balaṃ ca | ācāryayoge phalatīti cāhur brahmārthayogena ca brahmacaryam || 14 ||
Padaccheda
ज्ञान-आदयः द्वादश यस्य रूपम् अन्यानि च अङ्गानि तथा बलम् च आचार्य-योगे फलति इति च आहुः ब्रह्म-अर्थ-योगेन च ब्रह्मचर्यम्
jñāna-ādayaḥ dvādaśa yasya rūpam anyāni ca aṅgāni tathā balam ca ācārya-yoge phalati iti ca āhuḥ brahma-artha-yogena ca brahmacaryam
Word by word
(The man) whose form is knowledge and the other twelve (virtues), the other limbs too, and the strength (to keep them): (all this) bears fruit (only) in union with the teacher, they say; and brahmacarya (bears fruit) by its joining to the purpose of Brahman.

Introduction Knowledge and the rest bear fruit in the presence of the teacher.

Bhāṣya Jñānādayaḥ = the twelve virtues stated before, beginning ‘jñānaṃ ca’ (2.19), of the man whose form they are; the other limbs = those stated in the two verses ‘śreyāṃs tu ṣaḍvidhas tyāgaḥ’ (2.25) and ‘satyaṃ dhyānam’ (2.28); balam = the power to maintain those dharmas. All this bears fruit only in union with the teacher, not without it. He cites Chāndogya 4.9.3 (‘knowledge learned from a teacher’) and 6.14.2 (‘a man who has a teacher knows’). brahmacaryam: the service and the rest done in the teacher’s presence bears fruit by joining to the purpose of Brahman, that is, by bringing about the oneness of one’s own Self with Brahman, being, consciousness and bliss without a second.

एतेन ब्रह्मचर्येण देवा देवत्वमाप्नुवन् । ऋषयश्च महाभागा ब्रह्मचर्येण चाभवन् ॥ १५ ॥
etena brahmacaryeṇa devā devatvam āpnuvan | ṛṣayaś ca mahābhāgā brahmacaryeṇa cābhavan || 15 ||
Padaccheda
एतेन ब्रह्मचर्येण देवाः देवत्वम् आप्नुवन् ऋषयः च महा-भागाः ब्रह्मचर्येण च अभवन्
etena brahmacaryeṇa devāḥ devatvam āpnuvan ṛṣayaḥ ca mahā-bhāgāḥ brahmacaryeṇa ca abhavan
Word by word
By this brahmacarya the gods gained godhood, and by brahmacarya the greatly blessed seers became (seers).

Introduction Introducing verses 15–16: he praises brahmacarya in two verses.

Bhāṣya The gods gained godhood by it; the seers too gained seerhood by it.

एतेनैव सगन्धर्वा रूपमप्सरसोऽजयन् । एतेन ब्रह्मचर्येण सूर्य अह्नाय जायते ॥ १६ ॥
etenaiva sagandharvā rūpam apsaraso 'jayan | etena brahmacaryeṇa sūrya ahnāya jāyate || 16 ||
Padaccheda
एतेन एव स-गन्धर्वाः रूपम् अप्सरसः अजयन् एतेन ब्रह्मचर्येण सूर्यः अह्नाय जायते
etena eva sa-gandharvāḥ rūpam apsarasaḥ ajayan etena brahmacaryeṇa sūryaḥ ahnāya jāyate
Word by word
By this very (brahmacarya) the Apsarases, with the Gandharvas, won beauty; by this brahmacarya the sun comes into being, to light (the worlds).

Bhāṣya Sagandharvāḥ = together with the Gandharvas; rūpam = lovely forms, which they won by this brahmacarya. ahna = a mass of radiance; ahnāya = to illuminate the worlds, the sun comes into being. He cites ‘ahna is also called radiance’.

आकाङ्क्षार्थस्य संयोगाद्रसभेदार्थिनामिव । एवं ह्येतत्समाज्ञाय तादृग्भावं गता इमे ॥ १७ ॥
ākāṅkṣārthasya saṃyogād rasabhedārthinām iva | evaṃ hy etat samājñāya tādṛgbhāvaṃ gatā ime || 17 ||
Padaccheda
आकाङ्क्षा-अर्थस्य संयोगात् रस-भेद-अर्थिनाम् इव एवम् हि एतत् समाज्ञाय तादृक्-भावम् गताः इमे
ākāṅkṣā-arthasya saṃyogāt rasa-bheda-arthinām iva evam hi etat samājñāya tādṛk-bhāvam gatāḥ ime
Word by word
As (wish-fulfilling gems give) to those who seek different flavours (each what he seeks), through union with the thing desired, (so brahmacarya gives each his desire); understanding this thus, these (gods and the rest) have reached such a state.

Introduction How does the one brahmacarya bring about fruits of many kinds?

Bhāṣya As the wish-fulfilling gem and the like give to those who seek different flavours the very thing each desires, through union with the thing desired, so this brahmacarya gives whatever is desired. Knowing this, and practising brahmacarya for this fruit or that, these, the gods and the rest, reached such a state. Since the highest human end comes from brahmacarya practised in the teacher’s presence, one should enter the womb that is the teacher, become an embryo, and practise brahmacarya.

अन्तवन्तः क्षत्रिय ते जयन्ति लोकाञ्जनाः कर्मणा निर्मितेन । ज्ञानेन विद्वांस्तेज अभ्येति नित्यं न विद्यते ह्यन्यथा तस्य पन्थाः ॥ १८ ॥
antavantaḥ kṣatriya te jayanti lokāñ janāḥ karmaṇā nirmitena | jñānena vidvāṃs teja abhyeti nityaṃ na vidyate hy anyathā tasya panthāḥ || 18 ||
Padaccheda
अन्तवन्तः क्षत्रिय ते जयन्ति लोकान् जनाः कर्मणा निर्मितेन ज्ञानेन विद्वान् तेजः अभ्येति नित्यम् न विद्यते हि अन्यथा तस्य पन्थाः
antavantaḥ kṣatriya te jayanti lokān janāḥ karmaṇā nirmitena jñānena vidvān tejaḥ abhyeti nityam na vidyate hi anyathā tasya panthāḥ
Word by word
Worlds that have an end, O warrior, those men win by action they have wrought. By knowledge the knower reaches the eternal light; for there is no other path to it.

Introduction To the doubt that the human end comes not from steadfastness in knowledge but from action alone, he says this.

Bhāṣya Antavantaḥ, read with lokān = worlds that have an end, the worlds of the ancestors, of the gods and the rest; men do not win by action the endless world, the supreme Self that is their own Self. By knowledge, not by action, the knower reaches the eternal light, imperishable, his own Self. Why by knowledge alone? Because for that light of full bliss there is no path but knowledge. He cites Śvetāśvatara 3.8 (‘knowing him thus one goes beyond death; there is no other path for going’).

धृतराष्ट्र उवाचdhṛtarāṣṭra uvāca
आभाति शुक्लमिव लोहितमिवाथो कृष्णमथार्जुनं काद्रवं वा । यद्ब्राह्मणः पश्यति यत्र विद्वान्कथंरूपं तदमृतमक्षरं परम् ॥ १९ ॥
ābhāti śuklam iva lohitam ivātho kṛṣṇam athārjunaṃ kādravaṃ vā | yad brāhmaṇaḥ paśyati yatra vidvān kathaṃrūpaṃ tad amṛtam akṣaraṃ param || 19 ||
Padaccheda
आभाति शुक्लम् इव लोहितम् इव अथो कृष्णम् अथ अर्जुनम् काद्रवम् वा यत् ब्राह्मणः पश्यति यत्र विद्वान् कथं-रूपम् तत् अमृतम् अक्षरम् परम्
ābhāti śuklam iva lohitam iva atho kṛṣṇam atha arjunam kādravam vā yat brāhmaṇaḥ paśyati yatra vidvān kathaṃ-rūpam tat amṛtam akṣaram param
Word by word
Does it appear as if white, or as if red, or black, or silver-white, or tawny, that which the brāhmaṇa, the knower, sees, (and) where? Of what form is that deathless, imperishable, supreme (Brahman)?

Introduction What does the Brahman that the knower sees by knowledge appear like? Dhṛtarāṣṭra asks.

Bhāṣya Does the Brahman the knower sees by knowledge appear as if white, as if red, as if black, silver-white or tawny? In what place does it shine? Of what form is that deathless, imperishable, supreme Brahman?

सनत्सुजात उवाचsanatsujāta uvāca
नाभाति शुक्लमिव लोहितमिवाथो कृष्णमथार्जुनं वा काद्रवं वा । न पृथिव्यां तिष्ठति नान्तरिक्षे नैतत्समुद्रे सलिलं बिभर्ति ॥ २० ॥
nābhāti śuklam iva lohitam ivātho kṛṣṇam athārjunaṃ vā kādravaṃ vā | na pṛthivyāṃ tiṣṭhati nāntarikṣe naitat samudre salilaṃ bibharti || 20 ||
Padaccheda
न आभाति शुक्लम् इव लोहितम् इव अथो कृष्णम् अथ अर्जुनम् वा काद्रवम् वा न पृथिव्याम् तिष्ठति न अन्तरिक्षे न एतत् समुद्रे सलिलम् बिभर्ति
na ābhāti śuklam iva lohitam iva atho kṛṣṇam atha arjunam vā kādravam vā na pṛthivyām tiṣṭhati na antarikṣe na etat samudre salilam bibharti
Word by word
It does not appear as if white, or as if red, or black, or silver-white, or tawny. It does not stand on the earth, nor in the mid-region; it does not bear water in the ocean (a body made of the elements).

Bhāṣya Brahman does not appear in the form of white and the rest, because it is formless. He cites Śvetāśvatara 3.10 (‘what is higher than that is formless and free from ill’) and Kaṭha 1.3.15 (‘without sound, without touch, without form’). Nor does it stand on the earth or in the mid-region: scripture shows that it rests in nothing else, Chāndogya 7.24.1 (‘on what does it rest, sir?’ ‘On its own greatness’).

Bhāṣya Why does it not stand on the earth and the rest? Because it does not bear water in the ocean, that is, it bears no body made of the five elements. The word salila stands for the five elements, as ap does in ‘he first created the waters and placed his seed in them’ (Manu 1.8); and in Chāndogya 5.9.1 (‘in the fifth offering the waters come to be called man’) the waters themselves are called man. The sense: were Brahman within saṃsāra, it would, having entered saṃsāra, have colour and form like a pot and rest in something else; but this Brahman, having nothing before it and the rest, has not entered saṃsāra. Therefore it is without colour and form.

नैवर्क्षु तन्न यजुःषु नाप्यथर्वसु न दृश्यते वै विमलेषु सामसु । रथन्तरे बार्हते वापि राजन्महाव्रतस्यात्मनि दृश्यते तत् ॥ २१ ॥
naivarkṣu tan na yajuḥṣu nāpy atharvasu na dṛśyate vai vimaleṣu sāmasu | rathantare bārhate vāpi rājan mahāvratasyātmani dṛśyate tat || 21 ||
Padaccheda
न एव ऋक्षु तत् न यजुःषु न अपि अथर्वसु न दृश्यते वै विमलेषु सामसु रथन्तरे बार्हते वा अपि राजन् महाव्रतस्य आत्मनि दृश्यते तत्
na eva ṛkṣu tat na yajuḥṣu na api atharvasu na dṛśyate vai vimaleṣu sāmasu rathantare bārhate vā api rājan mahāvratasya ātmani dṛśyate tat
Word by word
It is not seen in the Ṛc verses, nor in the Yajus formulas, nor in the Atharvan hymns, nor in the stainless Sāman chants, nor even in the Rathantara or the Bṛhat chant, O king: it is seen in the self of one of great vows.

Introduction Then is it not perceived by anyone anywhere? He says this.

Bhāṣya Mahāvratasya = of one endowed with the virtues stated from ‘jñānaṃ ca satyaṃ ca’ to ‘mahāvratā dvādaśa brāhmaṇasya’ (2.19): in his self the supreme Brahman is seen. It is not established as ‘this’, like a pot, but in the self, as the Self.

अवारणीयं तमसः परस्तात्तदन्ततोऽभ्येति विनाशकाले । अणीयरूपं च तथाप्यणीयसां महत्स्वरूपं त्वपि पर्वतेभ्यः ॥ २२ ॥
avāraṇīyaṃ tamasaḥ parastāt tad antato 'bhyeti vināśakāle | aṇīyarūpaṃ ca tathāpy aṇīyasāṃ mahatsvarūpaṃ tv api parvatebhyaḥ || 22 ||
Padaccheda
अवारणीयम् तमसः परस्तात् तत् अन्ततः अभ्येति विनाश-काले अणीय-रूपम् च तथा अपि अणीयसाम् महत्-स्वरूपम् तु अपि पर्वतेभ्यः
avāraṇīyam tamasaḥ parastāt tat antataḥ abhyeti vināśa-kāle aṇīya-rūpam ca tathā api aṇīyasām mahat-svarūpam tu api parvatebhyaḥ
Word by word
(What is seen in the self of one of great vows is Brahman,) not to be obstructed, beyond darkness (ignorance); to it (the world) goes at the end, at the time of destruction. It is smaller in form than the smallest, and greater in its nature than the mountains.

Introduction Introducing verses 22–23: now he states its nature, the seeing of it and its fruit, in two verses.

Bhāṣya What is seen in the self of one of great vows is Brahman, not to be obstructed because it goes everywhere; tamasaḥ = ignorance, beyond which it is. tad antato ’bhyeti vināśakāle: to that Brahman the world goes at the end, at the time of destruction, the dissolution (pralaya). He cites Kaṭha 1.2.20 (‘smaller than the small, greater than the great’).

तदेतदह्ना संस्थितं भाति सर्वं तदात्मवित्पश्यति ज्ञानयोगात् । तस्मिञ्जगत्सर्वमिदं प्रतिष्ठितं य एतद्विदुरमृतास्ते भवन्ति ॥ २३ ॥
tad etad ahnā saṃsthitaṃ bhāti sarvaṃ tad ātmavit paśyati jñānayogāt | tasmiñ jagat sarvam idaṃ pratiṣṭhitaṃ ya etad vidur amṛtās te bhavanti || 23 ||
Padaccheda
तत् एतत् अह्ना संस्थितम् भाति सर्वम् तत् आत्म-वित् पश्यति ज्ञान-योगात् तस्मिन् जगत् सर्वम् इदम् प्रतिष्ठितम् ये एतत् विदुः अमृताः ते भवन्ति
tat etat ahnā saṃsthitam bhāti sarvam tat ātma-vit paśyati jñāna-yogāt tasmin jagat sarvam idam pratiṣṭhitam ye etat viduḥ amṛtāḥ te bhavanti

All this (world), set (in Brahman) by its light, shines (as Brahman). That the knower of the Self sees by the yoga of knowledge. In it this whole world is established. Those who know this become deathless.

अपानम् गिरति प्राणः प्राणम् गिरति चन्द्रमाः आदित्यः गिरते चन्द्रम् आदित्यम् गिरते परः योगिनः तम् प्रपश्यन्ति भगवन्तम् सनातनम्
apānam girati prāṇaḥ prāṇam girati candramāḥ ādityaḥ girate candram ādityam girate paraḥ yoginaḥ tam prapaśyanti bhagavantam sanātanam
Word by word
Prāṇa swallows apāna; the moon (the mind) swallows prāṇa; the sun (the understanding) swallows the moon; the supreme (Brahman) swallows the sun. Yogins (alone) see him, the eternal Lord.

Introduction He now shows yoga.

Bhāṣya Prāṇa swallows, withdraws, apāna. The moon, the mind, withdraws prāṇa: the moon is the deity of the mind, so the word ‘moon’ means the mind. The sun, the understanding, swallows that moon, the mind: the sun is the deity of the understanding. The supreme, the supreme Brahman, swallows that sun, the understanding. This is what is said: at the time of absorption (samādhi), having withdrawn apāna into prāṇa, prāṇa into the mind, the mind into the understanding and the understanding into the supreme Self, one abides as Brahman, one's own nature, consciousness, being and bliss without a second.

एकं पादं नोत्क्षिपति सलिलाद्धंस उच्चरन् । तं चेत्सततमुत्क्षिपेन्न मृत्युर्नामृतं भवेत् । योगिनस्तं प्रपश्यन्ति भगवन्तं सनातनम् ॥ ११ ॥
ekaṃ pādaṃ notkṣipati salilād dhaṃsa uccaran | taṃ cet satatam utkṣipen na mṛtyur nāmṛtaṃ bhavet | yoginas taṃ prapaśyanti bhagavantaṃ sanātanam || 11 ||
Padaccheda
एकम् पादम् न उत्क्षिपति सलिलात् हंसः उच्चरन् तम् चेत् सततम् उत्क्षिपेत् न मृत्युः न अमृतम् भवेत् योगिनः तम् प्रपश्यन्ति भगवन्तम् सनातनम्
ekam pādam na utkṣipati salilāt haṃsaḥ uccaran tam cet satatam utkṣipet na mṛtyuḥ na amṛtam bhavet yoginaḥ tam prapaśyanti bhagavantam sanātanam
Word by word
Rising from the water (saṃsāra), the swan (the supreme Self) does not lift out one foot (the jīva). If he were to lift out that ever-moving (foot), there would be neither death nor deathlessness. Yogins (alone) see him, the eternal Lord.

Introduction He now shows the supreme abiding as the jīva.

Bhāṣya Haṃsa is the supreme Self, because he destroys (hanti) ignorance and its effect. salilāt: from saṃsāra, made of the elements and their products. uccaran: moving above, staying outside saṃsāra. He does not lift out, withdraw, the one foot called the jīva: he stays as the counterpart in every form (‘rūpaṃ rūpaṃ pratirūpaḥ’; the Kaṭha: ‘ekas tathā sarvabhūtāntarātmā’). Why does he not lift it out? If he were to lift out that foot, the jīva, which goes on without break (satatam, santatayāyinam), that is, if he did not by his own māyā make himself into endless distinctions, prāṇa and the rest, enter them and abide as the jīva, there would be no death, no saṃsāra, the harm of birth, death and the rest, since there would be no jīva in saṃsāra; nor would there be deathlessness, liberation, since what had not entered could not be seen. For this very purpose the śruti shows the entering (‘rūpaṃ rūpam’). So the Atharvaṇa śruti gives the same verse (‘ekaṃ pādaṃ notkṣipati salilād dhaṃsa uccaran, sa ced utkṣipet pādaṃ na mṛtyur nāmṛtaṃ bhavet’) and ‘ekaṃ rūpaṃ bahudhā yaḥ karoti’. Him who abides as the jīva in the form of a foot and in three feet as Brahman, consciousness, being and bliss without a second, the supreme Self, yogins alone see.

अङ्गुष्ठमात्रः पुरुषोऽन्तरात्मा लिङ्गस्य योगेन स याति नित्यम् । तमीशमीड्यमनुकल्पमाद्यं पश्यन्ति मूढा न विराजमानम् । योगिनस्तं प्रपश्यन्ति भगवन्तं सनातनम् ॥ १२ ॥
aṅguṣṭhamātraḥ puruṣo 'ntarātmā liṅgasya yogena sa yāti nityam | tam īśam īḍyam anukalpam ādyaṃ paśyanti mūḍhā na virājamānam | yoginas taṃ prapaśyanti bhagavantaṃ sanātanam || 12 ||
Padaccheda
अङ्गुष्ठ-मात्रः पुरुषः अन्तरात्मा लिङ्गस्य योगेन सः याति नित्यम् तम् ईशम् ईड्यम् अनुकल्पम् आद्यम् पश्यन्ति मूढाः न विराजमानम् योगिनः तम् प्रपश्यन्ति भगवन्तम् सनातनम्
aṅguṣṭha-mātraḥ puruṣaḥ antarātmā liṅgasya yogena saḥ yāti nityam tam īśam īḍyam anukalpam ādyam paśyanti mūḍhāḥ na virājamānam yoginaḥ tam prapaśyanti bhagavantam sanātanam
Word by word
Puruṣa, the inner Self, the size of a thumb through union with the subtle body, ever goes (from birth to birth): (because) the deluded do not see him, the lord, to be praised, who enters all and shapes (it), the first, the shining. Yogins (alone) see him, the eternal Lord.

Introduction By what limiting adjunct, then, does the supreme abide as the foot? Asking this, he shows the jīva as the supreme itself with the subtle body (liṅga) as its adjunct.

Bhāṣya He alone, being, consciousness and bliss without a second, the inner Self of all beings, Puruṣa, the full, the supreme Self, through union with the subtle body becomes the size of a thumb, limited to that measure, and goes, transmigrates, ever. Why does he so transmigrate? Because the deluded, who do not discriminate and take the two bodies for the self, do not see him: the lord of all, to be praised, anukalpa (he enters all and shapes it by himself), the first (who was in the beginning), shining. Through not knowing the Self as Brahman they transmigrate. Him, not seeing whom they transmigrate, yogins alone see.

गूहन्ति सर्पा इव गह्वरेषु क्षयं नीत्वा स्वेन वृत्तेन मर्त्यान् । ते विप्रमुह्यन्ति जना विमूढास्तैर्दत्ता भोगा मोहयन्ते भवाय । योगिनस्तं प्रपश्यन्ति भगवन्तं सनातनम् ॥ १३ ॥
gūhanti sarpā iva gahvareṣu kṣayaṃ nītvā svena vṛttena martyān | te vipramuhyanti janā vimūḍhās tair dattā bhogā mohayante bhavāya | yoginas taṃ prapaśyanti bhagavantaṃ sanātanam || 13 ||
Padaccheda
गूहन्ति सर्पाः इव गह्वरेषु क्षयम् नीत्वा स्वेन वृत्तेन मर्त्यान् ते विप्रमुह्यन्ति जनाः विमूढाः तैः दत्ताः भोगाः मोहयन्ते भवाय योगिनः तम् प्रपश्यन्ति भगवन्तम् सनातनम्
gūhanti sarpāḥ iva gahvareṣu kṣayam nītvā svena vṛttena martyān te vipramuhyanti janāḥ vimūḍhāḥ taiḥ dattāḥ bhogāḥ mohayante bhavāya yoginaḥ tam prapaśyanti bhagavantam sanātanam
Word by word
Like snakes in their holes, (the senses) hide (themselves), having led mortals to destruction by their own conduct (giving the poison of objects). Those people, bewildered, are utterly deluded; the enjoyments given by them delude (men) for becoming (birth and death). Yogins (alone) see him, the eternal Lord.

Introduction He now shows that the senses and their objects are the cause of harm.

Bhāṣya As snakes come out of their holes, by their conduct, the giving of poison, lead mortals to destruction and then hide themselves in the holes, so the snakes that are the senses, lying in the ear and the rest, come out of them, by their own conduct, the giving of the poison of objects, lead mortals to destruction and hide themselves in the holes. Those people, overcome by the poison of objects, are utterly deluded: they know nothing beyond (the objects), as the śruti says, ‘yathā priyayā striyā saṃpariṣvaktaḥ’. The enjoyments given by the snakes of the senses, objects like poison, delude mortals: they are the cause of delusion again and again; and this deluding by objects is for bhava, the saṃsāra of womb, birth, old age and death. Him, the blameless, who shapes all, the first, not seeing whom they are deluded, blinded by the poison of objects, yogins alone see.

असाधना वापि ससाधना वा समानमेतद्दृश्यते मानुषेषु । समानमेतदमृतस्येतरस्य युक्तास्तत्र मध्व उत्सं समापुः । योगिनस्तं प्रपश्यन्ति भगवन्तं सनातनम् ॥ १४ ॥
asādhanā vāpi sasādhanā vā samānam etad dṛśyate mānuṣeṣu | samānam etad amṛtasyetarasya yuktās tatra madhva utsaṃ samāpuḥ | yoginas taṃ prapaśyanti bhagavantaṃ sanātanam || 14 ||
Padaccheda
असाधनाः वा अपि स-साधनाः वा समानम् एतत् दृश्यते मानुषेषु समानम् एतत् अमृतस्य इतरस्य युक्ताः तत्र मध्वः उत्सम् समापुः योगिनः तम् प्रपश्यन्ति भगवन्तम् सनातनम्
asādhanāḥ vā api sa-sādhanāḥ vā samānam etat dṛśyate mānuṣeṣu samānam etat amṛtasya itarasya yuktāḥ tatra madhvaḥ utsam samāpuḥ yoginaḥ tam prapaśyanti bhagavantam sanātanam
Word by word
Whether without the means or with the means, this (Self) is seen the same among men; this is the same for deathlessness and for the other (saṃsāra). Those joined (to the means) have reached there (in the supreme abode of Viṣṇu) the spring of honey (Brahman, the full bliss). Yogins (alone) see him, the eternal Lord.

Introduction He states what liberation is for the men of knowledge.

Bhāṣya In those without the means, without calm, restraint and the rest, and in those with them, the same, common nature of the Self is seen among men. It is the same too for deathlessness, liberation, and for the other, saṃsāra, whether (the means) are there or not. Among them, those who are joined to the means, calm, restraint and the rest, reach there, in the supreme abode of Viṣṇu, the spring of honey: they reach Brahman, the full bliss. The spring, the full bliss, which the joined reach, yogins alone see.

उभौ लोकौ विद्यया व्याप्य यान्ति तदाहुतं चाहुतमग्निहोत्रम् । मा ते ब्राह्मी लघुतामादधीत प्रज्ञानं स्यान्नाम धीरा लभन्ते । योगिनस्तं प्रपश्यन्ति भगवन्तं सनातनम् ॥ १५ ॥
ubhau lokau vidyayā vyāpya yānti tad āhutaṃ cāhutam agnihotram | mā te brāhmī laghutām ādadhīta prajñānaṃ syān nāma dhīrā labhante | yoginas taṃ prapaśyanti bhagavantaṃ sanātanam || 15 ||
Padaccheda
उभौ लोकौ विद्यया व्याप्य यान्ति तत् आहुतम् च अहुतम् अग्निहोत्रम् मा ते ब्राह्मी लघुताम् आदधीत प्रज्ञानम् स्यात् नाम धीराः लभन्ते योगिनः तम् प्रपश्यन्ति भगवन्तम् सनातनम्
ubhau lokau vidyayā vyāpya yānti tat āhutam ca ahutam agnihotram mā te brāhmī laghutām ādadhīta prajñānam syāt nāma dhīrāḥ labhante yoginaḥ tam prapaśyanti bhagavantam sanātanam
Word by word
Having pervaded both worlds by knowledge, they go to That; (therefore) the agnihotra not offered is offered. Your knowledge of Brahman will not bring lightness (mortality, as action does): it would be named ‘consciousness’ (Brahman); the wise gain it. Yogins (alone) see him, the eternal Lord.

Bhāṣya Ubhau lokau: this world and the other; pervading them by the knowledge whose object is Brahman as the Self, one goes to That, Brahman, the full bliss. Since one goes pervading both worlds by knowledge, the agnihotra not offered is offered by this knowledge of the Self, offered directly (ābhimukhyena): all the fruit of the agnihotra and the other rites is gained by this alone. Since one pervades both worlds by knowledge, and the agnihotra not offered is offered, therefore your knowledge whose object is Brahman does not bring lightness, mortality, as action does; rather it makes consciousness, the supreme Self beyond darkness, one's own self. When one engaged in the knowledge of Brahman knows the supreme Self as the self, the name ‘consciousness’ belongs to it: the name ‘Brahman’ (the śruti: ‘prajñānaṃ brahma’). That Brahman, consciousness, the wise, men of understanding, gain. Him, Brahman, consciousness, whom the wise gain, yogins alone see.

Bhāṣya Śaṅkara glosses the verse's yānti with a singular, yāti; and he reads tat as the goal (‘That, Brahman, the full bliss’), joining the agnihotra clause to it by ‘therefore’.

एवंरूपो महानात्मा पावकं पुरुषो गिरन् । यो वै तं पुरुषं वेद तस्येहात्मा न रिष्यते । योगिनस्तं प्रपश्यन्ति भगवन्तं सनातनम् ॥ १६ ॥
evaṃrūpo mahān ātmā pāvakaṃ puruṣo giran | yo vai taṃ puruṣaṃ veda tasyehātmā na riṣyate | yoginas taṃ prapaśyanti bhagavantaṃ sanātanam || 16 ||
Padaccheda
एवम्-रूपः महान् आत्मा पावकम् पुरुषः गिरन् यः वै तम् पुरुषम् वेद तस्य इह आत्मा न रिष्यते योगिनः तम् प्रपश्यन्ति भगवन्तम् सनातनम्
evam-rūpaḥ mahān ātmā pāvakam puruṣaḥ giran yaḥ vai tam puruṣam veda tasya iha ātmā na riṣyate yoginaḥ tam prapaśyanti bhagavantam sanātanam
Word by word
Of such a form (Brahman, consciousness alone), Puruṣa, swallowing the fire (the cause, with its effects), (becomes) the great Self. He who knows that Puruṣa, his self does not perish here. Yogins (alone) see him, the eternal Lord.

Bhāṣya He who abides of such a form, as Brahman whose only taste is consciousness, that self becomes great: becomes Brahman itself. pāvakam: the fire, the cause, whose form is to withdraw all; swallowing it, withdrawing into himself the effect with its cause. He who knows that Puruṣa, whose only taste is knowledge, the full, who dwells in the city (puriśaya), directly, as ‘this I am’: for him, as he knows the supreme Self, which is consciousness, as the self, the self does not perish here, in this very body; for the knower does not depart, and perishing is caused by departing. So the śruti shows, with a question, that he does not depart: ‘ud asmāt prāṇāḥ krāmanti āho neti neti hovāca yājñavalkyo 'traiva samavanīyante’, and ‘na tasya prāṇā utkrāmanti brahmaiva san brahmāpyeti ya evaṃ veda’. Him, knowing whom one does not perish, yogins alone see.

तस्मात्सदा सत्कृतः स्यान्न मृत्युरमृतं कुतः । सत्यानृते सत्यसमानुबन्धिनी सतश्च योनिरसतश्चैक एव । योगिनस्तं प्रपश्यन्ति भगवन्तं सनातनम् ॥ १७ ॥
tasmāt sadā satkṛtaḥ syān na mṛtyur amṛtaṃ kutaḥ | satyānṛte satyasamānubandhinī sataś ca yonir asataś caika eva | yoginas taṃ prapaśyanti bhagavantaṃ sanātanam || 17 ||
Padaccheda
तस्मात् सदा सत्-कृतः स्यात् न मृत्युः अमृतम् कुतः सत्य-अनृते सत्य-समानुबन्धिनी सतः च योनिः असतः च एकः एव योगिनः तम् प्रपश्यन्ति भगवन्तम् सनातनम्
tasmāt sadā sat-kṛtaḥ syāt na mṛtyuḥ amṛtam kutaḥ satya-anṛte satya-samānubandhinī sataḥ ca yoniḥ asataḥ ca ekaḥ eva yoginaḥ tam prapaśyanti bhagavantam sanātanam
Word by word
Therefore one should always be ‘honoured with being’ (think oneself Brahman); (for him) there is no death: whence deathlessness? Truth and untruth are bound to the true; the source of what is and of what is not is one alone. Yogins (alone) see him, the eternal Lord.

Introduction Since from knowing That alone there is no perishing of the self —

Bhāṣya Sadā: at all times, day and night. satkṛtaḥ syāt: he who thinks of himself as Brahman, being, consciousness and bliss without a second, is ever ‘made sat’; for him there is no death, no saṃsāra of birth and death. amṛtaṃ kutaḥ: deathlessness depends on death; where death is not, how could it arise? (the śruti: ‘mṛtyur nāsty amṛtaṃ kutaḥ’). Truth and untruth exist bound to the true: bound to the one substrate, the true in the highest sense, as the snake to the rope. How is this known? Because they declare the one Brahman without a second the source of the ‘what is’, the worldly, and of the ‘what is not’, the merely conventional, such as silver (seen in shell); therefore truth and untruth are bound to the true, their cause. Him, by the knowledge of whose truth as the self death is destroyed, to whom truth and untruth are bound, yogins alone see.

aṅguṣṭhamātraḥ puruṣo 'ntarātmā na dṛśyate 'sau hṛdaye niviṣṭaḥ | ajaś caro divārātram atandritaś ca sa taṃ matvā kavir āste prasannaḥ || 18 ||
Padaccheda
अङ्गुष्ठ-मात्रः पुरुषः अन्तरात्मा न दृश्यते असौ हृदये निविष्टः अजः चरः दिवारात्रम् अतन्द्रितः च सः तम् मत्वा कविः आस्ते प्रसन्नः
aṅguṣṭha-mātraḥ puruṣaḥ antarātmā na dṛśyate asau hṛdaye niviṣṭaḥ ajaḥ caraḥ divārātram atandritaḥ ca saḥ tam matvā kaviḥ āste prasannaḥ

Puruṣa, the inner Self, the size of a thumb, settled in the heart (having created the world), unborn, moving (the self of all that moves and does not move), is not seen (as he is). Unwearied day and night (coming out of the five sheaths), having known him, the wise man remains serene.

Bhāṣya Having created the world, from space down to the body, he has entered the heart; unborn, cara: the self of the moving and the unmoving; he is not seen in his own nature, consciousness, being and bliss without a second. Unwearied day and night, having come out of the five sheaths and known him, the inner Self of all, the wise man remains serene: he has gained his end.

Bhāṣya Śaṅkara joins divārātram atandritaḥ with the wise man, not with the Self. The verse has no refrain in the edition.

From That (Brahman) the wind has come, and in That likewise is (its) dissolution; from That fire and the moon, and from That prāṇa has come.

Bhāṣya After this verse: by these two verses (19–20) it is said that all this arose from Brahman and dissolves in it; the next verse explains it.

उभौ च देवौ पृथिवीं दिवं च दिशश्च शुक्रं भुवनं बिभर्ति । तस्माद्दिशः सरितश्च स्रवन्ति तस्मात्समुद्रा विहिता महान्तः ॥ २१ ॥
ubhau ca devau pṛthivīṃ divaṃ ca diśaś ca śukraṃ bhuvanaṃ bibharti | tasmād diśaḥ saritaś ca sravanti tasmāt samudrā vihitā mahāntaḥ || 21 ||
Padaccheda
उभौ च देवौ पृथिवीम् दिवम् च दिशः च शुक्रम् भुवनम् बिभर्ति तस्मात् दिशः सरितः च स्रवन्ति तस्मात् समुद्राः विहिताः महान्तः
ubhau ca devau pṛthivīm divam ca diśaḥ ca śukram bhuvanam bibharti tasmāt diśaḥ saritaḥ ca sravanti tasmāt samudrāḥ vihitāḥ mahāntaḥ

The pure (Brahman) bears both the gods, the earth and heaven, the quarters and the world. From That the quarters and the rivers flow; from That the great oceans were made.

Bhāṣya The sense of the verse is plain.

यः सहस्रं सहस्राणां पक्षानाहृत्य संपतेत् । नान्तं गच्छेत्कारणस्य यद्यपि स्यान्मनोजवः । योगिनस्तं प्रपश्यन्ति भगवन्तं सनातनम् ॥ २२ ॥
yaḥ sahasraṃ sahasrāṇāṃ pakṣān āhṛtya saṃpatet | nāntaṃ gacchet kāraṇasya yady api syān manojavaḥ | yoginas taṃ prapaśyanti bhagavantaṃ sanātanam || 22 ||
Padaccheda
यः सहस्रम् सहस्राणाम् पक्षान् आहृत्य संपतेत् न अन्तम् गच्छेत् कारणस्य यदि अपि स्यात् मनः-जवः योगिनः तम् प्रपश्यन्ति भगवन्तम् सनातनम्
yaḥ sahasram sahasrāṇām pakṣān āhṛtya saṃpatet na antam gacchet kāraṇasya yadi api syāt manaḥ-javaḥ yoginaḥ tam prapaśyanti bhagavantam sanātanam
Word by word
He who, taking a thousand thousands of wings, should fly, would not reach the end of the cause (the supreme Self), even if he were swift as the mind. Yogins (alone) see him, the eternal Lord.

Introduction He now tells of the endlessness of Brahman.

Bhāṣya A man who took a thousand thousands of wings, making them his own, and flew, many times over, even for crores of kalpas, would not reach the end of the cause, the supreme Self, the cause of all; even if he were swift as the mind, he would not reach its end. Since he would not reach the end, the supreme Self is endless. Him, the endless supreme Self, yogins alone see.

अदर्शने तिष्ठति रूपमस्य पश्यन्ति चैनं सुसमिद्धसत्त्वाः । हीनो मनीषी मनसाभिपश्येद्य एनं विदुरमृतास्ते भवन्ति ॥ २३ ॥
adarśane tiṣṭhati rūpam asya paśyanti cainaṃ susamiddhasattvāḥ | hīno manīṣī manasābhipaśyed ya enaṃ vidur amṛtās te bhavanti || 23 ||
Padaccheda
अदर्शने तिष्ठति रूपम् अस्य पश्यन्ति च एनम् सु-समिद्ध-सत्त्वाः हीनः मनीषी मनसा अभिपश्येत् ये एनम् विदुः अमृताः ते भवन्ति
adarśane tiṣṭhati rūpam asya paśyanti ca enam su-samiddha-sattvāḥ hīnaḥ manīṣī manasā abhipaśyet ye enam viduḥ amṛtāḥ te bhavanti
Word by word
His form stands out of sight; yet those whose mind is well kindled (made clean by sacrifice and the rest) see him. (Therefore) the wise man, bereft (of the stains of passion and the rest), should see (him) with the mind. Those who know him (as ‘I am’) become immortal.

Bhāṣya Adarśane: in a domain not fit for seeing his form stands (the śruti: ‘na saṃdṛśe tiṣṭhati rūpam asya’). Though it stands where it cannot be seen, they see the supreme Self. Who? Those whose inner organ (sattva) is well and rightly kindled, being made spotless by sacrifice and the rest. Since this is so, the wise man, hīna, free from the stains of passion, hatred and the rest, of pure mind, should see with the mind. Those who know this supreme Self as ‘I am’ become immortal, free of death.

Note The verse has no refrain in the edition.

इमं यः सर्वभूतेषु आत्मानमनुपश्यति । अन्यत्रान्यत्र युक्तेषु स किं शोचेत्ततः परम् ॥ २४ ॥
imaṃ yaḥ sarvabhūteṣu ātmānam anupaśyati | anyatrānyatra yukteṣu sa kiṃ śocet tataḥ param || 24 ||
Padaccheda
इमम् यः सर्व-भूतेषु आत्मानम् अनुपश्यति अन्यत्र अन्यत्र युक्तेषु सः किम् शोचेत् ततः परम्
imam yaḥ sarva-bhūteṣu ātmānam anupaśyati anyatra anyatra yukteṣu saḥ kim śocet tataḥ param
Word by word
He who sees this Self in all beings, joined (to bodies and senses) here and there, why should he grieve after that?

Bhāṣya This, the innermost of all; he who sees it in all beings, in all creatures. In what kind of beings? anyatrānyatra yukteṣu: in those joined to body, senses and the rest, who take the body and the rest for the self. Seeing his self in all beings, why should he grieve after that? Seeing the Self that dwells in all beings, he has gained his end and does not grieve (the śruti: ‘tatra ko mohaḥ kaḥ śoka ekatvam anupaśyataḥ’).

यथोदपाने महति सर्वतःसंप्लुतोदके । एवं भूतेषु सर्वेषु ब्राह्मणस्य विशेषतः ॥ २५ ॥
yathodapāne mahati sarvataḥsaṃplutodake | evaṃ bhūteṣu sarveṣu brāhmaṇasya viśeṣataḥ || 25 ||
Padaccheda
यथा उदपाने महति सर्वतः-संप्लुत-उदके एवम् भूतेषु सर्वेषु ब्राह्मणस्य विशेषतः
yathā udapāne mahati sarvataḥ-saṃpluta-udake evam bhūteṣu sarveṣu brāhmaṇasya viśeṣataḥ
Word by word
As (there is no need) of a great well when (all is) flooded with water on every side, so (there is no need) of anything among all beings for the brāhmaṇa (who sees the Self in them), above all.

Introduction He says the same.

Bhāṣya As one who has done what is to be done has no use for a great well when water floods on every side, so for the brāhmaṇa who sees the Self in all beings there is, above all, no purpose at all; so the Lord Vāsudeva says (Gītā 3.18): ‘na cāsya sarvabhūteṣu kaścid arthavyapāśrayaḥ’.

अहमेवास्मि वो माता पिता पुत्रोऽस्म्यहं पुनः । आत्माहमस्मि सर्वस्य यच्च नास्ति यदस्ति च ॥ २६ ॥
aham evāsmi vo mātā pitā putro 'smy ahaṃ punaḥ | ātmāham asmi sarvasya yac ca nāsti yad asti ca || 26 ||
Padaccheda
अहम् एव अस्मि वः माता पिता पुत्रः अस्मि अहम् पुनः आत्मा अहम् अस्मि सर्वस्य यत् च न अस्ति यत् अस्ति च
aham eva asmi vaḥ mātā pitā putraḥ asmi aham punaḥ ātmā aham asmi sarvasya yat ca na asti yat asti ca
Word by word
I am your mother, (I your) father; I am the son too. I am the Self of all, of what is not and of what is.

Introduction Now, to make what has been said firm, he shows his own experience, as Vāmadeva and others did.

Bhāṣya O Dhṛtarāṣṭra, I am your mother, who bore you; your father too am I; I am your son: I am Duryodhana and the rest. What more? I am the Self of all creatures; of what is not and of what is, I am the self.

पितामहोऽस्मि स्थविरः पिता पुत्रश्च भारत । ममैव यूयमात्मस्था न मे यूयं न चाप्यहम् ॥ २७ ॥
pitāmaho 'smi sthaviraḥ pitā putraś ca bhārata | mamaiva yūyam ātmasthā na me yūyaṃ na cāpy aham || 27 ||
Padaccheda
पितामहः अस्मि स्थविरः पिता पुत्रः च भारत मम एव यूयम् आत्म-स्थाः न मे यूयम् न च अपि अहम्
pitāmahaḥ asmi sthaviraḥ pitā putraḥ ca bhārata mama eva yūyam ātma-sthāḥ na me yūyam na ca api aham
Word by word
I am the aged grandfather, the father and the son, O Bhārata. You stand in my Self alone; (yet in truth) you are not in me, nor I (in you).

Introduction So far father and the rest have been shown as regards beings; now he shows himself as father and the rest as regards the deities.

Bhāṣya The aged grandfather of Indra and the others, the beginningless supreme Self: that too am I. The father of Indra and the others, Hiraṇyagarbha: that too am I. So you stand in my Self alone. Yet, in the highest truth, you are none of you established in my self, nor am I established in you; so the Lord says (Gītā 9.4–5): ‘matsthāni sarvabhūtāni’.

आत्मैव स्थानं मम जन्म चात्मा ओतप्रोतोऽहमजरप्रतिष्ठः । अजश्चरो दिवारात्रमतन्द्रितोऽहं मां विज्ञाय कविरास्ते प्रसन्नः ॥ २८ ॥
ātmaiva sthānaṃ mama janma cātmā otaproto 'ham ajarapratiṣṭhaḥ | ajaś caro divārātram atandrito 'haṃ māṃ vijñāya kavir āste prasannaḥ || 28 ||
Padaccheda
आत्मा एव स्थानम् मम जन्म च आत्मा ओत-प्रोतः अहम् अजर-प्रतिष्ठः अजः चरः दिवारात्रम् अतन्द्रितः अहम् माम् विज्ञाय कविः आस्ते प्रसन्नः
ātmā eva sthānam mama janma ca ātmā ota-protaḥ aham ajara-pratiṣṭhaḥ ajaḥ caraḥ divārātram atandritaḥ aham mām vijñāya kaviḥ āste prasannaḥ
Word by word
(Yet) the Self alone is my abode, and my birth is the Self; I am woven across and along (through all), established in the ageless (my own greatness). Unborn, moving, unwearied day and night am I; knowing me, the wise man remains serene.

Introduction Although you do not stand in my self, nor I in you, yet —

Bhāṣya The Self alone is the abode, the support; and birth is the Self: from this Self all has arisen (the śruti: ‘ātmaivedaṃ sarvam’). I alone stand woven across and along, the self of the world, your begetter. ajarapratiṣṭhaḥ: he stands in his own greatness, ageless, free of old age and death (the śruti: ‘sa bhagavaḥ kasmin pratiṣṭhitaḥ sve mahimni yadi vā na mahimni’). He does not gloss the second half (compare 4.18).

अणोरणीयान्सुमनाः सर्वभूतेष्ववस्थितः । पितरं सर्वभूतानां पुष्करे निहितं विदुः ॥ २९ ॥
aṇor aṇīyān sumanāḥ sarvabhūteṣv avasthitaḥ | pitaraṃ sarvabhūtānāṃ puṣkare nihitaṃ viduḥ || 29 ||
Padaccheda
अणोः अणीयान् सु-मनाः सर्व-भूतेषु अवस्थितः पितरम् सर्व-भूतानाम् पुष्करे निहितम् विदुः
aṇoḥ aṇīyān su-manāḥ sarva-bhūteṣu avasthitaḥ pitaram sarva-bhūtānām puṣkare nihitam viduḥ
Word by word
Subtler than the subtle, of pure mind, I abide in all beings. (The knowers of Brahman) know the father of all beings, placed in the lotus (of the heart).

Bhāṣya Subtler than the subtle. sumanāḥ: whose mind is good: free of passion, hatred, pride, envy, grief, delusion and the like, pure, of the form of Brahman, consciousness, being and bliss without a second. In all beings, all creatures, in the midst of the lotus of the heart, I abide as the Self.

Bhāṣya So far his own experience has been shown. Now: not our experience alone is the evidence here; others know it so too. Sanaka, Sanandana, Vāmadeva and the other knowers of Brahman know the father of all creatures, the supreme Lord who begets them, placed in the lotus, in the midst of the lotus of the heart: they know the supreme Self as the self. The śruti shows their experience: in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka, ‘tad dhaitat paśyann ṛṣir vāmadevaḥ pratipede 'haṃ manur abhavaṃ sūryaś ca’; in the Taittirīya, ‘etat sāma gāyann āste’, where the knower's own experience is shown by the singing of the sāman, to make known that he has gained his end; in the Chāndogya too, ‘tad dhāsya vijajñāv iti’; and in the Talavakāra the knower's own experience is shown with ‘aham annam’ and what follows.

Bhāṣya Śaṅkara closes the bhāṣya with three verses of his own: meditating on the Lord, ever pure, awake and free, as the self, restraining the six senses, one stays unmoving; there is a reality, a mass of consciousness, the cause from which the world comes forth, not perishable, not born from it, that dispels darkness; denoted by the one word tat, ever immortal, spotless, the seer of the mind's activities, bliss: that I am, that I am.
