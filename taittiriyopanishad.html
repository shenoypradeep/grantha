Śīkṣāvallī · The vallī on pronunciation
ॐ शं नो मित्रः शं वरुणः । शं नो भवत्वर्यमा । शं न इन्द्रो बृहस्पतिः । शं नो विष्णुरुरुक्रमः । नमो ब्रह्मणे । नमस्ते वायो । त्वमेव प्रत्यक्षं ब्रह्मासि । त्वामेव प्रत्यक्षं ब्रह्म वदिष्यामि । ऋतं वदिष्यामि । सत्यं वदिष्यामि । तन्मामवतु । तद्वक्तारमवतु । अवतु माम् । अवतु वक्तारम् । ॐ शान्तिः शान्तिः शान्तिः ॥ १ ॥

ॐ शम् नः मित्रः शम् वरुणः शम् नः भवतु अर्यमा शम् नः इन्द्रः बृहस्पतिः शम् नः विष्णुः उरु-क्रमः नमः ब्रह्मणे नमः ते वायो त्वम् एव प्रत्यक्षम् ब्रह्म असि त्वाम् एव प्रत्यक्षम् ब्रह्म वदिष्यामि ऋतम् वदिष्यामि सत्यम् वदिष्यामि तत् माम् अवतु तत् वक्तारम् अवतु अवतु माम् अवतु वक्तारम् ॐ शान्तिः शान्तिः शान्तिः

Word by word
Om. May Mitra be propitious to us, and Varuṇa propitious; may Aryaman be propitious to us; may Indra and Bṛhaspati be propitious to us; may Viṣṇu of the wide stride be propitious to us. Homage to Brahman. Homage to you, O Vāyu. You indeed are the perceptible Brahman. You alone I shall declare to be the perceptible Brahman. (You) I shall declare to be the right; (you) I shall declare to be the true. May that protect me; may that protect the teacher. May it protect me; may it protect the teacher. Om. Peace, peace, peace.

Introduction Introducing the Upaniṣad, after three verses (homage to the Self that is knowledge, from which the whole world is born, in which it dissolves and by which it is upheld; homage to the teachers who of old explained all the Vedāntas by word, sentence and proof; and that he composes this exposition of the essence of the Taittirīyaka by his teacher's grace, for those who like the meaning made plain): the obligatory rites, which wear away sin already incurred, and the optional rites, for those who want their fruits, were treated in the earlier part of the text. Now the knowledge of Brahman is begun, to remove the cause of taking up action. That cause is desire, for desire sets one moving; those whose desires are fulfilled, having no desire, abide in their own Self and cannot be moved to act. To desire the Self is to have every desire fulfilled, for the Self is Brahman, and the Upaniṣad will say that its knower attains the highest. So attaining the highest is abiding in one's own Self when ignorance has ceased (2.7, 2.8).

Bhāṣya The objections: (1) liberation, abiding in one's own Self, comes without effort: one does not undertake optional and forbidden acts, the action that has begun to bear fruit is used up in experiencing it, and the obligatory rites are done so that no fault of omission arises. (2) Or, since the unsurpassed joy called heaven is caused by action, liberation comes from action alone.

Bhāṣya The reply to (1): actions are many. The actions of many births, with opposed fruits, some begun to bear and some not, cannot all be used up in one birth, so another body will arise from what remains (Chāndogya 5.10.7). The obligatory rites do not destroy the action that has not begun to bear: a fault is taught for omitting them, so they serve to avert that coming pain; and even if they destroyed anything, it would be the impure only, not the pure, with which they do not conflict. Nor, without knowledge, can desire, the cause of action, cease: the desire of one who does not know the Self is for fruits that are not the Self, while in one's own Self, always attained, desire cannot arise. And the omission of an obligatory rite is an absence, from which no fault can arise; the not-doing is only the mark of a fault that comes from sins stored up earlier (so the present participle in ‘not doing what is enjoined’ is in place), otherwise being would arise from non-being, against all means of knowledge. So ‘abiding in the Self without effort’ does not hold.

Bhāṣya The reply to (2): liberation is eternal, and nothing eternal is produced; what is produced is not eternal. Nor can rites joined with knowledge produce what is eternal: ‘eternal’ and ‘produced’ contradict each other. Nor is liberation like the absence that follows destruction, produced and yet lasting: liberation is a positive being, and that such an absence ‘is produced’ is a mere manner of speaking, since absence has no distinctions of its own and is only spoken of through the being it negates. Nor is liberation eternal because the agent of knowledge and rites is eternal and their stream unbroken like the Ganges: agency is itself pain, and when agency stops liberation would break off. Therefore liberation is abiding in one's own Self when ignorance, desire and action, the causes of taking up (a body), have ceased; the Self is itself Brahman, and ignorance ceases by knowing it. For that knowledge the Upaniṣad is begun. ‘Upaniṣad’ means the knowledge: it wears away womb, birth and old age for those devoted to it, or brings them to Brahman, or the highest good is seated in it; the book is called so because it serves that knowledge.

Bhāṣya Mitra is the deity who presides over the activity of prāṇa and over the day; Varuṇa over the activity of apāna and the night; Aryaman over the eye and the sun; Indra over strength; Bṛhaspati over speech and understanding; Viṣṇu of the wide stride over the feet. When these deities of the body give well-being, knowledge is heard, held and used without hindrance. The seeker bows to Vāyu and calls him Brahman to quiet the obstacles to the knowledge of Brahman: Vāyu is Brahman because the fruits of all acts depend on him, so ‘homage to Brahman’ and ‘homage to you’ both address Vāyu, indirectly and directly. He is the perceptible Brahman because, beside the eye and the other senses, he is outside, near and unconcealed.

Bhāṣya Ṛta is what is firmly settled in the understanding, as scripture and duty have it; satya is that same thing carried out in speech and body. Both depend on Vāyu, so the sense is ‘you I shall call ṛta, you I shall call satya.’ ‘May that protect me,’ the seeker of knowledge, by joining me with knowledge; ‘the speaker’ is the teacher, protected by being given the power to teach. The repetition shows earnestness. ‘Peace’ is said thrice to quiet the obstacles to gaining knowledge that come from oneself, from other beings and from the gods.

ॐ शीक्षाम् व्याख्यास्यामः वर्णः स्वरः मात्रा बलम् साम सन्तानः इति उक्तः शीक्षा-अध्यायः

Word by word
Om. We shall explain pronunciation: sound, accent, length, effort, evenness, continuity. Thus the chapter on pronunciation has been stated.

Introduction The Upaniṣad is chiefly for the knowledge of its meaning; so that care in reciting the text is not given up on that account, the chapter on pronunciation is begun.

Bhāṣya Śīkṣā is śikṣā with a Vedic long vowel: that by which the uttering of sounds and the rest is learnt, or the sounds and the rest that are learnt. Sound is a and the others; accent is udātta and the others; length is short and the others; effort is a particular exertion; sāman is evenness, uttering the sounds at a middle pace; santāna is continuity, that is, saṃhitā. ‘Has been stated’ closes the chapter.

Word by word
May fame be ours together; may the radiance of sacred knowledge be ours together. Now, therefore, we shall explain the secret teaching of the conjunction (saṃhitā) under five heads: concerning the worlds, concerning the lights, concerning knowledge, concerning progeny, concerning the body. These they call the great conjunctions. Now concerning the worlds: the earth is the prior form, heaven the latter form, space the junction, air the joining. So much concerning the worlds. Now concerning the lights: fire is the prior form, the sun the latter form, the waters the junction, lightning the joining. So much concerning the lights. Now concerning knowledge: the teacher is the prior form, the pupil the latter form, knowledge the junction, teaching the joining. So much concerning knowledge. Now concerning progeny: the mother is the prior form, the father the latter form, offspring the junction, begetting the joining. So much concerning progeny. Now concerning the body: the lower jaw is the prior form, the upper jaw the latter form, speech the junction, the tongue the joining. So much concerning the body. These are the great conjunctions. He who thus meditates on these great conjunctions as explained is joined with offspring, with cattle, with the radiance of sacred knowledge, with food to eat and with the heavenly world.

Bhāṣya The prayer for fame and radiance is the pupil's wish, for the pupil has not yet gained his end; the teacher has. ‘Now’: after the rule of recitation just given. ‘Therefore’: because an understanding steeped in the text cannot at once be brought down to the knowledge of its meaning, a meditation that stays close to the text is explained first. The five heads are five supports, objects of knowledge. They are called great conjunctions because they concern great things, the worlds and the rest, and concern the saṃhitā.

Bhāṣya The prior form is the prior letter: one is to look on the prior letter of the saṃhitā as the earth, and so on. The junction is the middle, where the prior and latter forms are joined; the joining is that by which they are joined. ‘Now’ in each place marks the order of the meditations.

Bhāṣya ‘knows’ means ‘meditates’, as the context is knowledge and as 1.6 says ‘meditate’. Meditation (upāsana) is a continuous stream of like cognitions, unmixed with unlike ones, on a support that scripture names; the word is used in the world of one who continually attends on a teacher or a king, and such a one gains the fruit. So here he who meditates thus gains the fruits from offspring up to heaven.

Introduction For one who desires intelligence and one who desires prosperity, the means to them are taught, a recitation (japa) and an oblation (homa); ‘may he strengthen me with intelligence’ and ‘then bring me prosperity’ show it.

Bhāṣya The bull of the Vedas is Om, being foremost; it is of all forms because it pervades all speech (Chāndogya 2.23.3). The Vedas are the immortal; ‘arose from them’ means that when Prajāpati performed austerity to draw the best essence from the worlds, the gods, the Vedas and the vyāhṛtis, Om appeared to him as that essence, for the eternal Om cannot literally be born. Indra is the supreme Lord, master of all desires. spṛṇotu: may he gladden or strengthen; strength of wisdom is what is asked. ‘Of the immortal’: of the knowledge of Brahman, the cause of immortality. vicarṣaṇam: able, fit (‘may it be’ is supplied). viśruvam: may I be a hearer. The sense of the whole: may my body and organs be fit for the knowledge of the Self; intelligence is asked for that alone.

Bhāṣya ‘sheath of Brahman’, of the supreme Self, as of a sword, being the place where it is found: Om is the symbol of Brahman, and in it Brahman is perceived. ‘Covered by intelligence’: by worldly intelligence, that is, its truth is unknown to those of common understanding. ‘What has been heard’: the knowledge of the Self and the rest, gained by hearing; ‘guard’ it: let me gain it and not forget it. These mantras are for the recitation of one who desires intelligence.

Bhāṣya The mantras that follow are for the oblation of one who desires prosperity. acīram is aciram, ‘quickly’, with a Vedic long vowel; or ‘for long’. gāvaḥ stands for gāḥ. ‘Then’: after intelligence has been brought about, for prosperity in one without intelligence only brings harm. ‘Woolly’: with goats, sheep and the like. It is Om that is addressed throughout. svāhā marks the end of a mantra used for an oblation.

Bhāṣya Yaśaḥ means ‘famous’. vasyasaḥ is vasīyasaḥ: may I excel the wealthier among men of my own kind. ‘That you’: you who are the sheath of Brahman; ‘may I enter’, and having entered be not other than you; ‘enter into me’: let the two of us be one. ‘Of a thousand branches’: of many divisions; in you I cleanse my sinful deeds. aharjara is the year: turning by days it wears out the worlds, or the days are worn away, contained, in it. prativeśa is a house close by where weariness is put off; so Om is the place where the sin and pain of those devoted to it are put off. ‘Shine upon me’: show yourself to me; ‘come to me’: make me of your own nature, as metal pierced by quicksilver.

Bhāṣya The desire for prosperity is spoken of in a section on knowledge because wealth is for rites, and rites are for wearing away sin already incurred; when that is worn away knowledge shines forth. So the smṛti (Mahābhārata 12.197.8): knowledge arises in men when sinful action is worn away; as on the clear surface of a mirror, they see the Self in the self.

Introduction The meditation on the saṃhitā was taught, and after it the mantras for one who desires intelligence and prosperity, which serve knowledge indirectly. Next comes the inward meditation on Brahman as the vyāhṛtis, whose fruit is sovereignty.

Bhāṣya ‘indeed’ recalls the three vyāhṛtis as well known. Māhācamasya is the son of Mahācamasa; ‘made known’, with u ha sma telling of what is past, means he knew, he saw it. He is named so that the seer is remembered: remembering the seer is part of the meditation. Mahaḥ is Brahman, for Brahman is great (mahat); it is the Self (ātman, from āp, ‘to pervade’), because the other vyāhṛtis, as worlds, gods, Vedas and prāṇas, are pervaded by mahaḥ as the sun, the moon, Brahman and food. ‘Deities’ stands also for the worlds and the rest: all are its limbs, and so it is said that by the sun and the others the worlds and the others grow great, as the limbs grow by the self.

Bhāṣya Each vyāhṛti is fourfold. Bhūḥ: this world, fire, the Ṛgveda, prāṇa. Bhuvaḥ: the mid-region, air, the Sāmans, apāna. Suvaḥ: yonder world, the sun, the Yajus formulas, vyāna. Mahaḥ: the sun, the moon, Brahman, food. ‘Brahman’ among the Vedas is Om, nothing else being possible where words are the subject. They are taught again as ‘four, fourfold’ so that they are meditated on in just that order.

Bhāṣya On ‘he knows Brahman’: it may be asked why this is said, when Brahman is already known from ‘that is Brahman, that is the Self.’ There is no fault: its particulars are still to be told, that it is found within the heart, consists of mind, and the rest down to ‘rich in peace’ (1.6); meaning to tell them, the text speaks as though Brahman were not yet known. So this anuvāka makes one sentence with the next, and the two teach one meditation: ‘as bhūḥ he is established in fire’ (1.6) shows it, and no word here enjoins a separate meditation. All the gods, being his limbs, bring him tribute once sovereignty is attained.

Introduction This teaching of duties is begun to show that before the knowledge of Brahman the rites of śruti and smṛti must be done as a rule. The instruction is for the refining of the man; in one refined, whose mind is pure, the knowledge of the Self arises with ease (Manu 12.104; ‘seek to know Brahman by austerity’, 3.2). ‘Instructs’: to go against an instruction is a fault. Rites are set out first, before the knowledge of Brahman alone is begun; and once that knowledge has risen the Upaniṣad shows that nothing of rites is left (2.7, 2.9). So rites serve the arising of knowledge by wearing away the sins stored up before (Īśa 11). The right and the rest were taught earlier (1.9) to keep them from being thought useless; here, to rule that they must be done, since they serve the arising of knowledge.

Bhāṣya ‘having taught the Veda’, the teacher then makes the pupil grasp its meaning; so one who has learnt the Veda should not return from the teacher's house without inquiring into dharma. ‘Speak the truth’: say what is to be said as it is known by valid means. dharma is the general word for what is to be practised. Having given the teacher the wealth he wants as the fee for knowledge, and with his leave taken a fitting wife, do not break the line of offspring: even if no son is born, effort is to be made by the rites for a son; this is why offspring, begetting and propagation were named as three (1.9). ‘Not heedless of truth’: untruth is not to be spoken even through forgetfulness, as the word ‘heedless’ shows. Heedlessness of dharma is not practising it. kuśala is action for one's own protection; bhūtyai: from auspicious action for the sake of prosperity. Mother, father, teacher and guest are to be honoured like gods.

Bhāṣya ‘blameless’ actions are those uncensured, marked as the conduct of the cultured; others, blameworthy, are not to be done even if the cultured do them. ‘Our good deeds’: those of the teachers that do not go against scripture are alone to be followed, for their unseen fruit; not the contrary, even if teachers do them. ‘Better than we’: marked out by being teachers and the like, and brāhmaṇas, not kṣatriyas or others; their weariness is to be removed by offering a seat and so on. Bhāṣya The inquiry into knowledge and rites, the question: does the highest good come from rites alone, from rites helped by knowledge, from knowledge and rites together, from knowledge helped by rites, or from knowledge alone? First view: from rites alone; for only he who knows the whole meaning of the Veda, the Upaniṣads' knowledge of the Self included, is entitled to rites (Manu 2.165), the whole Veda is held to be for rites, and if the highest good did not come from rites the Veda would be useless. Reply: liberation is eternal, and what rites effect is known to be non-eternal. That liberation comes without knowledge, by not doing optional and forbidden acts and so on, was answered (note on 1.1): the action left over brings another body, and obligatory rites do not destroy it. As for the entitlement: meditation is other than the knowledge got by hearing. One is entitled to rites by learning alone; meditation is enjoined as a different thing with liberation for its fruit, as ‘to be reflected on, to be meditated on’ are enjoined as separate efforts after ‘to be heard’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.4.5).

Bhāṣya Second and third views: then from rites helped by knowledge; as poison or curd, which of themselves bring death or fever, produce another effect when joined with a mantra or sugar, so rites with knowledge produce liberation. No: what is produced is non-eternal. Nor is a produced thing eternal because a text says so: a text makes known what is, it does not make what is not; not by a hundred texts is the eternal produced or the produced made imperishable. This also answers the view that knowledge and rites together produce liberation. Nor do the two remove what obstructs liberation: rites are seen to have other fruits, namely producing, refining, changing or reaching something, and liberation is the opposite of these.

Bhāṣya On reaching: if it is said that liberation is reached, as the texts on going show (Muṇḍaka 1.2.11, Kaṭha 2.3.16), no: Brahman is all-pervading, being the cause of space and the rest, and is not other than those who go; all knowing selves are non-different from it. One goes to a place other than oneself; what is not other than a thing is not reached by it (2.6; Gītā 13.2). The texts on going and on lordly powers (Chāndogya 7.26.2, 8.2.1, 8.12.3) concern the effected Brahman; women and the rest are there, not in the cause (Chāndogya 6.2.1, 7.24.1; Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.4.14).

Bhāṣya On combining: knowledge and rites cannot be joined, for they conflict. Knowledge has for its object the reality in which the distinctions of agent and the other factors are dissolved; rites are accomplished by those very factors. One thing cannot in truth be seen both as having these distinctions and as void of them; one view must be false, and it is fitting that duality, the object of natural ignorance, is the false one (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.4.14, 1.4.10; Kaṭha 2.1.10; Chāndogya 7.24.1; here 2.7), and oneness the true (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.20; Chāndogya 6.2.1, 7.25.2; Muṇḍaka 2.2.11). Without seeing the difference of recipient and the other factors no rite is possible, and in the sphere of knowledge the seeing of otherness is denied a thousand times.

Bhāṣya On the injunctions: it is objected that rites are enjoined; if the knowledge of oneness wipes out the factors of action, as knowledge of the rope wipes out the snake, the texts enjoining rites lose their object, and scripture, being authoritative, cannot conflict with itself. Reply: scripture aims at teaching man's good. The texts on knowledge proceed to reveal knowledge, since man is to be freed from worldly existence and its cause, ignorance, removed by knowledge. The texts on rites do not teach that the factors of action exist; taking their existence as commonly accepted, they enjoin rites for wearing away sin incurred, for seekers of liberation, and as means to fruits for those who want fruits. In one obstructed by stored-up sin knowledge does not arise; when that is worn away knowledge arises, then ignorance ceases, then worldly existence ends for good. Again: he who sees what is not the Self desires what is not the Self; desiring, he acts; thence worldly existence, the taking of bodies to enjoy the fruits. For him who sees the oneness of the Self there is no object, so no desire; and abiding in one's own Self is liberation. For this reason too knowledge and rites conflict, and knowledge does not depend on rites for liberation. But the obligatory rites become causes of the arising of knowledge by removing the obstruction of sin stored up before; that is why they are set out in this section. So the texts enjoining rites are not contradicted, and it is established that the highest good comes from knowledge alone.

Bhāṣya On the other stages of life: it is objected that then there are no other āśramas; knowledge arises through rites, rites are enjoined for the householder, so there is one āśrama only, and the texts on lifelong rites agree. Reply: rites are many. Not only the Agnihotra and the like are rites: celibate studentship, austerity, truthfulness, quiet of mind, restraint, non-injury and the like, well known in the other āśramas, are the best means to knowledge and are unmixed, and so are meditation and the fixing of the mind (3.2). Knowledge may arise even before the householder's stage from the rites of former births; that stage is taken up for the sake of rites, and when knowledge, which rites are to bring, is already there, taking it up is pointless. Sons and the rest are for the worlds; how should one act who has turned from this world, the world of the ancestors and the world of the gods, who sees the ever-attained Self and no use in rites? Even one who has become a householder, when knowledge arises and ripens and he is dispassionate, will cease from rites (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.5.2).

Bhāṣya The last objections: the śruti takes more pains over rites, the Agnihotra and the like needing many means and much labour, while austerity and celibacy are shared by the householder and need little; so the other āśramas are not an equal option. Reply: the Agnihotra and celibacy practised in former births also help knowledge to arise; some are seen dispassionate from birth, others busy with rites, without dispassion, hostile to knowledge; for the dispassionate, the other āśramas are what is wanted. The pains taken over rites are explained by the countless fruits of rites (sons, heaven, the radiance of sacred knowledge) and men's many desires for them; and rites are the means, and it is over the means, not the end, that pains are taken. If it is said that, knowledge arising from rites by the removal of obstruction, the effort of hearing the Upaniṣads apart from rites is useless: no, there is no rule that knowledge arises only from the removal of obstruction and not from the Lord's grace, austerity, meditation and the like; non-injury and celibacy help knowledge, and hearing, reflection and meditation are its direct causes. Om. The knower of Brahman attains the highest. On that this (verse) has been uttered: ‘Brahman is the real, knowledge, the infinite. He who knows it, set in the cave (of the intellect) in the highest space (of the heart), enjoys all desires at once, as Brahman, the all-knowing.’ From that, from this Self, space arose; from space, air; from air, fire; from fire, the waters; from the waters, earth; from earth, herbs; from herbs, food; from food, man. This man, indeed, is made of the essence of food. This itself is his head; this (right arm) is the right wing; this (left arm) is the left wing; this (middle of the body) is the trunk; this (below the navel) is the tail, the support. On that too there is this verse.

Note Before this vallī the bhāṣya recites two peace invocations: śaṃ no mitraḥ (as 1.1) and oṃ saha nāv avatu, saha nau bhunaktu, saha vīryaṃ karavāvahai, tejasvi nāv adhītam astu, mā vidviṣāvahai, oṃ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ. Śaṅkara: the invocation of 1.12 was to quiet the obstacles to the knowledge already gained; these are to quiet the obstacles to the knowledge of Brahman now to be told. ‘May it protect us both together’, pupil and teacher; ‘may it nourish us both together; may we together bring about the strength that comes of knowledge; may what we two, being bright, have studied be well studied’, fit for knowing the meaning; ‘may we not hate each other’, for hatred may come to pupil or teacher from a wrong done through negligence in the taking of knowledge. The gaining of the knowledge of the Self without hindrance is prayed for, since the highest good is rooted in it.

Introduction Introducing the vallī: the meditations on the saṃhitā and the rest, which do not conflict with rites, were taught, and then the seeing of the Self with adjuncts, within, through the vyāhṛtis, with sovereignty for its fruit. That does not wholly crush the seed of worldly existence; so this is begun, to remove ignorance, the seed of all trouble, and for the seeing of the Self free of every adjunct. The purpose of this knowledge of Brahman is the ceasing of ignorance and through it the final ending of worldly existence (2.9, 2.7). The text states the purpose itself at the outset, ‘the knower of Brahman attains the highest’, for one sets about hearing, grasping, holding and practising a knowledge only when its connection and purpose are known.

Bhāṣya Brahman is so called from being the greatest; ‘the highest’, the unsurpassed, is that same Brahman, for by knowing one thing another is not attained (Muṇḍaka 3.2.9). Objection: Brahman is all-pervading and the Self of all, so it cannot be attained; attaining is of one limited thing by another. Reply: attaining and not attaining Brahman depend on seeing and not seeing. The jīva, though in truth Brahman, sees as itself the outer, limited selves made of the elements, the one made of food and the rest, and its mind clings to them. As the man who himself makes up the number, with his mind on those to be counted outside him, sees himself as missing though he is not remote, so through ignorance, which is the not seeing of one's true nature as Brahman, one takes the selves made of food and the rest for oneself and thinks ‘I am none other than these.’ So Brahman, though one's own Self, is unattained through ignorance; and as that man, reminded by someone, attains himself again by knowledge, so Brahman is attained by the knowledge that sees the Brahman taught by the śruti as one's own Self.

Bhāṣya ‘the knower of Brahman attains the highest’ is the aphorism for the whole vallī. The verse is cited for three ends: to fix the nature of the Brahman so named by a definition able to mark it off from everything else; so that this Brahman is known specifically as one's inmost Self, not other than oneself; and to show that the fruit, attaining the highest Brahman, is being the Self of all, being Brahman beyond every trait of worldly existence, and nothing else.

Bhāṣya On the definition: ‘Brahman is the real, knowledge, the infinite’ is a sentence that defines Brahman. The three words qualify Brahman, which is the thing qualified because it is what is meant to be known; hence they stand in one case and refer to one thing. Objection: a qualifier has a use where several things of one class can take different qualifiers, as a blue or a red lotus; Brahman is one only, like the one sun. Reply: these qualifiers chiefly define. A qualifier marks a thing off from others of its own class; a definition marks it off from everything, as ‘space is that which gives room.’ The three words are not construed with one another; each goes with ‘Brahman’: Brahman is the real, Brahman is knowledge, Brahman is the infinite.

Bhāṣya On the three words: the real (satya) is what does not depart from the form in which it has been ascertained; what departs from it is unreal, so every modification is unreal (Chāndogya 6.1.4: the clay alone is real). ‘Brahman is the real’ thus turns Brahman away from modifications. It would follow that Brahman is a cause, and so a factor of action and, being a thing like clay, insentient; therefore ‘knowledge’ is said. jñāna here is knowing, awareness, not ‘knower’, since it qualifies Brahman together with ‘real’ and ‘infinite’: what changes as an agent of knowing could not be real or infinite, and the infinite is what is divided from nothing, while a knower is divided from the known and the knowing (Chāndogya 7.24.1). If it is said that ‘knows nothing else’ implies that it knows itself: no, that sentence is meant to define the vast (bhūman) by denying the familiar ‘one sees another’, not to affirm an act upon itself. In itself there is no division, so no knowing; if the Self were what is known there would be no knower; a partless thing cannot be knower and known at once; and if the Self were knowable like a jar, the teaching of knowledge would be useless. Pure being, too, would not hold if it had the mark of an agent of knowing. So ‘knowledge’ is used to exclude agency and the other factors and to exclude insentience. And since worldly knowledge is seen to be finite, ‘infinite’ is added to exclude that.

Bhāṣya The objection of emptiness: the words only exclude the unreal and the rest, and Brahman is not known beforehand as a lotus is; so the sentence means nothing, like ‘having bathed in the water of a mirage, with a garland of sky-flowers on his head, here goes the son of a barren woman, armed with a bow of hare's horn.’ Reply: no, for it is a definition, and a definition of nothing would be pointless. And the words do not give up their own meanings: only words that mean something can restrict Brahman from things of the opposite kind. ‘Infinite’ qualifies by denying finitude; ‘real’ and ‘knowledge’ qualify by conveying their own meanings.

Bhāṣya On knowledge as the nature of Brahman: the word ‘Self’ is used of Brahman (‘from this Self’), so Brahman is the knower's own Self; so also 2.8 (‘he passes into the self made of bliss’) and 2.6 (‘he entered it’). Objection: then, being the Self, it is the agent of knowing (‘he desired’, 2.6); and if knowledge is the bare sense of the verb, it is non-eternal and dependent, for the sense of a verb depends on the factors of action. Reply: awareness is the very nature of the Self, not apart from it, and so eternal; it is called an effect only figuratively. The intellect, the adjunct, changes through the eye and the other doors into the forms of objects; its appearances as sound and the rest are objects of the Self's awareness and arise pervaded by it. These, called ‘knowledge’ and being the sense of the verb, are fancied by the undiscerning to be changing properties of the Self itself. Brahman's knowledge is like the sun's light or fire's heat: not other than its nature, needing no other cause. Since all things are undivided from it in place and time, since it is the cause of time, space and the rest and is subtle beyond all, nothing is unknown to it; so Brahman is all-knowing (Śvetāśvatara 3.19; Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.3.30).

Bhāṣya The conclusion on the words: Brahman's knowing is therefore not the sense of a verb, and Brahman is not an agent of knowing; so it is not even directly named by the word ‘knowledge’. The word names its semblance, a property of the intellect, and by that Brahman is indicated, not expressed, for it has no class or other property that gives a word its footing. So too with ‘real’: the word, whose range is the general being of outer things, indicates Brahman. Standing together, the words restrict one another, turn Brahman away from what each literally names, and serve to define it. Hence Brahman is inexpressible (2.4, 2.7) and is not the sense of a sentence in the way ‘a blue lotus’ is.

Bhāṣya The cave is the intellect, in which knowledge, known and knower lie hidden, or in which enjoyment and liberation, the ends of man, lie hidden. The highest space is the unmanifest (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 3.8.11); or cave and space are one, the unmanifest, in which all things in the three times lie hidden. But it is right to take the highest space as that of the heart, since the space is meant as an aid to knowing (Chāndogya 3.12.7): in the cave, the intellect, within that space of the heart, Brahman is set, that is, is perceived distinctly; otherwise Brahman, all-pervading and without distinctions, has no tie to a particular place. ‘Enjoys all desires’: not one after another, as we enjoy sons, heaven and the rest, but together, in a single moment, by one perception that is eternal like the sun's light and is not other than the nature of Brahman. ‘As Brahman’: become Brahman, in that nature; not as the world enjoys, in a self made by adjuncts, a reflection like the sun in water, dependent on merit and on the eye and other organs. vipaścit: the all-knowing.

Bhāṣya On ‘real’ and ‘infinite’: the sense of the whole vallī, given as an aphorism in the brāhmaṇa sentence and briefly in the verse, is now determined at length. Infinity is of three kinds: in place, in time and as a thing. Space is infinite in place, but not in time or as a thing, being an effect. Brahman is no effect, so it is infinite in time. And as a thing: because it is not other than anything. A different thing is the limit of a thing: the thought ‘cow’ turns back from ‘horse’, so cowhood ends at horsehood. Brahman has no such difference, since it is the cause of all things, of time, space and the rest. Nor is it limited by its effect, for the effect is unreal, there being no effect apart from the cause (Chāndogya 6.1.4). As the cause of space it is infinite in place, for nothing all-pervading is seen to arise from what is not all-pervading. Hence its infinity is unsurpassed, and so is its reality.

Bhāṣya On the creation: ‘from that’ points to the Brahman of the brāhmaṇa sentence, ‘from this’ to the Brahman just defined in the verse, which is the Self of all (Chāndogya 6.8.7). Space has sound for its quality and gives room to things with form; air has two qualities, touch and the earlier one; fire three, with form; the waters four, with taste; earth five, with smell. From food turned into seed comes man, with the shape of head, hands and the rest. All creatures are alike modifications of the essence of food and of the line of Brahman, but man is named because he is foremost: he alone, being able, desirous and not debarred, is entitled to rites and knowledge (Aitareya Āraṇyaka 2.3.2).

Bhāṣya On the figure of the bird: it is man whom the text wishes to bring by knowledge to the inmost Brahman. His understanding, used to taking outer shapes that are not the Self for the Self, cannot at once be made to rest, without a support, on the inmost Self; so the text leads it inward by a likeness to the body that is seen, as the moon is shown by the branch of a tree. ‘This itself is the head’: the well-known head; it is said so that here, unlike the selves made of prāṇa and the rest where what is not a head is called head, the head is not taken as figurative. The right arm is the right wing of one who faces east; the middle of the body is the trunk (ātman); the part below the navel is the tail, the support, by which one stands, like a cow's tail in hanging down. On this pattern the figure holds for the selves made of prāṇa and the rest, like molten copper poured into a mould.

Bhāṣya Creatures, moving and unmoving, are born from food turned into the bodily fluids and the rest; ‘into it’ (api for prati): they dissolve into food when their life is over. Food is the eldest, the first-born, the cause of the other beings, those made of food and the rest; so all creatures arise from food, live by food and dissolve into food. It is the medicine of all because it allays the burning of the body in all that breathe. The fruit for the knower of food as Brahman: they obtain all food who meditate ‘I am born of food, I am food, I dissolve into food; therefore food is Brahman.’ The lines are repeated to give the reason and to close. The word anna is explained: it is eaten (adyate) by beings and itself eats (atti) beings.

Bhāṣya On the five sheaths: the scripture wishes to show Brahman, the inmost of the selves from the one made of food to the one made of bliss, as one's own inner Self by knowledge; so it removes the five sheaths made by ignorance, as one husks a grain of kodrava that has many husks to reach the kernel within. The self made of prāṇa is other than the lump made of the essence of food and within it, and like that lump is falsely imagined to be the Self; prāṇa is air, and it is made of that, mostly that. The self of food is filled by it as a bellows by air. It has the form of a man not of itself but after the human form of the self of food, like an image cast in a mould; so each later one has the form of the one before it, and each earlier one is filled by the later.

Bhāṣya Of the self made of prāṇa, a modification of air, the function called prāṇa, which goes out by mouth and nose, is conceived as the head, because the text says so; everywhere the wings and the rest are assigned on the word of the text alone. ‘Space’ is the function called samāna, seated in the space (of the body); it is the trunk because it is in the middle of the other functions. ‘Earth’ is the deity of earth, who holds up the prāṇa of the body (Praśna 3.8); otherwise the body would fly up by the action of udāna or fall by its weight. So she is the tail, the support.

‘The gods breathe after prāṇa, and so do men and beasts. For prāṇa is the life of beings; therefore it is called the life of all. They indeed reach the full span of life who meditate on prāṇa as Brahman. For prāṇa is the life of beings; therefore it is called the life of all.’ This (self made of prāṇa) indeed is the self, dwelling in the body, of that former one (made of food). Than this one made of prāṇa there is another, inner self, made of mind. By that this one is filled. That too is of the form of a man: following the human form of the former, this one is of the form of a man. The Yajus itself is its head; the Ṛk is the right wing; the Sāman is the left wing; the instruction (the Brāhmaṇa) is the trunk; the hymns of Atharvan and Aṅgiras are the tail, the support. Bhāṣya The gods, fire and the rest, having become one with prāṇa, whose nature is air and which has the power of breathing, perform the act of breathing after it; or, the body being the subject, the gods are the senses, which act after the chief prāṇa. So too men and beasts. Living beings, then, have a self not only in the limited one made of food but also in the one made of prāṇa within it, common to all and pervading the whole body; and so on with the subtler selves made of mind and the rest, down to the one made of bliss, each pervading the one before, all formed of the elements and made by ignorance. And they have their Self in that which is so by nature, the cause of space and the rest, eternal, changeless, all-pervading, defined as real, knowledge and infinite, beyond the five sheaths: that is in truth the Self of all. This too is said by implication.

Bhāṣya Prāṇa is the life of beings: as long as prāṇa dwells in this body, so long is life (Kauṣītaki 3.2); hence it is the life of all, for death is known to come when prāṇa leaves. Those who, leaving the outer self of food that is not common to all, meditate on the inner self of prāṇa, common to all, as Brahman (‘I am prāṇa, the self and life of all beings’), reach the full span of life in this world and do not die untimely; the full span is properly a hundred years. The reason is repeated, because whoever meditates on Brahman with a certain quality comes to share that quality. ‘Dwelling in the body’ (śārīra): being in the body made of food.

Bhāṣya Mind is the inner organ, whose nature is intention and the rest; the self made of it is within the one made of prāṇa. Yajus is a kind of mantra with no fixed number of syllables or end of feet; it is the head because it is foremost, helping directly in sacrifice, for the offering is given with a Yajus, with svāhā and the like. Or the head and the rest are assigned everywhere on the word of the text. A Yajus is a state of the mind that has for its object place of utterance, effort, sound, accent, letters, words and sentences, that consists of the intention of them and is formed by them, and that is marked by the convention ‘Yajus’ through the ear and the other organs; so too the Ṛk and the Sāman.

Bhāṣya On mantras as states of mind: only so is mental recitation possible, for it is the mental state that is repeated; a mantra that was an outer object, like a jar, could not be repeated in the mind, and yet the repetition of mantras is often enjoined in rites. If it is said that the mantra is repeated by repeating the memory of its syllables: no, for then the primary sense of the injunction ‘he recites the first verse thrice, the last thrice’ would be given up. Therefore the mantras are the consciousness of the Self, without beginning or end, limited by the adjunct of the mental states and resting in them, and named by the word Yajus. So the eternity of the Vedas holds; as outer objects they would be non-eternal like colour. The śruti ‘where all the Vedas become one, that is the Self in the mind’ (Taittirīya Āraṇyaka 3.11.1) and the mantra of Śvetāśvatara 4.8 agree with this.

Bhāṣya Ādeśa is the Brāhmaṇa, because it instructs in what is to be taught. The mantras and brāhmaṇa seen by Atharvan and Aṅgiras are the tail, the support, since they chiefly concern rites that give a firm footing, those for peace, prosperity and the like.

Bhāṣya Śaṅkara does not gloss the verse here; it comes again at 2.9, where he explains it.

Bhāṣya The self made of mind was said to consist of the Veda. Understanding (vijñāna) is the intellect that has the meaning of the Veda for its object and is of the nature of certainty; it is a property of the inner organ, marked by determination. The self made of understanding is formed by such certain cognitions, which are valid knowledge; for sacrifice and the rest are performed after valid knowledge, and the verse will say that it is the cause of sacrifice.

Bhāṣya In one who has such certainty, faith in what is to be done arises first; being first of all that is to be done, it is like the head. The right and truth are as explained (1.1). Yoga is being collected, composure; it is like the trunk, for in one who is collected and composed, faith and the rest become, like limbs, fit to grasp things as they are. Mahaḥ is the principle called the Great (mahat), the first-born (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 5.4.1); it is the tail, the support, because it is the cause, and the cause is the support of its effects, as earth is of trees and creepers; the Great is the cause of all cognitions.

‘Understanding performs the sacrifice, and it performs actions too. All the gods meditate on understanding as Brahman, the eldest. If one knows understanding as Brahman, and if one does not swerve from it, then, leaving sins in the body, one fully enjoys all desires.’ This (self made of understanding) indeed is the self, dwelling in the body, of that former one (made of mind). Than this one made of understanding there is another, inner self, made of bliss. By that this one is filled. That too is of the form of a man: following the human form of the former, this one is of the form of a man. Joy itself is its head; delight is the right wing; great delight is the left wing; bliss is the trunk; Brahman is the tail, the support. On that too there is this verse.

Bhāṣya It is one who has understanding that performs the sacrifice, with faith and the rest first; so understanding is the agent. Since all is done by understanding, it is fitting that the self made of understanding is Brahman. All the gods, Indra and the others, meditate on understanding as Brahman, the eldest, being first-born or because all activity comes after it; they meditate identifying themselves with it, and so have knowledge and lordly power. ‘If one does not swerve’: one used to taking outer things that are not the Self for the Self may fall from thinking of the Brahman of understanding as oneself; the sense is, if one gives up taking the selves of food and the rest for oneself and stays thinking of that alone as oneself. All sins have the identification with the body for their cause; when the cause is gone through identification with the Brahman of understanding, they go, as a shadow when the parasol is taken away. So, leaving in the body the sins born of the body, become the Brahman of understanding, one fully enjoys all the desires that lie in it, by the self made of understanding.

Bhāṣya On the self made of bliss: it is to be understood as a self that is an effect, not the supreme Self. (1) The context: effect-selves made of the elements, those of food and the rest, are the subject, and this one falls among them. (2) The suffix -maya means modification here, as in ‘made of food’. (3) The passing over: 2.8 says ‘he passes into the self made of bliss’, as into the self made of food; passing into is seen with effect-selves that are not the Self, and there is no passing into one's own Self, where there is no division, and Brahman is the very Self of him who passes. (4) A head and other parts cannot be imagined in the cause of space and the rest, which is no effect (2.7; Bṛhadāraṇyaka 3.8.8, 3.9.26). (5) The verse quoted next, ‘he becomes non-existent if he knows Brahman as non-existent’, would not fit: no one could doubt the existence of a self of bliss directly experienced with joy for its head. (6) And ‘Brahman is the tail, the support’ names Brahman separately as the support.

Bhāṣya Bliss is the fruit of knowledge and rites, and the self made of bliss is a modification of it. It is within the self of understanding, the cause of sacrifice and the rest: the fruit of knowledge and rites, being for the enjoyer, must be inmost, and knowledge and rites are undertaken for the sake of joy and the rest. The self made of bliss, formed by the latent tendencies of joy and the rest and resting on the self of understanding, is met with in dream. Joy (priya) is what arises from seeing a son or another loved object; it is like the head, being foremost. Delight (moda) is the gladness of gaining what is dear; great delight (pramoda) is that gladness at its height. Bliss (ānanda) is happiness in general, the trunk of joy and the other parts of happiness, since it runs through them.

Bhāṣya On bliss and Brahman: bliss is the supreme Brahman. It is manifested in a particular state of the inner organ that good action brings about, a state with a son, a friend or another object for its adjunct, clear and not covered by tamas; this is known in the world as the pleasure of objects. Since the action that brings such a state does not last, the pleasure is momentary. As the inner organ is made spotless by austerity that destroys tamas, by knowledge, by celibacy and by faith, so much, in the clear and limpid inner organ, a higher bliss rises and grows wide (2.7; Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.3.32); hence 2.8 will tell of bliss rising a hundredfold at each step as desire is stilled. Of the self made of bliss so rising, the supreme Brahman, the subject here, defined as real, knowledge and infinite, for the knowing of which the five sheaths were set out, which is within them and by which they all have a self, is the tail, the support. It is the non-dual Brahman, the end of all the duality imagined through ignorance, in which the self made of bliss ends in oneness.

‘Non-existent indeed does he become, if he knows Brahman as non-existent. If he knows that Brahman exists, then they know him as existent.’ This (self made of bliss) indeed is the self, dwelling in the body, of that former one (made of understanding). Now, therefore, the questions that follow: does anyone who does not know, on departing from here, go to that world (the supreme Self)? Or does anyone who knows, on departing, attain that world? He (the Self) desired: ‘May I be many, may I be born.’ He performed austerity (he reflected). Having performed austerity, he created all this, whatever there is. Having created it, he entered into that very thing. Having entered it, he became the formed and the formless, the defined and the undefined, the housed and the unhoused, the conscious and the unconscious, the true and the false: the Real became all this, whatever there is. Therefore they call it the Real. On that too there is this verse.

Bhāṣya On the verse: ‘non-existent’: like what is not, having no part in the ends of man; such is he who knows Brahman as not existing. The doubt arises because Brahman is beyond dealings: the understanding, used to taking as existent what falls within dealings, mere name though it is, takes what is beyond dealings as not existing, as a jar that is not met with is said not to be. Hence ‘if he knows that Brahman exists’, Brahman that is the ground of all imaginings and the seed of all activity, though every distinction has set in it. Him the knowers of Brahman know as existent, as one who has become the Self that is really existent: he is to be known by others as Brahman is. Or: he who thinks Brahman is not, having no faith, holds the whole good path, the order of class and stage of life, which is for reaching Brahman, to be nothing; such an unbeliever is called asat, ‘not good’, in the world; the opposite one, who knows that Brahman is, follows that path with faith, and the good know him as sat, ‘good’. So Brahman is to be understood as existing.

Bhāṣya On the questions: about the self made of bliss there is no doubt that it exists; the doubt is fitting about Brahman, from which every distinction is removed, and which is common to all. anupraśnāḥ are the pupil's questions that follow the teacher's words. Brahman, the cause of space and the rest, is common to the knower and the non-knower, so it is asked whether the non-knower too, departing, reaches that world, the supreme Self, or does not; and, if he does not, whether the knower reaches it or, like the non-knower, does not. samaśnutā3 u is samaśnute u, with protraction. The plural fits because a further question is implied: from ‘if he knows it as non-existent … as existent’ comes the doubt whether Brahman is or is not. So the three: is it or is it not; does the non-knower go to it or not; does the knower attain it or not. The rest of the text answers them.

Bhāṣya That Brahman exists: this also explains how Brahman is ‘the real’ (2.1), for to say that it is, is to say that it is real; the later sentences (‘they call it the Real’, ‘if this bliss were not in space’, 2.7) carry the same sense. Doubt: what exists is grasped with its particulars, like a jar; what is not is not found, like a hare's horn; Brahman is not found, so it is not. Reply: Brahman is the cause of space and the rest. All this effect is perceived as born from Brahman, and that from which something is born is seen to exist, as clay and seed, the causes of jar and sprout. No effect is found born of what is not; if name and form were born of the non-existent, having no self they would not be perceived, or would be perceived as shot through with non-existence; and they are not so. ‘How could what is be born of what is not?’ says another śruti (Chāndogya 6.2.2).

Bhāṣya That the cause is conscious: if it is a cause like clay or seed, is it not insentient? No, for it desires, and nothing that desires is insentient; Brahman was said to be all-knowing. Nor, because it desires, are its desires unfulfilled as ours are: it is independent. Desire and the other faults drive others, mastering them; Brahman's desires do not drive it. They are of the nature of the real and of knowledge, pure, being its own Self; Brahman moves them, with regard to the actions of living beings. Nor do they depend on merit and the like, or on a body and organs apart from itself: they are not other than itself.

Bhāṣya On ‘may I be many’: how can one thing become many without entering into other things? ‘May I be born.’ It is not a becoming many through another thing, as in begetting a son, but through the manifesting of name and form that lie unmanifest in the Self. When they are unfolded, they are unfolded in every state without giving up the nature of the Self, undivided from Brahman in place and time; that unfolding of name and form is Brahman's becoming many. In no other way can the partless Brahman become many or few, as the many and few of space are made only by other things. There is nothing other than the Self, divided from it in place or time, subtle, hidden or remote, past, present or to come. So name and form in every state have their self in Brahman alone; Brahman does not have its self in them. They are said to have it for their self because, when it is set aside, they are not. Through these two adjuncts Brahman comes to share in all dealings as knower, known, knowledge, word, meaning and the rest.

Bhāṣya Tapas here is knowledge (Muṇḍaka 1.1.9: ‘whose austerity consists of knowledge’); austerity of the other kind is impossible for one whose desires are fulfilled. He reflected on the arrangement of the world to be created, and then created all this in accord with the actions of living beings and other causes, with place, time, name and form, as it is experienced by all beings in all states.

Bhāṣya The inquiry into the entering: did the creator enter in that same form or in another? The absolutive (‘having created’) shows that the creator himself entered. Objections and replies: (1) If Brahman is a cause like clay, the effect is of its nature, the cause itself changing into the effect; a cause that has not entered cannot enter again after the effect has arisen, as clay does not enter the jar apart from becoming it. (2) As clay may enter a jar as powder, so the Self enters name and form in another form, as the jīva (Chāndogya 6.3.2)? No: Brahman is one; clay is many and has parts, and the powder has a place it has not yet entered; the one, partless Self has none. (3) Let it have parts, and enter like a hand into the mouth? No: there is no place empty of it to enter; and if the jīva entered the cause it would cease to be the jīva, as a jar entering clay ceases to be a jar; besides, the text says it entered ‘that very thing’ (the effect). (4) The effect that is the jīva becomes another effect, name and form? No: a jar does not become another jar; the texts that speak of the jīva as distinct from name and form would be contradicted; and liberation would be impossible, for one does not become the very thing from which one is to be freed, as a thief in chains does not become the chain. (5) The cause has changed into outer and inner, the body as container and the jīva within as contained? No: entering belongs to what was outside, as ‘he built a house and entered it’; what is already inside is not said to enter. (6) It enters as a reflection, like the sun in water? No: the Self is unlimited and formless; a reflection arises of a limited thing with form in something else that is clear, at a distance from it; the all-pervading Self has no other thing far from it to hold its reflection.

Bhāṣya The conclusion on the entering: then there is no entering, and yet the śruti, our means of knowledge in what is beyond the senses, says ‘he entered that very thing’; shall the sentence be cast aside as meaningless? No: it has another purpose, and the subject in hand is to be remembered: ‘the knower of Brahman attains the highest’, ‘the real, knowledge, the infinite’, ‘he who knows it set in the cave’. The knowledge of that Brahman is what is meant. To make its nature understood the effect, from space to the self made of food, was shown; then the inner selves, one within another, brought the hearer into the cave of understanding, and there the self made of bliss was shown. Beyond it, through that mark, the Self in which the growth of bliss ends, Brahman the tail and support, the ground of all imaginings and itself without them, is to be found in this very cave; so its entering is imagined. Brahman, being without distinctions, is not found elsewhere; a tie with something particular is what makes perception possible, as Rāhu is seen through his tie with moon and sun. So the tie with the cave of the inner organ, which is near and of the nature of light, is the cause of perceiving Brahman: as a jar is perceived along with light, the Self is perceived along with the light of the cognitions of the intellect. The same Brahman, the cause of space and the rest, having created the effect, is found in the cave of the intellect as though it had entered it, as seer, hearer, thinker, knower: that is its entering. Therefore Brahman, the cause, exists, and is to be perceived only as existing.

Bhāṣya On what it became: sat is the formed, tyat the formless; these two, name and form unmanifest in the Self, are unfolded by the Self within, and being undivided from it in place and time, the Self is said to have become them. The defined is what is marked off from things of like and unlike kind and pointed out as ‘this is that’, at a place and time; the undefined is the opposite. Having an abode, a nest, is a property of the formed; having none, of the formless. Tyat, the undefined and the unhoused, though properties of the formless, belong to what is unfolded, since they are heard of as coming after creation: tyat is prāṇa and the like. vijñāna is the conscious, avijñāna the unconscious, stone and the like. ‘The true’ here is the true of ordinary dealings, not the supremely real: water is true beside the water of a mirage, which is the false. What became all this is the Real, the supremely real, that is, Brahman; since all this mass of modification is the one Brahman named ‘being’, and name and form are not apart from it, the knowers of Brahman call it the Real.

Bhāṣya Summing up: in answer to the question whether Brahman is or is not, it has been said that the Self desired ‘may I be many’, created the effect as it desired, entered it, and became many, seeing, hearing, thinking, knowing. So one should know that this same cause of space and the rest, present in its effect, set in the cave of the heart in the highest space and perceived through the particular light of its cognitions, exists. The verse that follows shows, through the effect, the existence of the inmost Self.

‘In the beginning this was the non-existent (the unmanifest Brahman). From that the existent was born. That made itself by itself; therefore it is called the well-made (the self-made).’ That which is the self-made is indeed the savour; for on obtaining the savour one becomes blissful. Who indeed would breathe in, who would breathe out, if this bliss were not in the space (of the heart)? For this alone gives bliss. When one finds a fearless footing in this unseen, bodiless, undefined, abodeless (Brahman), then one has reached fearlessness. But when one makes even the smallest difference in it, then there is fear for him. That very (Brahman) is fear for the one who knows (difference) and does not think (of it as one). On that too there is this verse.

Bhāṣya On the verse: ‘the non-existent’ is the unmodified Brahman, the opposite of what is unfolded with particular names and forms; not what is utterly non-existent, for the existent is not born of that. ‘This’, the world unfolded with name and form, was before its arising Brahman itself, called non-existent; from it was born the existent, with names and forms divided. Is the effect divided from it, as a son from a father? No: it made itself by itself. Hence Brahman is called sukṛta, ‘self-made’: it is known as the self-agent, being the cause of all. Or, since it made everything by itself as the Self of all, the same Brahman, the cause, is called sukṛta also as merit; in either sense what is known in the world as sukṛta, the cause of the tie with fruits, is possible only if there is an eternal, conscious cause. So Brahman exists, because sukṛta is well known.

Bhāṣya Further reasons that Brahman exists: (1) it is the savour (rasa). A savour, sweet, sour and the like, is known as what satisfies and gives joy; on getting it one is happy, and what is not is never seen to cause joy. The knowers, brāhmaṇas who have no outer means of joy, who do not strive and do not seek, are seen to be full of joy as if they had obtained an outer savour; surely Brahman itself is their savour. (2) Breathing is seen. This body of a living man breathes out with prāṇa and in with apāna; such acts of the breaths and the senses are carried out by the body and organs working together, and things cannot join to serve one end without a conscious being that is not itself one of the joined. So ‘if this bliss were not in the space’, set in the cave in the highest space, who would breathe? And the world's bliss is from it: the supreme Self gives bliss to the world in accord with merit; the same Self, whose nature is bliss, is experienced by living beings as limited, through ignorance.

Bhāṣya (3) it is the cause of fearlessness for the knower and of fear for the non-knower; fearlessness comes of resting on a thing that is, not on what is not. ‘Unseen’: the seen is modification, which is there to be seen; Brahman is not a modification, not an object. Being unseen it is bodiless; being bodiless it is undefined, for a particular is what is defined, and a particular is a modification; hence it has no abode, no support: it is unlike every property of an effect. abhayam is adverbial (or agrees with pratiṣṭhām): when the seeker finds his standing in it, his being that as his Self, then, not seeing the manifoldness made by ignorance that is the cause of fear, he has reached fearlessness. Established in his own nature he sees nothing else, hears nothing else, knows nothing else; fear is of one from another, not of the Self from the Self. Brāhmaṇas are seen fearless on every side though causes of fear are present; that would not be fitting if there were no Brahman to protect from fear.

Bhāṣya On fear: in the state of ignorance, seeing a thing set up by ignorance like the second moon of a man with diseased eyes, one makes in this Brahman, the Self, ut, even, aram, a small, antaram, cleft: the seeing of difference; then, from that seeing of difference, he has fear. So for the non-knower the Self itself is the cause of fear. ‘That very Brahman is fear for the knower who does not reflect’: for one who knows it as different (‘the Lord is other than I, I am another, subject to worldly existence’) and does not think of it as one, that same Brahman, seen as different and called the Lord, is fear. He who does not see the one undivided truth of the Self is therefore no knower, learned though he be. Fear comes, to one who thinks himself destructible, from seeing a cause of destruction, which is itself indestructible. The whole world is seen to be in fear; so there must be a cause of fear, a cause of destruction that is itself indestructible, of which the world is afraid.

Bhāṣya On the verse: wind and the others, great and themselves lords, keep to their laborious tasks by rule; that is fitting only if there is a ruler. So there is a ruler they fear, Brahman, for fear of which they act like a king's servants; and that cause of fear is Brahman, which is bliss.

Bhāṣya On the inquiry: the question about bliss is whether it is born of the meeting of subject and object, like worldly bliss, or is natural. The highest worldly bliss, which comes of outer and inner means, is pointed out so that the bliss of Brahman may be reached through it: by this familiar bliss the bliss that is grasped by an understanding turned from objects can be approached. Worldly bliss too is a particle of the bliss of Brahman: when knowledge is covered by ignorance and ignorance is at its height, it is experienced by Brahmā and the rest according to their actions and knowledge and through means such as objects, and becomes the unsteady bliss of the world. As ignorance, desire and action grow less, that same bliss is experienced, at each higher stage from the human gandharvas on, a hundredfold greater, and is before the eyes of the knower versed in the Veda who is not smitten by desire, up to the bliss of Brahmā, Hiraṇyagarbha. When the division of subject and object made by ignorance is removed by knowledge, there is the natural, full, one bliss without a second.

Bhāṣya ‘young’, in the first age of life; ‘a good young man’, for a young man may not be good and a good man not young. adhyāyaka: one who has studied the Veda. āśiṣṭha: āśāstṛtama (the word meaning here gives the common sense, ‘most swift’); dṛḍhiṣṭha, most firm; baliṣṭha, most strong: so he has the inner means. The earth full of wealth, the means of enjoyment and of rites with seen and unseen fruit: he is a king, lord of the earth. Human gandharvas are men who have become gandharvas by particular rites and knowledge; they have powers such as vanishing and subtle bodies and organs, so they meet few obstacles and can resist the pairs of opposites; hence clearness of mind, and from greater clearness a greater happiness. So at each higher stage. The divine gandharvas are so by birth. The ancestors' world lasts long. Ājāna is a world of the gods; those born there by particular rites of smṛti are the ājānaja gods. The karmadevas reach the gods by Vedic rites alone, the Agnihotra and the like. ‘The gods’ are the thirty-three who receive the oblations; Indra is their lord; Bṛhaspati his teacher; Prajāpati is Virāj, whose body is the three worlds; Brahmā is Hiraṇyagarbha, collective and individual, pervading the round of worldly existence.

Bhāṣya On ‘versed in the Veda and not smitten by desire’: being versed in the Veda and being without sin (taken from ‘a good young man, learned’) are the same at every step; freedom from desire is what rises, and with it the bliss a hundredfold. So these are the three means, and freedom from desire the chief means of attaining the supreme bliss. It is not named with human bliss, so that the one versed in the Veda who is not smitten by desire for human enjoyments is said to have a bliss a hundredfold greater than the human, equal to the human gandharva's. The bliss of Brahmā, directly known to such a one, is a particle of the supreme bliss (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.3.32); that bliss whose particles are divided like drops of the sea, and in which they become one, is the supreme bliss, natural because non-dual; in it bliss and the blissful are not divided.

Bhāṣya On ‘he is one’: the outcome of the inquiry is summed up. He who is set in the cave in the highest space, who created the effect from space to the body of food and entered it, is ‘he who is here in man’; the supreme bliss, of which Brahmā and the other beings live on a part, is ‘he who is yonder in the sun’. He is one, as the space in jars in different places is one with space. If it is asked why the Self in man is named in general and not, as is usual, ‘in the right eye’: the supreme Self is the subject here (2.7; the verse and the inquiry of 2.8), and the knowledge of it is what is meant. The sun is named to remove higher and lower: if the highest reach of duality, the formed and the formless, which is within the sun, is equal, the particulars of man being set aside, when seen from the supreme bliss, then for one who has come to that there is neither higher nor lower, and ‘he finds a fearless footing’ is in place. So the first question, whether Brahman is or is not, is answered: by its effect, the gaining of the savour, breathing, the fearless footing and the sight of fear, Brahman, the cause of space and the rest, exists. Of the other two questions the last, whether the knower attains or not, is now answered; the middle one is answered by that.

Bhāṣya On the passing over: ‘he who knows thus’, who, leaving higher and lower, knows ‘I am the non-dual Brahman, real, knowledge, infinite’. ‘This world’ is the sum of desired objects seen and unseen; ‘departing’ from it is turning back from it, no longer needing it. He passes into the self made of food: he does not see the mass of objects as apart from the self of food, he sees all that is gross as that self; then into the self of prāṇa within it, undivided, present in all selves of food; then those of mind, understanding and bliss; then he finds the fearless footing in the unseen (2.7).

Bhāṣya The inquiry: is he who passes over other than the supreme Self, or the same? If other, the śruti is contradicted (2.6; Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.4.10; Chāndogya 6.2.1, 6.8.7). If the same, one thing cannot be both agent and object of the passing, and either the supreme would be subject to worldly existence or there would be no supreme. An objector says the inquiry is idle, and that one who holds oneness, standing alone against the many who hold manifoldness, will not settle it; the reply: that I am called a holder of the one with many opponents is itself a blessing to me; I shall overcome them all, and I begin the inquiry. He is the same, for becoming that is what is meant: by knowing it one becomes the supreme Self (‘the knower of Brahman attains the highest’), and one thing cannot become another. If it is said that neither can a thing become itself: the teaching is for removing the non-self made by ignorance. The gaining of one's own Self by the knowledge of Brahman is taught for removing the particular selves of food and the rest, which ignorance has made and which, not being the Self, are superimposed as the Self. This is known because knowledge alone is taught, and the seen effect of knowledge is the ceasing of ignorance.

Bhāṣya Objections on knowledge as the means: knowledge may be taught as the means though the goal is elsewhere, as the knowledge of the road is taught to one who is to reach a village? The case differs: there the knowledge taught is of the road, not of the village; here no knowledge of another means is taught apart from the knowledge of Brahman. That knowledge depends on rites and the like for attaining the highest was refuted (note on 1.11: liberation is eternal). The śruti ‘having created it he entered that very thing’ shows the effect to be of the nature of that. And the fearless footing holds only so: if the knower sees nothing other than himself, there is no other to cause fear; and if the other is made by ignorance, knowledge can see it as unreal. The second moon is unreal in that one whose eye is sound does not perceive it.

Bhāṣya On the three states: if it is said that the other is not thus unperceived: it is not perceived in deep sleep or in absorption. Nor is that like the inattention of one whose mind is elsewhere, for nothing at all is perceived. Nor does the perceiving of another in waking and dream prove that it exists: that perceiving is made by ignorance, being absent when ignorance is absent. Nor is the not perceiving in deep sleep also made by ignorance: it is natural. The truth of a thing is its not changing, which depends on nothing else; change is not its truth, for it depends on another. A particular depends on the factors of action, and a particular is a change; the perceiving in waking and dream is such a particular. What a thing is without need of another is its truth.

Bhāṣya On those who hold difference: for those to whom the Lord is other than the Self, and the effect other too, fear does not cease, for fear is caused by another, and an existent other cannot lose its being, nor a non-existent gain it. If it is said that the other causes fear only with the help of some cause such as demerit: that cause too, eternal or not, cannot lose its being, so fear does not cease; and if it could, being and non-being turning into each other, nothing could be relied on. On the view of oneness there is no fault, for worldly existence and its cause are imagined by ignorance; the second moon seen by diseased eyes neither comes to be nor perishes. Nor are knowledge and ignorance properties of the Self: they are perceived directly, like colour, as discernment and want of discernment in the inner organ, and what is perceived is not a property of the perceiver. Ignorance is marked by one's own experience (‘I am confused, my knowledge is not clear’), and so is knowledge; the wise teach their knowledge to others, who grasp it. So knowledge and ignorance belong to the side of name and form, which are imagined in the Self like day and night in the sun, and are not really there (Chāndogya 8.14.1).

Bhāṣya The conclusion: if there is no difference, how can he ‘pass into’ the self made of bliss, being both agent and object? The passing is knowledge only; it is not a passing like a leech's. It is not literal: no such movement is seen in passing into the self of food from the outer world; nor is it the self of mind or of understanding going out and returning to itself, for action on oneself is contradictory, and so too for the self of bliss. So the passing is not a reaching, nor is its agent any of the five selves; it is knowledge, and its agent is other than they. Of him who is within the self of bliss, the inmost of all, who created the effect and entered it, the error of taking the selves of food and the rest, which are not the Self, for the Self, through his tie with the cave of the heart, perishes when the discerning knowledge of the Self arises: the word ‘passing’ is used figuratively of that perishing of the error of ignorance. Becoming many, creation, entering, gaining the savour, fearlessness, passing over and the rest are thus imagined in Brahman, which is beyond all dealings, only so that the Self defined as real, knowledge and infinite may be understood; in truth no imagining holds in Brahman. Having passed in this order into that Self and known it, one fears nothing. The verse that follows gives in brief the sense of the whole vallī.

Bhāṣya The verse was cited at the self made of mind (2.4) because mind is the means of knowing Brahman; there Brahman-hood was superimposed on it and, in its praise, fear alone was denied (‘does not fear at any time’); here, where the non-dual is the subject, the very cause of fear is denied (‘from anything’).

Bhāṣya Is there not a cause of fear, the good left undone and the evil done? Not for him. ‘Why did I not do the good?’ is the remorse that comes when death is near; ‘why did I do evil?’ is the torment that comes from fear of the pain of hell and the like. These two do not torment the knower as they do the non-knower: knowing them thus, he gladdens or strengthens the Self; that is, he sees both as the supreme Self. Having emptied merit and sin of their own particular form, he sees them as the Self; so seen they are without force, do not torment, and do not begin another birth. ‘Such is the Upaniṣad’: the knowledge of Brahman taught in this vallī is the highest secret of all knowledge; the highest good is seated in it.

Bhāṣya Having named the doors, he stated the definition of Brahman: that from which these beings, from Brahmā down to a clump of grass, are born; by which, once born, they live, keep their breath and grow; and to which at the time of destruction they go, into which they enter, becoming of one nature with it. That whose nature beings do not leave at their arising, their staying and their dissolution: this is the definition of Brahman. ‘Seek to know that Brahman in particular’: attain through food and the rest the Brahman that has this mark. Another text says: ‘those who know the prāṇa of prāṇa, the eye of the eye, the ear of the ear, the food of food, the mind of mind, have ascertained the ancient, first Brahman’ (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.18), showing that these are the doors to its perception.

Bhāṣya Having heard from his father the doors to the perception of Brahman and its definition, Bhṛgu practised austerity as the means of attaining it. How did he take up austerity when it had not been taught as the means? Because what was said was incomplete. The father named food and the rest as doors and gave the definition ‘from which these beings are born’; that is incomplete, for Brahman was not pointed out directly. Otherwise he should have shown its own form to the son who wished to know: ‘this Brahman is such.’ He did not; he spoke with a remainder. So it is understood that the father surely expects another means for the knowledge of Brahman. Austerity in particular is fixed upon because it is the best of all means; it is well known in the world that for results bound to fixed means austerity is the best means. So Bhṛgu took up austerity though his father had not taught it. One should not turn anyone away in the matter of lodging: that is the vow. Therefore one should obtain much food by whatever means. ‘Food has been made ready for him,’ they say (to the guest). This food, made ready, (he gives) in the foremost way: in the foremost way food is made ready for him. This food, made ready, (he gives) in the middling way: in the middling way food is made ready for him. This food, made ready, (he gives) in the lowest way: in the lowest way food is made ready for him, who knows thus. (Brahman is to be meditated on) as safekeeping in speech; as gain and safekeeping in prāṇa and apāna; as action in the hands; as movement in the feet; as release in the anus: these are the human meditations. Now the divine: as satisfaction in rain; as strength in lightning; as renown in cattle; as light in the stars; as procreation, immortality and bliss in the organ of generation; as the all in space. One should meditate on that as the support: one becomes possessed of support. One should meditate on that as greatness: one becomes great. One should meditate on that as thinking: one becomes able to think. One should meditate on that as bowing: the things desired bow down to him. One should meditate on that as Brahman (the greatest): one becomes possessed of Brahman. One should meditate on that as Brahman's ‘dying around’: around him die the rivals who hate him, and around him those foes who are not dear to him. He who is here in man, and he who is yonder in the sun: he is one. He who knows thus, on departing from this world, having passed into this self made of food, having passed into this self made of prāṇa, having passed into this self made of mind, having passed into this self made of understanding, having passed into this self made of bliss, roaming through these worlds, having food at will and taking forms at will, remains singing this chant: ‘Oh! Oh! Oh! I am food, I am food, I am food! I am the eater of food, I am the eater of food, I am the eater of food! I am the maker of their union, I am the maker of their union, I am the maker of their union! I am the first-born of the true (this world), before the gods, the navel of immortality. He who gives me, he indeed thus protects me. I, food, eat him who eats food (without giving). I have overcome the whole world; (my) light is like the sun.’ (This is the fruit for him) who knows thus. Such is the secret teaching.

Bhāṣya On the vow and on the giving of food: likewise, for one who meditates on earth and space (as food and eater), the vow is not to turn away anyone who has come for lodging; and when lodging is given, food must certainly be given. Therefore one should gather much food by whatever means. Because the learned who have food say to the guest who seeks food ‘food has been made ready for him’ and do not refuse him with ‘there is none’, for that reason too one should obtain much food. The greatness of the gift of food is also stated: as and when one gives food, so and then it comes back to him. mukhataḥ = in the first age of life, or in the foremost manner, with honour: he gives the food made ready to the guest who seeks it (the sentence is to be so completed); the fruit is that food is made ready for that giver in his early age, or in the foremost manner; it comes to him as it was given. So madhyataḥ = in the middle age, or with middling courtesy; and antataḥ = in the last age, or with the lowest courtesy, with disrespect: in just that way food is made ready for him. ‘Who knows thus’: to him who knows the greatness of food as stated, and the fruit of giving it, that fruit comes.

Bhāṣya On the meditations: now the manner of meditating on Brahman is stated. kṣema = the preserving of what has been got: Brahman is to be meditated on as established in speech in the form of safekeeping. yoga = the getting of what has not been got. Though gain and safekeeping come about while prāṇa and apāna are there, they are not caused by prāṇa and apāna alone but by Brahman; so Brahman is to be meditated on as established in prāṇa and apāna as gain and safekeeping. In the others too Brahman alone is to be meditated on as this or that: since action is brought about by Brahman, as established in the hands as action; as movement in the feet; as release in the anus. These are the mānuṣīḥ samājñāḥ: belonging to men, concerning the body; samājñā = knowledges, that is, meditations. Then the divine ones, belonging to the gods. Since rain is the cause of satisfaction through food and the rest, Brahman itself abides in rain as satisfaction; likewise as strength in lightning, as renown in cattle, as light in the stars. Procreation, immortality (the attaining of immortality through a son, by release from the debt) and ānanda, pleasure: all this, which the organ of generation occasions, is Brahman established there in that form. All is established in space; so whatever is ‘all’ in space is Brahman, and that space is Brahman; therefore one should meditate on it as the support of all.

Bhāṣya On the fruits: by meditating on the quality of support one becomes possessed of support. So in the earlier ones too: whatever fruit is read there is Brahman itself, and by meditating on it one comes to possess it; another śruti says, ‘as they meditate on him, that he becomes’ (Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 10.5.2.20). mahaḥ = possessed of the quality of greatness. manaḥ = thinking (manana); mānavān = able to think. namaḥ = bowing: possessed of the quality of bowing; the kāmāḥ, the things desired, the objects of enjoyment, bow down to the meditator. brahma = the greatest (parivṛḍhatama); brahmavān = he comes to have that quality. brahmaṇaḥ parimaraḥ: that in which the five deities die around, lightning, rain, the moon, the sun and fire; so the wind is the parimara, as is well known from another śruti. This same wind is not other than space; so space is the parimara of Brahman, and one should meditate on space, whose self is the wind, as the parimara of Brahman. Rivals may be hating or not hating, so they are qualified: the rivals who hate him die around him, they give up their breath; and those foes who are not dear to him, even if they do not hate him, also die.

Bhāṣya On what the teaching of food and eater establishes: from ‘prāṇa indeed is food, the body the eater of food’ down to space, being food and eater has been stated of the effect alone. What follows from that? That transmigration, which is made by being enjoyed and enjoyer, belongs to the effect alone and not to the Self; of the Self it is spoken only figuratively, through error. Objection: the self too is an effect of the supreme Self, so transmigration fits it. No: the śruti of entering is of one who does not transmigrate. In ‘having created that, it entered that very thing’ (2.6) the entering into the effects is heard of the supreme Self, the cause of space and the rest, which does not transmigrate; so the jīva-self that has entered the effect is the supreme itself, not transmigrating. And the same agent is fitting in ‘having created, it entered’: the suffix ktvā is right only if the agent of creating and of entering is one. Objection: but the one who has entered comes to another state. No: entering was already shown (at 2.6) to have another sense. Objection: from the specific text ‘with this jīva’ (Chāndogya 6.3.2) the entering is with another property. No: ‘that thou art’ (Chāndogya 6.8.7) states its being that again. Objection: that is an imagined identification, to remove the other state from one who has come into it. No: because of the co-reference in ‘that is the true, that is the Self, that thou art’. Objection: the transmigration of the jīva is seen. No: the perceiver cannot be perceived. Objection: the self qualified by the properties of transmigration is perceived. No: properties are not separate from their bearer and so cannot be its objects, just as heat and light cannot be what is burnt and lit. Objection: from the sight of fear and the like, its being a sufferer is inferred. No: fear and the like, and pain, being perceived, are not properties of the perceiver. Objection: this conflicts with the reasoned systems of Kapila, Kaṇāda and others. No: having no root and conflicting with the Veda, they are fittingly in error. So by śruti and by reasoning it is established that the Self does not transmigrate; and from its oneness. How oneness? ‘He who is here in man, and he who is yonder in the sun: he is one’ and the rest, all as before (2.8).

Bhāṣya On the knower's roaming and chant: the sense of the verse ‘real, knowledge, infinite’ was explained at length by the Ānandavallī, but not the sense of its statement of the fruit, ‘he enjoys all desires, together with Brahman, the all-knowing’ (2.1): what the desires are, what their objects, and how he enjoys them together with Brahman. That is taken up now. In the story of father and son, a supplement to the earlier knowledge, austerity was taught as the means to the knowledge of Brahman; the effect from prāṇa to space was applied as food and eater; and meditations on Brahman were taught. All the desires that are won each by its own many means and have for objects the differing effects, space and the rest, have thus been shown. But in oneness there can be neither desire nor one who desires, all difference being the Self. How then does he who knows thus enjoy all desires at once in the form of Brahman? Because he is the self of all. How so? By the knowledge of the oneness of the self in man and in the sun he sets aside higher and lower, passes in order through the selves from the one made of food to the one made of bliss, which ignorance has fashioned, and reaches the fruit: Brahman, real, knowledge, infinite, unseen and so on, natural bliss, unborn, deathless, fearless, non-dual. Then he roams through these worlds, earth and the rest: kāmānnī, whose food is at his will; kāmarūpī, whose forms are at his will; roaming, that is, experiencing these worlds as himself, being the self of all. He remains singing this sāman: Brahman itself is the sāman, because it is the same (sama), not other than anything; singing = uttering, proclaiming the oneness of the Self for the good of the world, and its fruit, the utter fulfilment of all ends. hāvu means ‘oh!’, to make known extreme wonder.

Bhāṣya On the chant: what is the wonder? Though I am the non-dual, stainless Self, I myself am food and the eater of food. And I myself am the ślokakṛt: śloka is the union of food and eater, and its maker is the conscious one; or, the maker of the aggregate of food itself, which, being manifold and for the sake of another, the eater, is put together because it is for another. The saying thrice is to declare the wonder. prathamajāḥ = the first-born of ṛta, the true, this world with form and without form, and before the gods; amṛtasya nābhiḥ = the centre of immortality: the immortality of living beings rests in me. Whoever gives me, food, to those who seek food, declaring me as food, he thus protects me unharmed, as I am. But he who, when the time has come, eats food without giving me to those who ask, him, the man eating food, I who am food eat in return.

Bhāṣya On a doubt and the close: ‘then I am afraid of liberation, the attaining of being the self of all; let me stay in transmigration, since even when liberated I should, being food, be eaten.’ Do not fear so: the enjoying of all desires lies within the sphere of worldly dealing. The knower has by knowledge passed beyond that sphere, marked by food, eater and the rest and made by ignorance, and has reached Brahman-hood; for him there is no second thing of which he could be afraid; so liberation is not to be feared. Then why does he say ‘I am food, I am the eater of food’? This dealing marked by food and eater, an effect, is mere dealing and not a real thing; yet, being caused by Brahman and unreal apart from Brahman, it is spoken of in praise of the becoming Brahman that is the result of the knowledge of Brahman. So for one who has become Brahman there is not even a trace of fear and such faults, which come of ignorance, ignorance being cut off. bhuvanam = what beings from Brahmā down resort to, or that in which beings come to be; abhyabhavām = I overcome it by my own supreme, lordly nature. suvar na jyotiḥ: suvar is the sun and na has the sense of ‘like’: my light is like the sun, shining once for all. iti: the Upaniṣad is the knowledge of the supreme Self taught in the two vallīs; for him who, having become calm, restrained, withdrawn, forbearing and collected, and having taken up great austerity like Bhṛgu, knows this Upaniṣad as taught, there is this fruit, liberation as stated. (The commentary closes with the invocation saha nāv avatu.)
